A crowd waits outside a shop that has been "opening soon" for weeks. One morning someone at the front shouts that the door is open, the crowd surges forward, and then discovers the door was only unlocked for a second. Now everyone at the front is stuck, pushed in by the people behind them, with nowhere to go but back.

That is a **fakeout**, and it is the single most expensive lesson in market structure. The real thing, a **breakout**, is one of the most profitable moments on a chart. The fake one is designed, quite literally, to take your money. Telling them apart is a skill, not a guess, and this lesson gives you the checklist.

## What a breakout is

A <Term id="breakout">breakout</Term> is price moving through a support or resistance zone and continuing beyond it. If the zone was resistance, the breakout is upward. If the zone was support, it is a **breakdown**. Everything about breakdowns mirrors breakouts, so this lesson mostly talks about the upward case.

A breakout matters because the people who defended a level have just lost. Sellers who kept the ceiling in place have either given up or been overwhelmed. Buyers who were waiting for proof now pile in. And anyone who shorted the ceiling is now losing and has to buy back to close, adding even more fuel.

## The three signs of a real break

No breakout is guaranteed. But three things, seen together, tilt the odds heavily toward the break being real.

### 1. A close beyond the zone

Not a wick. A **close**. Wicks poke through levels all the time and mean very little; they are the crowd testing the door. A candle that *closes* beyond the zone means the people who traded that whole period were willing to finish it on the far side.

Which candle? The one on the timeframe you are analysing. On a daily chart, wait for the daily close. On a 1-hour chart, the hourly close. Many traders add a buffer: the close must be clear of the zone, not just a cent above it.

### 2. Volume expansion

From [the volume lesson](/learn/m01-reading-price/06-volume): volume is conviction. A real breakout is many people deciding at once, so the breakout candle should show volume noticeably above the recent average, often 1.5 to 2 times or more. In the figure above, the breakout bar has more than twice the volume of the bars before it.

A break on thin volume means few people believed it. That is the classic signature of the fakeout.

### 3. A successful retest

After breaking a ceiling, price often comes back down to touch it. This is the **retest**. If the old resistance now holds as support (the role reversal you meet next lesson), the breakout has been confirmed a second time, by a different set of buyers on a different day. In the figure, the retest near $104 holds and price runs to $115.

The retest is also the lower-risk entry. You buy at the level with a stop just below it, instead of buying the breakout candle with your stop far away at the bottom of the range.

<Callout type="eli5">

A real breakout is like a dam actually bursting: lots of water (volume), it keeps flowing (the close is beyond the wall), and when you look again later the water is still on the other side (the retest holds). A fake breakout is a splash over the top that dribbles straight back. Same wall, very different story.

</Callout>

## Anatomy of a fakeout

A <Term id="fakeout">fakeout</Term> (or false breakout, or **bull trap** if it fakes upward and **bear trap** if it fakes downward) is a move through a level that fails and reverses back inside.

<PatternFigure name="fakeout" />

Look at what the figure shows. Price pokes above $104 on volume that is actually *lower* than the bars before it. It reaches $106.50, stalls, and falls back inside the range. Then it keeps falling, all the way to $91.

Why does it keep falling? Because of **trapped traders**. Everyone who bought the breakout at $105 or $106 is now losing. As price drops back through $104 they panic and sell, adding to the down move. Their stop-losses sit just below the range and get hit, adding more selling. The fakeout doesn't just fail to go up; it actively powers the move down.

This is the important insight: a failed breakout is not a non-event. It is a signal in the opposite direction, often a strong one. The chart patterns module returns to this idea in [the lesson on pattern failure](/learn/m04-chart-patterns/10-pattern-failure).

## Stop hunts

Sometimes a fakeout is not an accident. Large participants know where the crowd's stops sit: just above a well-known resistance level, or just below a well-known support. Pushing price briefly through the level triggers those stops, which creates a burst of orders the large player can fill their own position against. Then price reverses.

You will hear this called a **stop hunt** or **liquidity grab**. Whether any specific one was deliberate is unknowable, but the pattern is real and common: a sharp wick through an obvious level, followed by an immediate reversal. The practical lesson is that the most obvious stop placement (a few cents beyond the level) is also the most likely to get hit. Give your stops room, or wait for the retest instead of buying the initial break.

<Callout type="danger" title="Buying the first candle through a level is the highest-risk version of this trade">

The moment price first breaks a level is the moment everyone is most excited and least informed. It is also where fakeouts do their damage. If you must enter on the break, wait for the close, check the volume, and size small. Better: let the retest come to you.

</Callout>

## A checklist for every break

Before you act on a breakout, ask:

1. Did the candle *close* beyond the zone, on my timeframe?
2. Was volume clearly above average on the break?
3. Is the breakout in the direction of the higher-timeframe trend?
4. Is there a retest I could wait for, and where would my stop go if I did?
5. If this is a fakeout, where would trapped traders be, and could I trade the reversal instead?

Three or more yeses and you have a breakout worth respecting. Fewer, and you are probably looking at the door being briefly unlocked.

## Honest odds

Even with every box ticked, breakouts fail. Some estimates suggest half or more of all breakouts from ranges reverse at some point. The checklist does not make you right; it makes you right *more often*, and it keeps you out of the worst traps. The rest is risk management, which is a whole module of its own.

<KeyTakeaways>
- A breakout is price closing through a support or resistance zone and continuing; a fakeout is a break that reverses back inside.
- The three signs of a real break: a close beyond the zone (not a wick), volume expansion, and a retest that holds.
- The retest is the lower-risk entry, with a stop just beyond the level instead of at the far side of the range.
- Fakeouts trap traders, and their forced exits power the move in the opposite direction.
- Stop hunts target the obvious stop placement just beyond a level, so give stops room or wait for the retest.
- Even good-looking breakouts fail often; the checklist improves your odds, it does not remove the risk.
</KeyTakeaways>
