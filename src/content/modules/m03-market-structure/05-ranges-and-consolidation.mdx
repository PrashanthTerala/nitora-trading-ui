Every trend lesson makes the market sound like a marathon runner: always moving, always heading somewhere. The truth is closer to a cat. Markets nap. They spend most of their time going nowhere in particular, bouncing between a floor and a ceiling while everyone waits for a reason to move.

Studies of price behaviour across stocks, currencies and indices generally find that markets trend clearly only a minority of the time. The rest is sideways chop. If you only know how to trade trends, you will spend most of your life either bored or losing money forcing trades that are not there.

This lesson is about the pause: what a **range** is, why it forms, how to recognise it early, and the two very different ways to trade it.

## What a range is

A range (also called **consolidation**, a **trading range**, or a **sideways market**) is a stretch where price makes no meaningful higher highs or lower lows. Instead, it oscillates between a support zone and a resistance zone, as you drew them in the [support and resistance lesson](/learn/m03-market-structure/03-support-and-resistance).

Imagine a ball in a box with a lid. The ball bounces off the bottom, goes up, bounces off the lid, comes back down. It never leaves the box, so nothing dramatic happens. A range is a box, and the interesting question is not what the ball does inside but which wall it will eventually break through.

## Why ranges form

Ranges form when the market has run out of new information. After a strong move, the people who wanted to buy have bought and the people who wanted to sell have sold. What remains is a standoff.

Ranges also form around news. Before an earnings report or a central bank decision, nobody wants to commit, so price drifts between levels until the announcement resolves the argument. And ranges form when a big player is quietly building or unloading a position, buying every dip and selling every rally to avoid moving price. That last case is the accumulation and distribution idea you will meet in the [Wyckoff lesson](/learn/m03-market-structure/09-market-cycles-wyckoff).

Whatever the cause, a range is stored energy. The longer and tighter the range, the bigger the move that tends to follow it. Volume usually dries up inside a range and expands when it ends.

## Recognising a range early

You will always recognise a range late, because you need at least two touches of each boundary to be sure. Some signs help you spot one forming:

- The most recent swing high failed to exceed the prior high, *and* the most recent swing low failed to break the prior low.
- Candles are getting smaller and overlapping more, with more dojis and spinning tops (indecision candles from the [doji lesson](/learn/m02-candlestick-patterns/02-doji-family)).
- Volume is shrinking.
- Your trendline has been broken but price has not gone anywhere.

When two or three of those appear together, stop thinking in trend terms and start drawing a box.

## Two ways to trade a range

There are exactly two strategies, and they are opposites.

### Fade the edges

**Fading** means trading against the move. You buy near the bottom of the range, expecting the bounce, and sell near the top, expecting the rejection. Your stop goes a little outside the boundary; your target is the other side of the box.

This works because ranges hold more often than they break, at least in the middle of their life. The maths can be attractive too: buy at $95 with a stop at $93 and a target at $105, and you risk $2 to make $10.

But fading has a nasty failure mode. The one time the level breaks, you are on the wrong side of the breakout with a small stop that gets run over fast. Only fade a range when the boundaries are well tested and price arrives at the edge with some sign of rejection, such as a long wick or an engulfing candle.

### Trade the break

The other approach is to wait for the range to end and go with the breakout. You risk missing the tidy bounces, but you catch the big move that the range was storing energy for. The next lesson is entirely about how to tell a real break from a fake one.

Most traders should pick one of these approaches per range and stick to it. Trying to fade the edges *and* trade the breakout on the same box means you are always on the wrong side of something.

<Callout type="warning" title="The middle of a range is a no-trade zone">

Buying at $100 in a $94-to-$106 range gives you $6 of risk to make $6 of reward at best, with no level to lean on. Ranges are only tradable at the edges. If price is in the middle, wait.

</Callout>

## Ranges inside trends

Zoom out and most ranges are just pauses inside a bigger trend. A three-week range inside a year-long uptrend is more likely to break up than down, because the larger flow of money is still bullish. Knowing the higher-timeframe trend tilts the odds on which side of the box eventually gives way. It also means fading the bottom of the range, in the direction of the bigger trend, is usually a safer fade than fading the top.

In the chart patterns module, this same idea gets a name: the **rectangle** as a continuation pattern. A range that forms after a strong move and then breaks in the same direction is one of the most common shapes on any chart.

## The end of a range

Ranges end in one of two ways: an honest breakout, or a fakeout that traps everyone who bet on the break and then reverses. The signs you will learn next lesson (close beyond the zone, volume expansion, successful retest) tell the two apart. Until you see them, assume the box still holds.

<KeyTakeaways>
- A range is a stretch with no meaningful higher highs or lower lows; price bounces between a support zone and a resistance zone.
- Markets spend more time in ranges than in trends, so you must know how to handle them.
- Ranges are stored energy: volume dries up inside and expands when they end, and long tight ranges tend to produce big moves.
- You can fade the edges (buy the floor, sell the ceiling with tight stops) or trade the breakout, but not both on the same range.
- The middle of a range is a no-trade zone because there is no level to lean on.
- Ranges inside a bigger trend usually resolve in the trend's direction.
</KeyTakeaways>
