Imagine watching a child climb a staircase. Each step up, they pause, maybe wobble back half a step, then climb again. You don't need to see the top of the stairs to know which way they're going. Every new foothold is higher than the last one, and every pause happens higher than the previous pause.

That is a **trend**, and it is the single most useful thing you can learn to see on a chart. Everything else in this module, from support levels to breakouts, sits on top of it. Get the trend right and a lot of average decisions still work out. Get it wrong and even good entries fight the current.

In [Module 1](/learn/m01-reading-price/01-what-is-a-chart) you learned that a chart is price history drawn as a picture. This lesson teaches you to read the shape of that picture with a definition so precise that two people looking at the same chart should agree.

## The definition, exactly

Price never moves in a straight line. It pushes forward, pulls back, pushes forward again. The points where it turns are called **swings**. A peak is a <Term id="swing-high">swing high</Term> and a trough is a <Term id="swing-low">swing low</Term>. The next lesson shows you how to mark them consistently; for now, just picture the peaks and valleys.

An **uptrend** is a sequence of **higher highs (HH)** and **higher lows (HL)**. Each peak is above the peak before it, *and* each trough is above the trough before it. Both conditions must hold. If the peaks are rising but the troughs are not, you don't have a clean uptrend yet.

<PatternFigure name="uptrend" />

Look at the figure. The first peak is near $98, the next near $105, the next near $111: higher highs. The pullbacks bottom out near $95, then $101, then $107: higher lows. That pattern is the entire definition. There is nothing hidden.

A **downtrend** is the mirror: **lower highs (LH)** and **lower lows (LL)**. Each bounce peaks below the last bounce, and each drop bottoms below the last drop.

<PatternFigure name="downtrend" />

When neither is true, when highs and lows are roughly level, the market is in a **range** (sometimes called sideways or consolidation). Ranges get their own lesson later. For now, the key idea is that a market is always doing one of three things: trending up, trending down, or going sideways.

<Callout type="eli5">

Think of a tug of war between a Buyers team and a Sellers team. In an uptrend, the Buyers keep dragging the rope past where they got to last time, and when the Sellers pull back they can't even reach the spot where they were before. Higher highs means Buyers are winning the pulls. Higher lows means Sellers are losing the pulls. When both are true, one team is clearly stronger.

</Callout>

## Why the definition matters

Beginners describe trends by feel: "it looks like it's going up." Feelings are not repeatable. On a fast-moving chart you can convince yourself of almost anything, especially after you have already bought.

The HH and HL definition is repeatable. Better still, it tells you *exactly* what would have to happen for the trend to be in doubt. In an uptrend, the first warning sign is a **lower low**: price pulls back and drops beneath the previous trough. That single event does not guarantee a reversal, but it means the Sellers finally won a pull, which had not happened before.

The reverse also works. If a downtrend prints its first higher high, buyers just managed something they could not manage before. The trend has not flipped yet, but the pattern that defined it has been broken.

This matters for your trades in a very practical way. If you are long in an uptrend, the most recent higher low is a natural place to consider putting a stop-loss, because if price goes below it, the reason you bought (the uptrend) is no longer intact. You'll revisit that idea properly in the risk module.

## The trend has stages: impulse and pullback

Within a trend, the moves in the direction of the trend are called **impulses** (or legs) and the moves against it are **pullbacks** (or retracements, or corrections). Impulses tend to be faster and larger; pullbacks tend to be slower and smaller.

Compare the two on volume, which you met in [the volume lesson](/learn/m01-reading-price/06-volume). In a healthy uptrend, volume often expands on the impulses and shrinks on the pullbacks. That combination says the crowd is committed when price goes up and merely resting when it comes down. When you see the opposite, big volume on a pullback and thin volume on the push higher, treat it as a yellow flag.

A trend where the impulses shrink and the pullbacks grow is losing strength, even if it still technically makes higher highs. Think of the child on the stairs taking smaller and smaller steps and longer and longer pauses.

## Strength, angle and how far to zoom

Two uptrends can look completely different. One climbs at a gentle 20-degree slope with tidy pullbacks. Another rockets almost vertically. Steep trends are exciting but fragile; they often end with a sharp break because nobody who chased is protected by a nearby higher low.

Zoom matters too. Timeframes were covered in [the timeframes lesson](/learn/m01-reading-price/05-timeframes), and here is where they bite: a 5-minute chart can show a crisp downtrend that is nothing more than one pullback inside a daily uptrend. Neither view is wrong. They are different zoom levels of the same staircase. The last lesson in this module, on multi-timeframe analysis, gives you a routine for holding both in your head at once.

<Callout type="warning" title="The trend can change while you are still calling it a trend">

A trend is defined by the past. By the time you have three higher highs and three higher lows, the move might be mostly done. Never assume a trend will continue simply because it has continued so far. Use the structure to define your risk, not to predict the future.

</Callout>

## A simple counting exercise

Try this on any chart. Find the most recent swing high and swing low. Then find the ones before them. Ask two questions:

1. Is the latest high above the previous high?
2. Is the latest low above the previous low?

Two yeses: uptrend. Two noes: downtrend. One of each: the trend is unclear, which is itself useful information. When you can't classify a chart in ten seconds, that chart is probably not offering a clean trade right now.

Write your answer down before you look at anything else. It is astonishing how often traders decide on a trade and only afterwards ask which way the market is moving.

<KeyTakeaways>
- An uptrend is higher highs and higher lows; a downtrend is lower highs and lower lows; anything else is a range.
- Both conditions must hold. Rising peaks with flat troughs is not a clean uptrend.
- The first lower low in an uptrend (or higher high in a downtrend) is the earliest structural warning that the trend is in doubt.
- Impulses are the moves with the trend and pullbacks are the moves against it; healthy trends have bigger, faster impulses and smaller pullbacks.
- The same market can trend in different directions on different timeframes, so always know which zoom level you are describing.
- The trend defines your risk (where you are wrong), not a promise about the future.
</KeyTakeaways>
