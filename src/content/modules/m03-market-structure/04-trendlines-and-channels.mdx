Give a ruler to a beginner and a chart, and within a minute the chart is covered in diagonal lines. Every one of them "fits." Every one of them is meaningless. A line that touches two points is not analysis; any two points make a line. The skill is knowing which lines the market is actually respecting.

A **trendline** is a sloping support or resistance level. It connects the swing lows in an uptrend or the swing highs in a downtrend, and it gives you a picture of the *pace* of the trend, not just its direction. Used carefully it is one of the cleanest tools on the chart. Used carelessly it is a machine for seeing what you want to see.

This lesson gives you the rules. They are stricter than you expect, and that is the point.

## Which points to connect

In an uptrend, draw the trendline *under* the price, connecting the higher lows. It is the floor the trend keeps bouncing off. In a downtrend, draw it *over* the price, connecting the lower highs. It is the ceiling.

Use the swing points you marked with the [3-bar pivot rule](/learn/m03-market-structure/02-swing-highs-and-lows). Do not connect random candles that happen to line up. If it isn't a swing, it isn't a trendline anchor.

## Two touches to draw, three to confirm

Here is the rule that separates real lines from wishful ones.

- **Two touches** let you draw a line. That is all they do. Any two swings define a slope, so this line is a guess.
- **Three touches** confirm it. When price returns to the line a third time and bounces, the market has shown that other people are watching the same line. Now it has meaning.

A trendline that has been touched three or four times, with clear reactions each time, is a strong reference. A two-touch line is a hypothesis you are waiting to test.

<Callout type="eli5">

If you throw a ball and it bounces once, you know very little. If it bounces twice in the same spot, you can guess the floor is there. If it bounces a third time, you're sure. Trendlines work the same way: two bounces is a guess, three is a floor.

</Callout>

## Wicks or bodies?

Should the line touch the wick tips or the candle bodies? Traders argue about this endlessly. The honest answer: it doesn't matter much, as long as you are consistent and you treat the line as a zone, exactly as you did with [horizontal levels](/learn/m03-market-structure/03-support-and-resistance).

A trendline is a sloping zone. Price will overshoot it by a wick, undershoot it by a body, and generally refuse to land on it perfectly. If a tiny wick pierces your line and price closes back inside, the line has held. If price *closes* beyond it decisively, it has broken.

## The angle matters

A trendline's slope tells you how fast the trend is moving. There are three broad cases.

**Gentle (roughly 20 to 45 degrees).** Sustainable. Pullbacks have room to breathe, and the line gets tested at reasonable intervals. Most long-lasting trends look like this.

**Steep (over about 60 degrees).** Exciting and fragile. Price is going almost vertical, which means nobody is getting a chance to buy a pullback and nobody who bought recently is protected. Steep lines break often, and when they break, the drop can be fast. A break of a steep line usually just means the trend is slowing to a more sustainable angle, not reversing.

**Flat (under about 15 degrees).** Barely a trend. Treat it as a range with a slight lean.

Angles depend on how you zoom the chart, so the degree numbers are rough guides, not laws. The useful habit is to compare the slope of the current trendline with the slope of the previous one on the same chart. When each successive trendline gets steeper, the trend is accelerating and getting closer to exhaustion. When the trendline is repeatedly broken and redrawn at a shallower slope, the trend is fading.

## Channels: the trendline's twin

If you draw a trendline under the higher lows and then draw a parallel line over the higher highs, you get a **channel**. The lower line is support, the upper line is resistance, and price tends to travel between them like a ball rolling down a sloped corridor.

Channels give you a target. If price bounces off the bottom of an ascending channel, the top of the channel is a natural place to expect resistance. Price failing to reach the top of a channel is a subtle sign that the trend is weakening; price breaking out of the top is a sign of acceleration (and often a short-lived one).

A range, which you meet in the next lesson, is simply a channel with zero slope.

<Callout type="warning" title="Never bend a line to fit">

The most common trendline sin: price breaks the line, so you redraw it slightly flatter to include the new low. Then it breaks again, so you redraw it again. Each redraw is the market telling you the trend is weakening, and each redraw is you refusing to hear it. If you have to redraw a line more than once, the trend it described is over.

</Callout>

## What a trendline break means (and doesn't)

When price closes beyond a confirmed trendline, that is information. But be careful about *what* information. A trendline break means the *pace* of the trend has changed. It does not by itself mean the trend has reversed.

Remember that the trend is defined by higher highs and higher lows. Price can break a rising trendline, drift sideways for a while, make a higher low that just happens to be below the old line, and then push to a new higher high. The trendline was broken. The trend was not.

A trendline break becomes much more serious when it is combined with a structural break: a lower low after the line gives way. Then two independent pieces of evidence agree, and that is worth acting on.

## Objective trendline checklist

Before you trust a line, run through this:

1. Does it anchor on genuine swings, not random candles?
2. Has it been touched at least three times?
3. Did price *react* at each touch, or just pass through?
4. Is the angle sustainable, or is it near-vertical?
5. Would someone else, given the same chart, draw roughly the same line?

The fifth question is the real test. Trendlines only work because many traders see them. A line only you can see has no crowd behind it.

<KeyTakeaways>
- A trendline is sloping support (under higher lows) or sloping resistance (over lower highs), anchored on real swing points.
- Two touches let you draw a line; three touches confirm that the market is respecting it.
- Treat a trendline as a zone: wicks may poke through, but a decisive close beyond it is a break.
- The angle matters: gentle slopes last, steep ones break, and steepening lines signal acceleration toward exhaustion.
- A channel adds a parallel line and gives you a natural target on the far side.
- A trendline break signals a change of pace, not a reversal; it needs a structural break (a lower low or higher high) to mean more.
</KeyTakeaways>
