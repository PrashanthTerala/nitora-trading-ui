Think of a fence between two gardens. When you are on one side, it is the thing stopping you from going further. Climb over it, and the same fence is now the thing behind you, keeping you from going back. The fence hasn't changed. Your relationship to it has.

Support and resistance levels work the same way. A ceiling that gets broken does not disappear. It becomes a floor. A floor that gives way becomes a ceiling. This flip is called **role reversal** (or sometimes "support becomes resistance" and "flip zones"), and it is the mechanism behind the retest entry you learned in the [breakouts lesson](/learn/m03-market-structure/06-breakouts-and-fakeouts) and behind most pullback trades you will ever take.

It is a small idea. It pays for itself over and over.

## The flip, in one picture

<PatternFigure name="role-reversal" />

Look at the level near $102.30. Twice, price rallies to it and gets rejected: it is resistance. Then price breaks through and runs to $108. On the way back down, price returns to $102.30 and this time it *holds*. It holds again a few bars later. The old ceiling has become the new floor.

## Why the flip happens

The reason is not magic; it is memory, and there are three groups of people who remember.

**Trapped sellers.** People who shorted at the $102.30 ceiling were right twice and wrong the third time. They are now losing. When price comes back down to $102.30 they can get out flat, and many will take that chance and buy back their shorts. Their buying supports the level.

**Regretful buyers.** People who wanted to buy the breakout but missed it are waiting for a second chance. The old ceiling is the obvious place. They put their buy orders there.

**Breakout buyers adding.** People who bought the breakout and are now in profit see price return to their entry area, still holding. Many add to their position at the level.

Three different crowds, all with a reason to buy at the same price. That is what a support zone is made of, and it is why the flip tends to hold on the first return.

<Callout type="eli5">

Imagine a queue for a ride that everyone said was too scary. A few brave kids go on and love it. Now the kids who chickened out feel silly and want a turn, the kids who went want to go again, and the ones who said it was too scary want to prove they are brave after all. The same ride went from something people avoided to something everyone lines up for.

</Callout>

## Where role reversal shows up

Once you know to look, it is everywhere.

*After a breakout.* The retest of the broken level is a role-reversal test. If the level holds in its new role, the breakout is confirmed.

*In pullbacks within a trend.* An uptrend is a series of ceilings being broken and turning into floors. Each higher low often lands right on the previous higher high, because that old high has flipped to support. This is why pullback traders draw so much attention to old swing highs.

*Around round numbers.* A stock that has struggled with $100 for months and finally breaks it will very often find buyers at $100 on the way back down.

*Around gaps.* From the [gaps lesson](/learn/m01-reading-price/07-gaps), the edges of a gap frequently act as support or resistance later. A gap up leaves an old resistance behind that often becomes support if price fills back toward it.

## How to trade the flip

The trade is simple to describe. After a confirmed break of a level, wait for price to come back to that level. Look for a sign that it is holding: a hammer, a bullish engulfing candle, a long wick rejecting the zone, or simply a bounce and a higher close. Enter in the direction of the break, with a stop a little beyond the far side of the zone.

That gives you a tight, logical stop. If price closes back through the flipped level, the flip has failed, the breakout was probably a fakeout, and you want to be out anyway.

The target is the next level ahead, or a measured move if the break came out of a pattern (covered in the chart patterns module).

<Callout type="warning" title="Not every flip holds">

Role reversal is a tendency, not a law. Sometimes price breaks a level, comes back, and slices straight through. That usually means the original break was a fakeout, and you will often see it happen on the very same chart where the flip had worked beautifully before. Treat the flipped level as a hypothesis, and let the stop decide.

</Callout>

## Draw it as a zone

Everything from the [support and resistance lesson](/learn/m03-market-structure/03-support-and-resistance) still applies. The flipped level is a zone, not a line. Price often dips slightly into the old ceiling before turning, because the sellers who were trapped there are not all at exactly one price. Put your stop beyond the whole zone, not a cent below the line, or the stop hunt from the last lesson will find you.

## The mirror image

Everything above also works upside down. A support level that breaks becomes resistance. Trapped buyers sell on the return to get out flat; short sellers who missed the breakdown sell at the old floor; breakdown shorts add. When price bounces back up to the old support in a downtrend and stalls there, that is the same flip, and the same trade in reverse.

<KeyTakeaways>
- A broken resistance level tends to become support, and a broken support level tends to become resistance.
- The flip is powered by memory: trapped sellers exiting, regretful buyers entering, and breakout buyers adding, all at the same price.
- Role reversal is the mechanism behind the breakout retest and behind most pullback entries in a trend.
- Trade it by waiting for price to return to the flipped level, looking for a sign it holds, and placing the stop beyond the far side of the zone.
- Draw the flipped level as a zone; price often dips into the old level before turning.
- The flip is a tendency, not a law; when it fails, the original break was probably a fakeout.
</KeyTakeaways>
