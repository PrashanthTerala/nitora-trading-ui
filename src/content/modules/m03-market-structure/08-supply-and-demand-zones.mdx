A bakery sells out of croissants at 8am every day. By 8:05 there are none left, and people who arrive late go home disappointed. The next morning, the same people come at 7:45. They remember where the croissants were and they get there earlier.

Big buyers in a market behave the same way. When a fund wants to buy a million shares, it cannot do it in one click without sending the price rocketing. So it buys quietly across a narrow price band, and often it does not finish before price runs away. The unfilled portion of that order stays in the fund's plan. When price returns to that band, the fund buys again.

Those bands are **supply and demand zones**. They are cousins of support and resistance, but they are found differently, and they explain something support and resistance alone cannot: why some levels produce explosive reactions while others produce polite bounces.

## Demand zones and supply zones

A **demand zone** is a price area where buying was so strong that price left it quickly and did not come back for a while. A **supply zone** is the opposite: an area where selling was so strong that price fell away sharply.

The key word is *sharply*. Support and resistance are found by looking at where price *turned*. Supply and demand zones are found by looking at where price *left in a hurry*. A fast move away from an area means the orders there were badly imbalanced: many more buyers than sellers (demand) or many more sellers than buyers (supply). Imbalance is the footprint of big participants.

<Callout type="eli5">

Imagine a spring-loaded trapdoor in the floor. When you step on the right spot, it launches you into the air. After landing, you know exactly where the spring is. Next time you walk past that spot, you expect to get launched again. A demand zone is where the spring was, and the impulse is the launch that showed you.

</Callout>

## How to find a zone: base before impulse

The recipe has two ingredients.

**1. An impulse.** Look for a strong, fast move: a run of large-bodied candles in one direction, ideally with little overlap and closes near their extremes (think of the marubozu from [Module 2](/learn/m02-candlestick-patterns/05-marubozu-spinning-top)). The bigger and faster the move relative to what came before, the stronger the imbalance behind it.

**2. A base just before it.** Now look immediately to the left of the impulse. You want a small cluster of quiet candles, usually one to five of them, with small bodies and lots of overlap. That base is where the big orders were placed. Draw the zone from the low of the base to the high of the base (for demand) or from the high to the low (for supply). Some traders draw only from the candle bodies; either works if you are consistent.

The base is the zone. The impulse is the proof that the zone mattered.

## Zones versus support and resistance

Support and resistance are drawn where price turned around, usually several times. Supply and demand zones are drawn where price *departed from*, usually only once. The two overlap often, and the strongest levels on any chart tend to be places where a demand zone and a horizontal support line coincide.

| | Support and resistance | Supply and demand zones |
|---|---|---|
| Found by | Repeated turning points | A base followed by a sharp impulse |
| Touches needed | Two or more | One is enough (the impulse is the proof) |
| Strength comes from | Number and size of reactions | Speed and size of the departure |
| Weakens when | Tested many times quickly | Every return uses up the remaining orders |

## Fresh zones are the best zones

Here is a rule that surprises people. A zone that has *never* been revisited since the impulse is stronger than one that has been touched three times. The reason is the bakery: each return to the zone fills some of the leftover orders. After enough visits, the orders are gone and the zone is just a line on a chart.

So, in order of quality:

1. **Fresh**: price has not returned since the impulse. Best odds.
2. **Tested once**: still decent, especially if the first test produced a strong reaction.
3. **Tested multiple times**: weak. Expect it to break.

This is the opposite of how beginners think about support, where more touches feel safer. Both ideas are true; they describe different mechanisms.

## Trading a zone

The trade is a limit order or a wait-and-see entry at the zone, with the stop just beyond its far side. If price returns to a fresh demand zone and the zone holds, you enter long with a small stop and aim at the next supply zone or resistance above.

The size of the stop is the appeal. Because the zone is narrow (it is one to five quiet candles), the distance from your entry to the far side of the zone is small. Small risk with a target at the next zone can give risk-to-reward ratios of 3-to-1 or better on paper. That is why zone traders accept a lower win rate.

<Callout type="warning" title="Zones fail, and they fail fast">

When a zone breaks, it usually breaks the same way it formed: quickly. Price arrives, does not bounce, and slices through in a candle or two. Your stop must be in place before you enter, not a plan you intend to act on manually. And zones on lower timeframes are more numerous and less reliable than zones on the daily chart.

</Callout>

## Common mistakes

**Drawing the zone too wide.** If you include the whole pullback rather than the tight base, your stop is far away and the edge is gone.

**Ignoring the impulse quality.** A drift up of a few percent over a week is not an impulse. You want the move that made you say "where did that come from?"

**Trading against the trend.** A supply zone inside a strong uptrend is a place to take profit, not a place to short. Zones aligned with the higher-timeframe trend from the [trends lesson](/learn/m03-market-structure/01-trends) work far better than zones fighting it.

**Forgetting the memory decays.** A demand zone from three years ago is unlikely to have leftover orders behind it. Recent zones matter more.

<KeyTakeaways>
- A demand zone is the quiet base just before a sharp rally; a supply zone is the base just before a sharp drop.
- The sharp impulse is proof of an order imbalance, which is the footprint of large participants.
- Zones are found by looking for where price departed quickly, not where it turned repeatedly, so one touch is enough.
- Fresh zones that have never been revisited are strongest; every return uses up leftover orders.
- Trade a zone with the stop just beyond its far side; the narrow zone gives a small stop and a good risk-to-reward.
- Zones in the direction of the higher-timeframe trend work best; zones against it are better used as profit targets.
</KeyTakeaways>
