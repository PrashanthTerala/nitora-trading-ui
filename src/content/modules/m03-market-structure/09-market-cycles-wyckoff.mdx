Nobody is surprised when winter follows autumn. The seasons are a cycle: seeds go in the ground when it is cold and nothing looks alive, growth happens in spring, the harvest is in summer, and then the leaves fall. You cannot predict the exact day it will snow, but you would never plant tomatoes in November.

A century ago a trader named Richard Wyckoff noticed that markets have seasons too. He spent decades studying how large, well-informed participants (he called them the "composite man") build and unload positions, and he found the same four phases repeating in stocks of every size. His framework is still taught today because it describes something true about crowds and money, not something specific to 1920s Wall Street.

The four seasons are **accumulation**, **markup**, **distribution** and **markdown**. Learn to spot which one you are in, and a lot of confusing price action suddenly makes sense.

Think of a toy that is about to become popular. First, a few clever kids quietly collect it while it is cheap and nobody cares (winter: accumulation). Then everyone at school wants it and the price shoots up (spring: markup). The clever kids sell theirs to the excited latecomers while the price is high and stays high for a while (summer: distribution). Then the craze ends and the price collapses (autumn: markdown). The clever kids are already looking for the next toy.

## Winter: accumulation

After a long decline, sellers are exhausted. The news is bad, sentiment is terrible, and most people have given up. This is when large, patient buyers start to build positions.

They cannot buy all at once without pushing the price up, so they buy on every dip, quietly, over weeks or months. The result is a **range** (from [the ranges lesson](/learn/m03-market-structure/05-ranges-and-consolidation)) that looks boring and directionless. Inside it, you often see:

- A sharp final drop on heavy volume (the **selling climax**), then a bounce.
- Repeated tests of the range low on *decreasing* volume, each one holding: sellers are running out.
- Sometimes a **spring**: a brief fakeout below the range low that reverses fast. This is the stop hunt from the [breakouts lesson](/learn/m03-market-structure/06-breakouts-and-fakeouts), and in Wyckoff's view it is where the last weak holders are shaken out.

Nobody rings a bell. Accumulation is only obvious afterwards. But a long range at low prices, with shrinking volume on the dips and a failed breakdown, is the best clue you will get.

## Spring: markup

Eventually the big buyers have most of what they want, supply is thin, and price breaks out of the range. This is **markup**: the uptrend from [lesson one](/learn/m03-market-structure/01-trends), higher highs and higher lows, with volume expanding on the impulses.

Markup is when the public notices. The news improves, analysts upgrade, and each pullback attracts new buyers who wish they had bought earlier. The role-reversal flips you learned about happen one after another as old ceilings become floors. This is the easiest season to trade because the trend is clear and the crowd is on your side.

## Summer: distribution

The trend slows. Price makes a new high, but the pullbacks get deeper and the rallies get shorter. The big buyers from winter are now selling, but they face the same problem in reverse: dumping everything at once would crash the price. So they sell into every rally, quietly, and price goes sideways near the top.

This is **distribution**, and it looks like accumulation flipped upside down:

- A buying climax: an exuberant surge on huge volume that fails to hold.
- Repeated tests of the range high, each one weaker, with rallies on shrinking volume.
- Sometimes an **upthrust**: a brief fakeout above the range high that reverses. Latecomers buy the breakout and become the trapped traders.

Sentiment during distribution is excellent. Everyone is bullish. That is precisely the point: large sellers need eager buyers to sell to.

## Autumn: markdown

When the smart sellers are done, there is nobody left to hold the price up. It breaks below the distribution range and the downtrend begins: lower highs and lower lows, with role reversal turning old floors into ceilings. Bounces get sold. Bad news arrives to "explain" the decline that had already started. Markdown continues until sellers are exhausted, and then it is winter again.

## Why the cycle matters

The point is not to predict exactly when each season turns. Nobody can. The point is to know what *kind* of market you are in, because each season rewards a different approach.

| Season | Structure | What usually works | What usually fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| Accumulation | Range after a decline, shrinking volume on dips | Fading the range low with tight stops; waiting for the breakout | Shorting breakdowns (they spring back) |
| Markup | Higher highs and higher lows | Buying pullbacks to flipped levels | Trying to pick the top |
| Distribution | Range after a rally, weak rallies | Taking profit; fading the range high | Buying breakouts (they upthrust) |
| Markdown | Lower highs and lower lows | Selling rallies; staying out | Buying the dip because it looks cheap |

The single most useful habit: when a range forms, ask *what came before it*. A range after a long decline is probably winter. A range after a long rally is probably summer. That one question changes which way you lean on the breakout.

<Callout type="warning" title="The cycle is a map, not a timetable">

Seasons on a chart do not last a fixed length. Markup can run for years or fizzle in a month. Distribution can be a clean range or a chaotic mess of fakeouts. Real charts are messier than the diagram, and you will often only be sure which phase you were in after it ends. Use the cycle to frame your expectations, and use structure (swings, levels, volume) to make actual decisions.

</Callout>

## Fractals: seasons within seasons

The cycle repeats at every timeframe. A daily chart in markup will contain small accumulation and distribution ranges on the hourly chart at each pullback. A weekly distribution can contain a full daily-chart cycle. This is exactly why the next lesson, on multi-timeframe analysis, matters: the season on the higher timeframe tells you which side of the smaller ranges to trust.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Markets move through four repeating phases: accumulation, markup, distribution, markdown, like the four seasons.
- Accumulation is a range after a decline where big buyers build positions quietly; markup is the uptrend that follows.
- Distribution is a range after a rally where those buyers sell to the excited public; markdown is the decline that follows.
- Springs (fakeouts below accumulation) and upthrusts (fakeouts above distribution) shake out weak hands and trap latecomers.
- To guess which season a range belongs to, ask what came before it.
- The cycle is a map for expectations, not a timetable; real phases vary wildly in length and messiness.
</KeyTakeaways>
