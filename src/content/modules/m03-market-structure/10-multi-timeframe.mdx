Planning a road trip, you start with a map of the whole country to pick the route. Then you zoom into the city to find the right roads. Only at the very end do you look at the individual street to find the driveway. You would never plan a trip by staring at one driveway, and you cannot park a car using a map of the country.

Charts work exactly the same way. Every timeframe you learned about in [the timeframes lesson](/learn/m01-reading-price/05-timeframes) is a different zoom level of the same road. The daily chart tells you which direction the traffic is flowing. The hourly chart shows you where the good junctions are. The 5-minute chart shows you the precise moment to turn.

**Multi-timeframe analysis** is the routine for using all three without getting lost. It is the last lesson in this module because it ties everything else together: trends, swings, levels, breakouts and cycles all look different depending on where you are standing.

## The problem it solves

Here is a scene that plays out thousands of times a day. A trader sees a beautiful uptrend on the 5-minute chart. Higher highs, higher lows, a clean trendline. They buy. Twenty minutes later the trade is underwater and the "uptrend" is gone.

What happened? Zoom out. On the hourly chart, that 5-minute uptrend was a small bounce inside a downtrend, right into a resistance zone. On the daily chart, that resistance zone was the underside of a level that broke a week earlier and flipped, exactly as the [role reversal lesson](/learn/m03-market-structure/07-role-reversal) described. The trade was doomed before it started, and the 5-minute chart could not have told you.

Every timeframe is true. But the higher timeframe has more money behind it. A daily-chart swing represents days of decisions by every participant; a 5-minute swing represents a few minutes of a few of them. When they disagree, the bigger one usually wins.

<Callout type="eli5">

A little boat on the sea can row in any direction it likes, but if the tide is going out, the boat ends up further from the beach no matter how hard it rows toward it. The daily chart is the tide. The 5-minute chart is the rowing. You want to row with the tide, not against it.

</Callout>

## The top-down routine

Pick three timeframes. A common set, and the one this lesson uses, is **daily, hourly, 5-minute**. Each one is roughly four to six times the one below it, which keeps them related without being redundant. Then work strictly from the top down.

### Step 1: Daily chart for bias

Ask one question: which way is the tide? Use the [trend definition](/learn/m03-market-structure/01-trends): higher highs and higher lows, lower highs and lower lows, or range. Mark the two or three nearest support and resistance zones. Note which Wyckoff season it looks like.

The output of this step is a single word: **long**, **short**, or **neutral**. That is your bias. If the daily chart is in an uptrend and price is above the nearest support, the bias is long. You will only look for buying setups today. You are not forbidden from ever shorting, but you accept you are fighting the tide when you do.

### Step 2: Hourly chart for the setup

Now zoom in. Given a long bias, where on the hourly chart would a buyer want to be? Usually one of:

- A pullback to a daily support zone that the hourly chart shows holding.
- A retest of a broken level, in role reversal.
- A small hourly range, ideally after a decline within the daily uptrend, that looks like accumulation.
- A fresh demand zone from the [supply and demand lesson](/learn/m03-market-structure/08-supply-and-demand-zones).

The output of this step is a **location**: a specific zone where, if price arrives and behaves, you will act. If there is no such location, there is no trade. That is a perfectly good outcome; it is most outcomes.

### Step 3: 5-minute chart for the entry

Price has arrived at your hourly location. Now, and only now, open the 5-minute chart. You are looking for the small structure to *turn* in the direction of your bias: a 5-minute lower low that fails to follow through, then a 5-minute higher high. Or a reversal candle from Module 2, such as a hammer or engulfing candle, printing right at the zone.

The 5-minute chart gives you two things: a precise entry price and a tight stop (just beyond the 5-minute swing low that formed at the zone). Your target still comes from the higher timeframes: the next daily or hourly resistance.

## Alignment: when all three agree

The best trades are the ones where all three timeframes tell the same story: daily uptrend, hourly pullback to support, 5-minute reversal at that support. When they align, the trade has the tide, the current and the rowing all going one way.

When two of the three agree, the trade is possible but weaker. When the daily and hourly disagree, most traders should wait. A daily uptrend with an hourly downtrend is just a pullback in progress; it is not a short, and it is not a long either until the hourly chart turns.

| Daily | Hourly | 5-minute | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Up | Up | Up | Strong, but possibly extended: look for a small pullback |
| Up | Down (pullback) | Turning up at support | The classic high-quality long |
| Up | Down | Down | Wait; the pullback is not finished |
| Range | Range | Anything | Fade the edges of the daily range only |
| Down | Up (bounce) | Turning down at resistance | The classic high-quality short |

## The most common mistakes

**Analysing bottom-up.** Finding a pretty 5-minute pattern and then hunting for a higher-timeframe reason to justify it. The routine only works top-down.

**Timeframe hopping to avoid a stop.** You are long on the 5-minute chart, it goes against you, so you "switch to the hourly" where the trade still looks fine. Your stop was set on the 5-minute structure. Honour it. Changing the ruler after the fact is the same sin as [moving your swing points](/learn/m03-market-structure/02-swing-highs-and-lows).

**Too many timeframes.** Three is plenty. Five is paralysis.

**Ignoring higher-timeframe levels on the lower chart.** A daily resistance zone is invisible on the 5-minute chart because it is off the screen. Draw the daily levels first, then zoom in with them still on the chart.

<Callout type="warning" title="Bias is not a prediction">

A long bias means you will only take longs, not that the market must go up. The daily uptrend can end today. That is what the stop is for. The bias filters which trades you consider; the structure on the lower timeframes decides whether you actually take one.

</Callout>

## Adapting the trio

The daily, hourly, 5-minute set suits day traders. Swing traders holding for days might use weekly, daily, 4-hour. Position traders might use monthly, weekly, daily. The names change; the roles do not. The highest timeframe gives bias, the middle gives the setup location, the lowest gives the entry trigger and the stop.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Every timeframe is true, but the higher one has more money behind it, so when they disagree it usually wins.
- Work top-down: the daily chart gives your bias, the hourly chart gives the setup location, the 5-minute chart gives the entry and the stop.
- The output of the daily step is one word (long, short or neutral); the output of the hourly step is a specific zone; the output of the 5-minute step is a turn in structure at that zone.
- The best trades have all three timeframes aligned; when the daily and hourly disagree, wait.
- Never hop timeframes to justify keeping a losing trade; the stop was set on the timeframe you entered on.
- Three timeframes, each four to six times the one below, is enough for any style.
</KeyTakeaways>
