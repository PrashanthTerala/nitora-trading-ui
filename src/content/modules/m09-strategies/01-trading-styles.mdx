Imagine four people fishing the same lake. One stands in the shallows with a tiny net, scooping minnows all day: hundreds of catches, each one small. One casts from the pier for an hour each morning and takes home a couple of fish. One sets a line in the evening and checks it every few days. One drops a lobster pot and comes back in a month.

Same lake, same fish, four completely different jobs. They need different equipment, different amounts of time, and different temperaments. The minnow-scooper would go mad waiting a month for a lobster pot. The lobster fisher would go broke chasing minnows.

Trading styles are exactly this. The market is one lake. How long you hold, how often you act, and how much of your day it eats are choices, and the most common beginner mistake is choosing the style that *looks* exciting rather than the one that fits your life.

## The four styles

**<Term id="scalping">Scalping</Term>** is the minnow net. Trades last seconds to a few minutes. The scalper takes many small profits, often just a few ticks, and relies on volume of trades. It requires a fast platform, tight spreads, full attention, and very low costs. Most beginners who try it lose to the [spread and commissions](/learn/m06-orders-and-execution/09-fees-commissions-settlement) before they lose to the market.

**<Term id="day-trading">Day trading</Term>** is the pier. Trades open and close within a single session; nothing is held overnight. Holding periods run from a few minutes to a few hours. It needs a chunk of dedicated screen time each day, typically the first and last hours when volume is highest.

**<Term id="swing-trading">Swing trading</Term>** is the evening line. Trades last from a couple of days to a few weeks, aiming to catch one "swing" of a trend from a [swing low to a swing high](/learn/m03-market-structure/02-swing-highs-and-lows). Analysis happens on daily and 4-hour charts, usually in the evening, and the trade is managed with resting orders. It fits around a job.

**<Term id="position-trading">Position trading</Term>** is the lobster pot. Trades last weeks to months, sometimes longer. The position trader rides an entire trend on weekly and daily charts, checks in rarely, and accepts large swings along the way. It sits on the border with investing, and the difference is that a position trader still has a defined exit.

<Callout type="eli5">

A scalper is a shopkeeper making a penny on every sweet sold, all day long. A position trader is a farmer planting in spring and harvesting in autumn. Both can make a living. The shopkeeper who tries to farm, or the farmer who tries to run a sweet shop, usually does not.

</Callout>

## Side by side

| | Scalping | Day trading | Swing trading | Position trading |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Holding period** | Seconds to minutes | Minutes to hours, flat by close | Days to weeks | Weeks to months |
| **Chart timeframes** | 1m and below, tick charts | 1m to 15m entries, 1h context | 1h to 4h entries, daily context | Daily entries, weekly context |
| **Screen time per day** | Full session, unbroken | 2 to 6 hours | 20 to 60 minutes | A few minutes, a few times a week |
| **Typical stop distance** | Very tight, a few ticks or 0.1% to 0.3% | 0.3% to 1.5% | 2% to 6% | 8% to 20% or more |
| **Trades per week** | Hundreds | 5 to 30 | 1 to 5 | Less than 1 |
| **Cost sensitivity** | Extreme; spread is most of the edge | High | Moderate | Low |
| **Overnight risk** | None | None | Yes, gaps matter | Yes, and news events |
| **Stress level** | Very high, constant | High, in bursts | Moderate, intermittent | Low day to day, high during drawdowns |
| **Capital needed** | Higher, because of costs and size per trade | Moderate, and some markets have minimum rules | Low to moderate | Low to moderate |
| **Skills that matter most** | Execution speed, order flow | Intraday structure, discipline | Patience, structure, risk | Patience, macro awareness |

A few things in that table deserve a closer look.

**Stop distance scales with holding period.** A scalper's stop might be 0.2%; a position trader's might be 15%. That is fine, because [position size](/learn/m07-risk-management/02-the-one-percent-rule) adjusts: the scalper buys many shares, the position trader buys few. Risk per trade stays at 1% either way. What changes is the number of trades needed to reach the same result.

**Costs hurt faster styles more.** If your average target is 0.3% and the spread plus commission is 0.1%, a third of every winner goes to costs. If your target is 10%, the same 0.1% is noise. This is why scalping is realistically closed to most retail traders: the math rarely works.

**Screen time and stress are linked.** The faster the style, the more of your day it consumes and the more decisions per hour you make. From the [psychology module](/learn/m08-trading-psychology/07-routines-and-discipline), you know that every decision spends willpower. Faster styles spend it faster.

## Which one fits you?

Answer honestly, not aspirationally.

**How many hours a day can you give the market, at the same time every day?** If the answer is "less than two, and they vary", day trading and scalping are out. Not because you lack skill, but because the style needs a fixed block of attention you do not have.

**How do you handle overnight uncertainty?** Swing and position traders hold through news, [gaps](/learn/m01-reading-price/07-gaps) and weekends. If a position open over the weekend would keep you awake, you either need a day-trading style or a much smaller size.

**Do you need frequent feedback?** Some people cannot wait three weeks to find out if a trade worked. Others find twenty decisions a day exhausting. Neither is right; they are just different, and they point to different styles.

**What are your costs?** Check your broker's spread and commission on the instruments you want to trade. If the round-trip cost is more than about 10% of your average expected win, the style is too fast for that broker or that instrument.

<Callout type="warning">

Beginners drift toward day trading and scalping because the action is constant and the feedback is instant. Those are exactly the properties that make them hardest: constant action means constant decisions, and instant feedback means constant emotion. Most people who are consistently profitable started slower and moved faster only after they had proven a process.

</Callout>

## The honest recommendation

If you have a day job, start with swing trading on the daily and 4-hour charts. It gives you time to think, uses resting orders so you are not watching every candle, and its costs are forgiving. The setups you learned in [market structure](/learn/m03-market-structure/01-trends) and [chart patterns](/learn/m04-chart-patterns/01-chart-patterns-intro) all work on those timeframes.

If you have a free block of two hours every morning and a calm temperament, day trading on 5m and 15m charts is workable, and the simulator's replay mode lets you practise it at any hour.

Whatever you pick, pick *one*. A day trader who also has three swing positions and a "long-term" bag they refuse to sell is running three strategies with none of the discipline of any of them.

<Callout type="tip">

The simulator can run any style. To feel the difference, replay the same week of NOVA three times: once on 1m making decisions every few bars, once on 15m, and once on 1D at high speed. Notice which one you found tiring and which one you found boring. Your style is somewhere between those.

</Callout>

<KeyTakeaways>
- The four styles differ mainly in holding period, which drives the timeframes, screen time, stop distance and costs that go with them.
- Scalping is realistically closed to most retail traders because spread and commission eat most of the edge.
- Stop distance scales with holding period, but risk per trade stays at 1% because position size adjusts.
- Choose a style by the hours you actually have, your tolerance for overnight risk, and your need for feedback, not by which looks exciting.
- If you have a job, swing trading on daily and 4-hour charts is the most forgiving place to start; whatever you pick, pick one.
</KeyTakeaways>
