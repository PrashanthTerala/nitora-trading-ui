Picture a rope with a red ribbon tied at the middle and a team pulling at each end. The referee marks where the ribbon starts, and marks it again when the whistle blows. But he also pushes two pins into the ground: one at the furthest point the ribbon ever reached towards the left team, one at the furthest it reached towards the right.

When the match ends you can read the whole contest from four marks. Where it started. Where it finished. How far each side ever got. You were not there, and you still know whether it was a rout, a nail-biter, or a comeback.

A **candlestick** is that referee's report. Four numbers, drawn as one small shape, summarising a battle between buyers and sellers over one period of time. This is the most important lesson in the module, because every pattern in the next sixteen lessons is just candles like these arranged in a row. Read this one slowly.

## The four prices

Every candle is built from exactly four numbers, always in this order. Traders say them as "OHLC", and as you saw in [Line, Bar and Candlestick Charts](/learn/m01-reading-price/02-line-bar-candle), an OHLC bar carries the identical four.

**Open.** The <Term id="open">open</Term> is the price of the first trade of the period. On a daily candle that is the first print at the opening bell. On a 5-minute candle it is the first trade in those five minutes.

**High.** The <Term id="high">high</Term> is the highest price anyone paid at any moment during the period. It may have happened in the first second or the last.

**Low.** The <Term id="low">low</Term> is the lowest price anyone paid during the period. Same deal: no information about when.

**Close.** The <Term id="close">close</Term> is the price of the final trade of the period. It is the most important of the four, because it is the price both sides were willing to live with when the period ran out.

Three of those four are facts about the journey. Only one, the close, is the verdict.

## The parts of the drawing

Those four numbers become two visible parts: a fat rectangle and one or two thin lines.

<PatternFigure name="anatomy-bullish" caption="A bullish candle. The body runs from the open at the bottom to the close at the top. The upper wick reaches the high, the lower wick reaches the low." />

**The body** is the rectangle between the open and the close. Its top and bottom are those two prices, and nothing else. The <Term id="body">body</Term> tells you the *net result* of the period: how far price travelled from start to finish, and in which direction.

**The upper wick** is the thin line poking out of the top of the body, running up to the high. It measures the ground buyers took and then gave back. If the high equals the top of the body, there is no upper wick at all.

**The lower wick** is the thin line below the body, running down to the low. It measures the ground sellers took and then gave back. <Term id="wick">Wicks</Term> are also called shadows, or tails, depending on the book.

Now here is the bearish version, where the same four labels sit in different places.

<PatternFigure name="anatomy-bearish" caption="A bearish candle. The open is now at the TOP of the body and the close at the bottom, because price finished lower than it started. The wicks still reach the high and the low." />

Compare the two figures carefully. In the bullish candle the open is the *bottom* of the body and the close is the *top*. In the bearish candle they swap: the open is the *top* and the close is the *bottom*. The high is always the very top of the whole shape and the low is always the very bottom, no matter the colour. Getting this swap into your bones is the single most valuable thirty seconds of this module.

<Callout type="eli5">

Imagine a swimmer in a lane. She starts at one end (the open) and finishes somewhere (the close) — that stretch is the body, the part that counts on the scoreboard. But during the race she also drifted to the deep end and to the shallow end for a moment. Those two furthest points are the wicks. They happened, but they are not where she ended up.

</Callout>

## What each part tells you about the battle

Now for the useful part. Every piece of the candle is evidence about who was in control and how hard the fight was.

### The total height: how violent the period was

From the low to the high is the period's **range**. A tall candle means price covered a lot of ground; a lot of people disagreed strongly about value. A short candle means everyone roughly agreed and the period was quiet. Before you read anything else, notice whether this candle is taller or shorter than its neighbours.

### The body: who won

A long body means one side dominated. The period opened, one side pushed, and price never really came back. A long green body says buyers took the period and kept it.

A tiny body means the fight ended roughly where it began. Whatever happened in the middle, neither side could bank a result. That is why a candle with almost no body has a special name, the doji, and a whole lesson devoted to it later.

### The wicks: where price was rejected

This is the part beginners skip, and it is the part professionals stare at. **A wick is a record of a failed attempt.** Price got there, and could not stay.

A long **lower** wick means sellers dragged price well down during the period, and buyers bought it all back before the close. The low was rejected. Whoever sold near the bottom is now looking at a worse price.

A long **upper** wick means buyers pushed price well up, and sellers sold it all back before the close. The high was rejected. The people who bought up there are underwater.

Look at that figure again. All three candles close at almost the same price. If you were reading a line chart, all three would be nearly identical dots. The candle tells you that one was a steady advance, one was a violent dip that got rescued, and one was a failed breakout. That difference is the entire reason candles exist.

<Callout type="warning" title="A candle hides the order of events">

A candle tells you the high and the low happened. It never tells you *when*. A candle with wicks on both ends could have gone up then down then back to the middle, or down then up then back. Two completely different stories produce the identical picture. If the order matters to your decision, you must drop to a smaller timeframe to see it.

</Callout>

## Reading a candle out loud

Build this habit now and it will pay you for years. When you look at a candle, narrate it in one sentence with real numbers.

"It opened at 100, sellers dragged it to 97, buyers took it all the way back and closed it at 104, above the open. Sellers tried and lost."

Notice the structure: open, worst point, best point, close, verdict. If you cannot say that sentence about a candle, you have not read it yet — you have only glanced at its colour.

- The high is always the very top of the shape and the low is always the very bottom, whatever the colour.
