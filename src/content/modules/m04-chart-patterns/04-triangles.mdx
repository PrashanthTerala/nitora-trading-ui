Two people are haggling over a bicycle. The seller starts at $100, the buyer offers $60. The seller comes down to $90, the buyer goes up to $70. Then $85 and $75. Each round the gap shrinks. You can feel that the argument is nearly over, and you know that when it ends, somebody is going to walk away happy and somebody is going to feel they lost.

A **triangle** is that haggle drawn on a chart. Price makes swings that get smaller and smaller, squeezed between two converging lines, until one side gives up and the argument ends with a breakout. Triangles are the most common consolidation shape in markets, and unlike the reversal patterns in the last two lessons, most of them are continuation patterns: a trend catching its breath.

There are three flavours, and the difference between them is simply which line is flat and which line is sloping.

## The ascending triangle

<PatternFigure name="ascending-triangle" />

The rules are strict and easy to check.

1. A roughly **horizontal resistance line** across two or more swing highs at about the same price.
2. A **rising support line** across two or more swing lows, each higher than the last.
3. At least four touches in total, ideally two on each line, with real space between them.
4. The lines converge toward an apex on the right.

Read the figure as a level being attacked. Sellers have a fixed price at which they are willing to sell, say $50, and they keep filling every rally there. Buyers, meanwhile, are getting more eager: they are no longer waiting for a deep pullback, and each dip is bought a little higher than the one before. The floor is rising toward a ceiling that is not moving.

That is a fight with a predictable ending. Every rally into the ceiling consumes some of the sell orders sitting there, exactly as in the [support and resistance lesson](/learn/m03-market-structure/03-support-and-resistance). Meanwhile the buyers' pullbacks get shallower, which means there is less and less room for anyone to buy cheaply. Eventually the sell orders at $50 run out and price pops.

Ascending triangles are therefore usually bullish, and they are more reliable when they appear inside an existing uptrend.

## The descending triangle

<PatternFigure name="descending-triangle" />

The mirror. A roughly **horizontal support line** across two or more swing lows, and a **falling resistance line** across lower highs. Buyers are defending a fixed price, say $30, while sellers are increasingly impatient and sell each bounce a little lower.

The psychology is that of a shelf being worn away. Someone is absorbing every sale at $30, perhaps a large buyer with a resting order. But nobody is willing to pay up any more, so the rallies get feebler. When the $30 buyer is finished, there is nothing underneath, and the drop can be fast.

Descending triangles are usually bearish and are most reliable inside an existing downtrend.

<Callout type="eli5">

Imagine a ball bouncing in a corner made of a floor and a slanted roof. Each bounce is smaller because the roof presses down. The ball has to come out somewhere, and the sooner it runs out of room, the harder it shoots out. A triangle is that corner, and the breakout is the ball escaping.

</Callout>

## The symmetrical triangle

<PatternFigure name="symmetrical-triangle" />

Neither line is flat. The highs are getting lower and the lows are getting higher, so both lines slope toward each other at roughly similar angles. This is pure indecision: buyers and sellers are both compromising, and the market is genuinely waiting for information.

Because neither side is dominant, a symmetrical triangle does not predict direction on its own. The usual working assumption is that it breaks in the direction of the trend that came before it, and that assumption is right somewhat more often than not, but it is a tendency and nothing more. Treat a symmetrical triangle as a coiled spring of unknown orientation and let the breakout tell you.

## Volume signature

All three share the same volume story, and it follows straight from [the volume lesson](/learn/m01-reading-price/06-volume).

- Volume **shrinks steadily** as the triangle narrows. Fewer people trade while the outcome is unknown.
- Volume **expands sharply** on the breakout candle. The waiting crowd acts all at once.
- Volume often stays elevated for several candles after a genuine break.

A breakout on volume no higher than the average inside the triangle is a warning. It suggests the move is a drift rather than a decision, and drifts get reversed.

## Completion trigger

The trigger is a **close beyond the converging line**, on the timeframe you are analysing. Not a wick through it, not an intraday poke, a close.

Triangles produce more fake pokes than almost any other pattern, because their boundaries are obvious lines that everybody draws in the same place, and that is exactly where stop orders cluster. The wick-versus-close rule matters here more than anywhere else in this module.

Two extra filters are worth knowing.

**The apex rule.** A triangle should break somewhere between halfway and about three-quarters of the way to the apex. If price drifts all the way into the point of the triangle, the energy has leaked out and the eventual break tends to be weak and choppy. The pattern has a shelf life.

**The retest.** As with the neckline in [head and shoulders](/learn/m04-chart-patterns/02-head-and-shoulders), a break that comes back to test the broken line and holds gives a much tighter entry through [role reversal](/learn/m03-market-structure/07-role-reversal). Roughly half of triangle breakouts offer some kind of retest.

## Measured-move target

Measure the **height of the triangle at its widest point**, on the left, and project that distance from the point where price broke out.

Ascending triangle, worked example:

- Horizontal resistance: $50
- First swing low, the widest part of the triangle: $44
- Height: $50 minus $44 = $6
- Breakout close: $50.40
- Target: $50.40 plus $6 = **$56.40**

Descending triangle, worked example:

- Horizontal support: $30
- First swing high: $36
- Height: $6
- Breakout close: $29.60
- Target: $29.60 minus $6 = **$23.60**

Symmetrical triangle, worked example:

- First swing high $60, first swing low $52, so height is $8
- The lines converge and price closes above the upper line at $56.50
- Target: $56.50 plus $8 = **$64.50**

Note that the symmetrical target is smaller in percentage terms the later the break comes, because you project from wherever the breakout happened, and a late breakout happens closer to the apex. That is the maths agreeing with the apex rule.

## Stop placement

**Conservative:** just beyond the opposite line of the triangle, or beyond the last swing inside it. For the ascending example, a stop under the most recent higher low, say $48.20. If price falls back inside and takes out that low, the triangle has failed.

**Tight:** just beyond the retest, once the broken line has flipped and held.

Do not put the stop a few cents inside the triangle boundary you just broke. That is the single most crowded price on the chart and a normal retest wick will take you out of a trade that then works.

<Callout type="warning" title="Two touches is a guess, not a triangle">

You can draw converging lines on almost any chart if you only need one touch per line. That is drawing, not analysing. Require at least two clear touches per line, separated by time, and require the lines to actually contain price. If you had to ignore a candle to make the line fit, redraw it or drop the idea.

</Callout>

## How reliable are they, honestly

Large-sample studies put upside breakouts from ascending triangles somewhere around 65 to 75 percent, downside breaks from descending triangles in a similar range, and symmetrical triangles noticeably lower, closer to a coin flip on direction with a modest tilt toward the prior trend. Those numbers vary a lot depending on how strictly the pattern is defined, so treat them as rough.

More usefully: even when the breakout goes the "right" way, reaching the full measured-move target is much less common than breaking out at all. Plan for partial targets.

The commonest failure is the **fake break followed by a break the other way**. Price closes just beyond one line, fails to follow through, reverses back through the triangle and breaks out of the opposite side. This traps everyone who bought the first break and often produces the strongest move of the whole pattern, which is the subject of [lesson 10](/learn/m04-chart-patterns/10-pattern-failure).

<KeyTakeaways>
- A triangle is converging price action: flat resistance with rising lows (ascending), flat support with falling highs (descending), or both lines sloping (symmetrical).
- Ascending usually breaks up, descending usually breaks down, symmetrical usually follows the prior trend but is close to neutral.
- Volume shrinks as the triangle narrows and should expand sharply on the break; a quiet breakout is suspect.
- The completion trigger is a close beyond the line, and the break should come between half and three-quarters of the way to the apex.
- Measured-move target: the widest height of the triangle projected from the breakout point, for example $50 resistance minus a $44 low gives $6, so a break at $50.40 targets $56.40.
- Stops go beyond the last swing inside the triangle or beyond the retest, never a few cents inside the broken line.
</KeyTakeaways>
