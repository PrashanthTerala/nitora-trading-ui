A lift travels up a building and stops at the fourth floor. The doors open, some people get out, some get in, and for thirty seconds nothing seems to be happening. Then the doors close and it carries on up. The pause was not a change of plan; it was the building doing its job.

A **rectangle** is that pause drawn on a chart. Price stops trending and moves sideways between a horizontal ceiling and a horizontal floor for a while, then resumes in the direction it was already going. It is the plainest pattern in this module, and it is also the most common, because markets spend far more time going sideways than going anywhere.

You already know most of this shape. A rectangle is simply a [range](/learn/m03-market-structure/05-ranges-and-consolidation) that happens to sit in the middle of a trend, which is what turns it from a place to trade to a pattern to trade.

## The shape and the rules

<PatternFigure name="rectangle" />

1. A **horizontal resistance** across two or more swing highs at roughly the same price.
2. A **horizontal support** across two or more swing lows at roughly the same price.
3. At least four touches in total, alternating: high, low, high, low.
4. A clear trend into the rectangle. Without one there is nothing to continue.
5. Volume shrinking through the middle of the pattern.

"Roughly the same price" is doing real work in those rules. Treat the boundaries as zones, not hairlines, exactly as you learned in [supply and demand zones](/learn/m03-market-structure/08-supply-and-demand-zones). A rectangle whose highs are within about half a percent of each other is a clean one.

## The psychology

The trend into the rectangle created two groups. Some people made money and want to bank it. Some people missed the move and want a second chance.

Inside the rectangle those two groups trade with each other. Profit-takers sell at the ceiling, second-chance buyers buy at the floor, and neither group is large enough to break the other. Each bounce is a little less enthusiastic than the last, which is why volume fades.

The pattern resolves when one of the two groups runs out. If the profit-takers finish selling first, price breaks the ceiling and the second-chance buyers get their fill at higher prices. If the second-chance buyers lose patience first, the floor gives way.

Because the prior trend represents the market's established opinion, the break goes with the trend somewhat more often than against it. That tilt, and nothing stronger than a tilt, is what makes a rectangle a continuation pattern.

<Callout type="eli5">

Think of a tug of war where both teams are tired and the rope's centre keeps wobbling over the same two marks on the ground. Nothing happens for ages. Then one team's anchor lets go, and the rope shoots past both marks at once. The rectangle is the wobble; the breakout is the anchor letting go.

</Callout>

## Darvas boxes

In the 1950s a professional dancer named Nicolas Darvas traded from telegrams while touring, with no chart software and no news. He needed a rule simple enough to apply from a hotel room, and what he came up with was the **Darvas box**.

The idea: when a stock makes a new high, wait. The high it just made becomes the top of a box. The low it pulls back to becomes the bottom. If price stays inside that box for a few days, the box is confirmed. When price closes above the top of the box, buy; stack a new box above the old one and repeat as the stock climbs. If price falls below the bottom of the box, get out.

It is a rectangle with a mechanical definition and a built-in stop, applied only to stocks already making new highs. Two things about it are still worth copying today: the boundaries are defined by price action rather than opinion, and the exit is decided before the entry. Whether the specific rules still have an edge is a separate question, and one you should test rather than assume.

## Volume signature

Volume fades through the middle of the rectangle and should expand on the break. This matters more for upside breaks than downside ones: rallies need buyers to actually show up, while a floor can give way simply because nobody is there.

One useful early clue: watch where the volume clusters inside the box. If the heavier volume days keep happening near the ceiling while price holds up, buyers are absorbing supply and an upside break is more likely. If the heavy days happen near the floor and price keeps sagging back, the opposite.

## Completion trigger

A **close beyond the boundary**, not a wick. Rectangles have the most obvious boundaries on any chart, which means they attract the most stop orders, which means they produce the most fake pokes. This is where the close rule earns its keep.

Many traders add a filter: the close must be beyond the boundary by some minimum, often a fraction of the box height or a fraction of [ATR](/learn/m05-indicators/08-atr), to avoid being triggered by a close one cent outside a level.

## Measured-move target

Measure the **height of the box** and project it from the breakout.

Worked example:

- Box ceiling: $45.00
- Box floor: $41.00
- Height: $45.00 minus $41.00 = **$4.00**
- Price closes above the ceiling at $45.30
- Target: $45.30 plus $4.00 = **$49.30**

Downside break at $40.70 would target $40.70 minus $4.00 = **$36.70**.

Taller boxes give bigger targets but also require bigger stops, so the risk-to-reward ratio is roughly constant. What changes with box height is the *time* the move is likely to take.

## Stop placement

**Structural:** back inside the box, below its midpoint for a long. If price re-enters the rectangle and gets past the middle, the breakout has failed and you are back in the range.

**Darvas-style:** below the floor of the box. Wider, slower to trigger, and it keeps you in through a retest.

**Tight:** below the retest low once the old ceiling has flipped to support through [role reversal](/learn/m03-market-structure/07-role-reversal).

The one to avoid is a stop a few cents back inside the boundary. Price returning to test a broken level is normal behaviour, not a failure.

<Callout type="warning" title="A rectangle without a trend is just a range">

If there is no clear trend leading into the sideways move, you do not have a continuation pattern. You have a range, and the right trade in a range is usually to fade the edges, not to chase the break. Chasing breakouts from directionless ranges is one of the most reliable ways to collect small losses, because those ranges break both ways repeatedly before they mean anything.

</Callout>

## Honest reliability

Rectangles in a clear trend continue in that direction somewhere in the region of 60 to 70 percent of the time in large samples, with the rest resolving against the trend. The false-break rate before the real break is high: it is common to see one or even two closes outside the box that reverse before the genuine move.

That is frustrating but it is also useful information. A rectangle that has already faked out once has trapped a group of traders, and their forced exits often power the real break when it comes.

<KeyTakeaways>
- A rectangle is a horizontal range with a trend leading into it, which makes it a continuation pattern rather than just a range.
- It needs at least four alternating touches of a horizontal ceiling and floor, and volume should fade through the middle.
- A Darvas box is a mechanical rectangle built on new highs, with the box bottom as a pre-planned exit.
- The completion trigger is a close beyond the boundary, ideally by a meaningful amount rather than a cent.
- Measured-move target: box height projected from the break, so a $4 box broken at $45.30 targets $49.30.
- Stops go back inside the box below the midpoint, or below the box floor, never a few cents inside the broken boundary.
</KeyTakeaways>
