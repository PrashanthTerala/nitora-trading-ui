Most of the patterns so far have been arguments: two sides fighting over a level, with a winner and a loser. This one is different. A **cup and handle** is what a chart looks like when nobody is arguing at all, and everyone quietly changes their mind over several months.

Picture a tea cup seen from the side. Price drifts down in a long, rounded curve, spends a while at the bottom, and drifts back up to roughly where it started. That is the cup. Then, near the old high, it dips gently one last time, and that small dip is the handle. When price breaks out above the handle, the pattern completes.

The shape was popularised by William O'Neil in the 1980s as a base-building pattern for growth stocks, and it is still one of the most-watched setups among swing traders. Its cousin, the **rounding bottom**, is the same curve without the handle.

## The cup

<PatternFigure name="cup-and-handle" />

The rules that matter:

1. **A prior advance.** The cup is a base, and a base needs something to consolidate. O'Neil wanted to see a meaningful uptrend, on the order of 30 percent, before the cup formed.
2. **A U, not a V.** The bottom should be rounded, with price spending time down there. A sharp V bottom means the decline was arrested by panic rather than by patience, and those bases are weaker.
3. **Sensible depth.** A cup that retraces roughly 12 to 33 percent of the prior advance is healthy. Much deeper than about a third and the "consolidation" is really a downtrend.
4. **Time.** Weeks to months on a daily chart, commonly seven weeks or more. A cup that forms in four days is not a cup.
5. **A right rim near the left rim.** The right side of the cup should come back up to roughly the level of the left rim, which is the old high.

### Psychology

The left rim is where an advance ran into sellers. Price rolls over, and the decline is gradual rather than violent: holders lose interest, sell slowly, and the stock goes quiet.

At the bottom, the people who were going to sell have sold. Volume dries up, which is the signature of a base. Then buyers reappear, gently, and price grinds back toward the old high.

The right rim is the interesting part. Everyone who bought at the left rim months ago and has been sitting on a loss is now back at break-even, and a chunk of them sell for relief. That selling is the handle.

<Callout type="eli5">

Imagine a class that failed a test, spent a term quietly studying, and is now taking it again. Just before the results come out, the ones who nearly failed last time get nervous and want to drop the class. That small wobble of nerves is the handle. When the results come back good and nobody drops, everyone piles back in.

</Callout>

## The handle

The handle is not decoration; it is the entry trigger and the risk control, and it has its own rules.

- It forms in the **upper half of the cup**. A handle that sags below the midpoint of the cup is a warning that the base is failing, not resting.
- It **drifts downward** gently, often in a small channel or [flag](/learn/m04-chart-patterns/05-flags-and-pennants) shape. A handle that shoots sideways-up into the rim is really just a breakout without a pause.
- It is **shallow**: commonly a 10 to 15 percent pullback from the right rim, and less on lower-volatility instruments.
- It is **short**: typically one to four weeks on a daily chart, versus months for the cup.
- Volume inside it **dries up noticeably**. This is the clearest confirmation that the dip is relief selling rather than new supply.

The high of the handle is the **pivot**, the price that triggers the trade.

## Volume signature

The cup and handle has the most distinctive volume shape in this module.

- Falling volume down the left side of the cup.
- The lightest volume of the whole pattern at the base.
- Slowly rising volume up the right side.
- A drop again inside the handle.
- A sharp expansion on the breakout, ideally well above the recent average.

That last point is close to a requirement rather than a nice-to-have. O'Neil's own rule of thumb was breakout volume at least 40 to 50 percent above normal. A cup and handle that breaks out on quiet volume has a poor record, because the whole premise is that a crowd of buyers has been waiting.

## Completion trigger

A **close above the high of the handle**, on expanding volume. Not a wick through the pivot, and not the right rim being touched. The handle high is the line that matters.

Waiting for the close costs you a little price and saves you from a large share of the failures, which is the same trade-off you have made in every lesson of this module.

## Measured-move target

Measure the **depth of the cup**, from the rim down to the base, and add it to the breakout point.

Worked example:

- Left rim (old high): $50.00
- Cup base: $40.00
- Cup depth: $50.00 minus $40.00 = **$10.00**
- The handle drifts from $50.00 down to $47.00
- Price closes above the handle high at $50.30
- Target: $50.30 plus $10.00 = **$60.30**

A more conservative second target uses the **handle depth** instead: $50.00 minus $47.00 = $3.00, giving $50.30 plus $3.00 = $53.30. Many traders take partial profit at the handle-depth target and let the rest run toward the cup-depth target. On a deep cup the full target can be a long way away and may take months, so knowing which number you are trading for changes how you manage the position.

## Stop placement

**Standard:** just below the **low of the handle**, $46.80 in the worked example. If price falls back through the handle, the relief selling was not relief selling.

**Tighter:** below the low of the breakout day, or below the retest low if the pivot flips to support.

**Percentage backstop:** O'Neil paired the pattern with a hard rule to cut any loss at 7 to 8 percent from entry, so you exit at whichever comes first, the handle low or that percentage. Whether or not you use that exact number, having a maximum loss that does not depend on where the chart happens to put a swing low is a sound idea, and it connects directly to [position sizing](/learn/m07-risk-management/02-the-one-percent-rule).

## Rounding bottoms and rounding tops

<PatternFigure name="rounding-bottom" />

A **rounding bottom**, or saucer, is the cup without the handle: a long, slow, symmetrical U that turns a downtrend into an uptrend over weeks or months. Volume traces its own U shape, heavy at the edges and thin at the base.

Because there is no handle, there is no neat pivot to trade. The usual trigger is a close above the level of the left rim, and the target is the depth of the saucer added to that level. The trade is slower and the stop is wider, so size accordingly.

**Rounding tops** exist too: the same curve upside down, at the end of an advance. They are less reliable than rounding bottoms, for a reason worth remembering. Bottoms form through exhaustion and boredom, which take time and therefore curve. Tops often form through a sudden shock, which spikes. A genuinely rounded top usually means distribution, but you will misidentify more of them than you will bottoms.

<Callout type="warning" title="The handle is where most people get this wrong">

Two handle mistakes cost the most money. The first is buying the right rim instead of waiting for the handle and the pivot break, which means buying straight into the relief selling. The second is accepting a handle that sags into the lower half of the cup and calling it "a deep handle". A handle below the cup's midpoint has a materially worse record; at that point the base is being rebuilt, not finished.

</Callout>

## Honest reliability

Cup and handle is a genuine pattern with a genuine edge in the right conditions, which are: a real prior uptrend, a market that is generally rising, a rounded base and a volume expansion at the pivot. Under those conditions, reported success rates sit in the same broad band as the other good patterns in this module, roughly two in three.

Outside those conditions it degrades quickly. The pattern was designed for growth stocks in bull markets, and it performs noticeably worse in falling markets, on indices, and on anything with no real earnings story behind it. The commonest failure is the **breakout that immediately fails back below the pivot**, trapping breakout buyers. If that happens on heavy volume, exit rather than hope; the next lesson but one covers why those failures move so fast.

<KeyTakeaways>
- A cup and handle is a long rounded base after an advance, followed by a short shallow dip near the old high; the handle high is the pivot that triggers the trade.
- The cup should be a U rather than a V, retrace roughly 12 to 33 percent of the prior advance, and take weeks to months.
- The handle must form in the upper half of the cup, drift gently down and show clearly shrinking volume.
- The completion trigger is a close above the handle high on volume well above average.
- Measured-move target: cup depth added to the breakout, so a $10 cup broken at $50.30 targets $60.30, with the shallower handle-depth target at $53.30 as a first stop.
- A rounding bottom is the same curve without a handle, triggered on a close above the left rim; rounding tops are the same shape but less reliable because tops more often spike than curve.
</KeyTakeaways>
