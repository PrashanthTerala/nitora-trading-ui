If you compress a spring by four centimetres and let go, you expect it to fly roughly four centimetres past where it started. You do not expect exactly four, because of friction and air and the fact that springs are not perfect. But four is a much better guess than zero, and it is a far better guess than "it will fly forever".

That is the entire idea behind a **measured move**. Every pattern in this module has a height. The height is how far price travelled while the argument was going on. When the argument resolves, the crowd tends to push price about that same distance again, because the same people, with the same amount of money and the same appetite for risk, are still doing the pushing.

This lesson pulls all the target rules together, works four of them in numbers, and then does the more important job: telling you honestly how often targets are actually reached and what to do about it.

One note before the arithmetic: the figures below are reminders of each shape, while the prices in each worked example are round numbers chosen to keep the sums readable. Do not try to read the example prices off the charts.

## The general rule

Every measured move in this module is the same three steps.

1. **Find the height of the pattern.** Measure from its extreme to the line that defines it, at the pattern's widest point.
2. **Find the completion point.** This is where price closed beyond the line, not where the line sits.
3. **Project the height from the completion point**, in the direction of the break.

That is it. Head and shoulders, double tops, triangles, rectangles, flags, wedges and cups all use variations on those three steps. The differences are only in what counts as "the height" for each shape.

<Callout type="eli5">

Measure how deep the puddle is. That is roughly how high the splash will be. It is not a law, it is a sensible guess based on the fact that the same amount of water is involved either way.

</Callout>

## Worked example 1: head and shoulders

<PatternFigure name="head-and-shoulders" />

- Head high: **$220.00**
- Neckline at the break: **$190.00**
- Height: $220.00 minus $190.00 = **$30.00**
- Completion: a close below the neckline at $189.40
- Target: $189.40 minus $30.00 = **$159.40**

Note that the height is measured from the head, the pattern's extreme, and not from the shoulders. The shoulders are how the pattern is recognised; the head is how big it is.

## Worked example 2: ascending triangle

- Horizontal resistance: **$75.00**
- The first swing low, the widest part of the triangle: **$68.00**
- Height: **$7.00**
- Completion: a close above resistance at $75.40
- Target: $75.40 plus $7.00 = **$82.40**

The widest point matters. If you measure the triangle near its apex, where it might only be $1.50 tall, you get a target barely above the entry. The pattern's energy came from the whole coil, so measure the whole coil.

## Worked example 3: bull flag

<PatternFigure name="bull-flag" />

- Flagpole: price ran from **$12.00** to **$15.00**, so the pole is **$3.00**
- The flag drifts back to $14.20
- Completion: a close above the flag at $14.60
- Target: $14.60 plus $3.00 = **$17.60**

Flags are the one case where the height being measured is not the consolidation itself but the move *before* it. The flag is the pause; the pole is the energy.

## Worked example 4: double top

<PatternFigure name="double-top" />

- Peaks: **$64.00**
- Neckline through the trough between them: **$58.00**
- Height: **$6.00**
- Completion: a close below the neckline at $57.70
- Target: $57.70 minus $6.00 = **$51.70**

## Turning a target into a decision

A target on its own is just a number on a chart. It becomes useful when you combine it with your stop to get an **R-multiple**, the idea you will meet properly in [risk to reward](/learn/m07-risk-management/04-risk-reward-ratio).

Take the double top above.

- Entry on the neckline break: $57.70
- Stop above the neckline retest high: $59.20
- Risk per share: $59.20 minus $57.70 = **$1.50**
- Target: $51.70, so reward per share is $57.70 minus $51.70 = **$6.00**
- Reward divided by risk: $6.00 / $1.50 = **4R**

A 4R setup is worth taking even if it only works four times in ten. That single calculation is what the whole measured move is for: not to predict the future, but to tell you whether the trade is worth the risk *before* you take it. If the sums come out at 0.8R, the pattern can be textbook-perfect and the trade is still not worth doing.

## Structure beats the formula

Here is the most important practical rule in this lesson. **If a significant level sits between the entry and the measured target, the level is the more realistic target.**

Continue the double top example. Suppose there is a well-tested support shelf at $54.00, a level price bounced off three times last year. Price falling from $57.70 will very likely react there, and a lot of the time it will stop there.

- Realistic target: $54.00
- Reward: $57.70 minus $54.00 = **$3.70**
- R-multiple: $3.70 / $1.50 = about **2.5R**

Still a good trade, but 2.5R is the number to plan around, not 4R. Traders who ignore intervening levels systematically overestimate their targets, hold winners past the point where price stalls, and watch 2R profits turn into break-even.

The routine is: calculate the measured move, then look left. Round numbers, old swing highs and lows, [supply and demand zones](/learn/m03-market-structure/08-supply-and-demand-zones), moving averages and the prior day's extremes all count. Whatever you find first in the path of the trade is the target you should expect.

## How often targets are actually hit

Honestly: much less often than patterns complete.

Breaking out of a pattern in the expected direction happens roughly 55 to 75 percent of the time depending on the pattern, as [lesson 1](/learn/m04-chart-patterns/01-chart-patterns-intro) noted. Reaching the full measured target is a different and much harder bar. Broad studies put it somewhere near half of successful breakouts for most patterns, which means that from the moment a pattern completes, the full target is reached well under half the time overall.

Two things follow.

**Scale out.** Take part of the position off at a nearby structural level or at 1R to 2R, move the stop to break-even, and let the remainder run at the measured target. This converts a low hit-rate target into a strategy that still makes money when price only goes halfway.

**Never widen the stop to reach the target.** The target is an estimate; the stop is the definition of being wrong. Moving the stop further away to "give the trade room to reach the target" is the exact mechanism by which a planned 1R loss becomes a 4R loss.

<Callout type="danger" title="A target is not a prediction and not a promise">

The most common misuse of measured moves is emotional. Traders calculate a target, become attached to it, and then refuse to take a good profit at 2R because "it should go to 4R". Markets do not owe you the measured move. If price stalls, volume dies and the candles start overlapping well short of your target, that is information, and taking what is there is a professional decision rather than a failure.

</Callout>

## A quick reference

| Pattern | Height measured as | Projected from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Head and shoulders | head to neckline | the neckline break close |
| Double or triple top or bottom | peaks or troughs to neckline | the neckline break close |
| Triangle | widest part, on the left | the breakout close |
| Rectangle or box | ceiling to floor | the breakout close |
| Flag or pennant | the flagpole, not the pause | the breakout close, or the flag extreme for a conservative target |
| Wedge | widest part, at the start | the breakout close, with the wedge origin as a second target |
| Cup and handle | rim to cup base | the close above the handle high |

Every one of these projects from where price actually closed through the line, which is why a late entry produces a smaller realistic target than an early one, and why chasing a breakout that has already run half the height is a poor trade even when the pattern is genuine.

<KeyTakeaways>
- A measured move projects the pattern's height from the point where price closed beyond the line, in the direction of the break.
- Measure from the pattern's extreme at its widest point, and for flags and pennants measure the pole rather than the pause.
- A target is only useful next to a stop: a $6.00 target with a $1.50 risk is a 4R trade, and the R-multiple is what decides whether to take it.
- If a significant support, resistance or round number sits in the path, that level is the realistic target, not the formula's number.
- Full measured targets are reached in well under half of completed patterns, so scale out at structure and let a remainder run.
- Never move the stop to make room for the target; the target is a guess and the stop is the definition of being wrong.
</KeyTakeaways>
