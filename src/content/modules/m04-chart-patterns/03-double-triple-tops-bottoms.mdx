Someone tries to push open a heavy door. It doesn't budge. They step back, take a run at it, hit the same spot, and bounce off again. After the second failure they usually walk away and look for another door. Occasionally they try a third time, and if that fails too, they are definitely done.

That is the **double top**: price hits a ceiling, retreats, tries the same ceiling again, and fails. Flip it and you have the **double bottom**. Add a third attempt and you have triple tops and bottoms. These are the simplest reversal patterns in the book, and they are built from a single idea you already know: a [resistance or support zone](/learn/m03-market-structure/03-support-and-resistance) that gets tested and holds.

## The double top

<PatternFigure name="double-top" />

The shape is a capital M. Price rises to a peak (about $108 in the figure), pulls back to a trough (about $100), rallies to roughly the same peak, and falls again. The two peaks do not need to be identical; within a percent or two is normal, and the second is often slightly lower, as in the figure.

The line through the trough between the peaks is the **neckline**, drawn here at $100. The pattern completes when price closes below it.

### Psychology

The first peak is a normal top: price reaches a level where sellers appear. The pullback is bought, because the uptrend is still intact and dip buyers are used to being rewarded.

The second rally is the test. Buyers push back to the same area, and every trader who sold the first peak is watching. If price cannot close above the first peak, the buyers have failed twice at the same spot, and the people who bought the second rally are now holding near the highs with no follow-through. When price breaks the neckline, they are trapped, and their selling completes the reversal.

### Volume

Volume is usually lower on the second peak than the first. Fewer people are willing to buy at a level that has already rejected once. The neckline break should show expanding volume. A second peak on heavier volume than the first is a warning: buyers may be about to succeed instead.

<Callout type="eli5">

Think of a high jumper who fails at a certain height. They rest, try again, and fail at the same height. Everyone watching knows that height is their limit today. The double top is the bar that was not cleared twice, and the neckline break is the jumper packing up and leaving.

</Callout>

## The double bottom

<PatternFigure name="double-bottom" />

The mirror image, shaped like a W. Price falls to a low (about $92), bounces to a peak (about $100), falls to the same low again, and bounces. The neckline runs through the peak between the bottoms at $100. The pattern completes on a close above the neckline.

The second bottom often dips slightly below the first, the way the figure's second low sits at $92.30 after a first low near $92. That small undercut is frequently a [stop hunt](/learn/m03-market-structure/06-breakouts-and-fakeouts) that flushes the last weak holders, and it does not invalidate the pattern. Volume should be lighter on the second bottom and should expand on the neckline break; rallies need buyers to show up.

## Completion trigger and entry

For both patterns, the trigger is a **close through the neckline**. Two peaks or two troughs are not a signal; they are a range with two touches. Plenty of double tops go on to make a third peak or break out to new highs. The neckline close is what turns the shape into a pattern.

A retest of the neckline after the break, flipping in [role reversal](/learn/m03-market-structure/07-role-reversal), is the lower-risk entry, exactly as with head and shoulders.

## Measured-move target

Measure the height from the peaks (or troughs) to the neckline, and project that distance from the neckline break.

Double top, using the figure:

- Peaks: $108
- Neckline: $100
- Height: $8
- Target: $100 minus $8 = **$92**

Double bottom, using the figure:

- Troughs: $92
- Neckline: $100
- Height: $8
- Target: $100 plus $8 = **$108**

Both figures happen to run right to their targets. Live charts are less polite; treat the number as an estimate.

## Stop placement

Conservative: beyond the pattern's extreme, meaning above the double top peaks or below the double bottom troughs. If price gets past there, the reversal idea is dead.

Tight: beyond the neckline retest, once it has happened.

Avoid placing the stop right at the neckline. It is the most crowded stop on the chart.

## Triple tops and bottoms

<PatternFigure name="triple-top" />

Three tests of the same level instead of two. The triple top is rarer than the double top and, when it does complete, often produces a stronger move, because three failed attempts trap more buyers and prove the level more thoroughly.

<PatternFigure name="triple-bottom" />

The neckline for a triple pattern runs across the two troughs (or peaks) between the three tests. The completion trigger, target formula and stop logic are identical to the double version: height from the tests to the neckline, projected from the break.

There is a subtlety. From the [support and resistance lesson](/learn/m03-market-structure/03-support-and-resistance), each test of a level uses up some of the orders defending it. A triple top that goes on to a fourth test is often about to break *up*, not down. Three is a strong pattern; four or five tests is a level being worn down.

<Callout type="warning" title="Two touches is a range, not a top">

The commonest mistake with these patterns is calling every two-touch resistance a double top and shorting it at the second peak. That is fading a range edge, which is a different trade with a different stop. The double top only exists once the neckline breaks. Until then, the level might hold, or it might break upward and trap you.

</Callout>

## Common failure modes

**Breakout instead of breakdown.** The second or third test succeeds and price breaks the resistance instead. Traders who shorted the peak are trapped and fuel the rally.

**Neckline fakeout.** Price closes below the neckline, then reverses back above it within a bar or two. Shorts are trapped; expect a sharp rally. This failed double top is a useful signal, covered in [lesson 10](/learn/m04-chart-patterns/10-pattern-failure).

**Peaks too far apart in time or price.** Two peaks separated by a year, or with a 10 percent height difference, are usually two unrelated tops, not one pattern. Look for peaks of similar height, separated by weeks rather than days on the daily chart, with a meaningful pullback between them (at least a few percent).

**Pattern in the wrong place.** A double top after a tiny rally in the middle of a range has nothing to reverse. Require a prior trend.

<KeyTakeaways>
- A double top is an M: two failed tests of the same ceiling, completed by a close below the neckline through the trough between them.
- A double bottom is a W: two holds of the same floor, completed by a close above the neckline through the peak between them.
- Volume is typically lighter on the second test and expands on the neckline break.
- Measured-move target: height from the tests to the neckline, projected from the break, for example $108 peaks and a $100 neckline give $92.
- Triple tops and bottoms use the same rules, are rarer, and often produce stronger moves, but a fourth test usually means the level is being worn down.
- Two touches of a level is a range, not a pattern; the neckline close is what makes it a top or bottom.
</KeyTakeaways>
