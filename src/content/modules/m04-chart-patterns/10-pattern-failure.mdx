Everyone on a small boat leans over the left side to look at a dolphin. The boat tips left. Then the dolphin surfaces on the right, everyone scrambles across, and the boat lurches right far harder than it ever leaned left, because now the passengers are not just standing on the right, they are *running* to it.

That is a failed pattern. A recognisable shape forms, a crowd of traders position themselves for the textbook outcome, and then price does the opposite. The move that follows is usually faster and larger than the move the pattern was supposed to produce, for a reason that has nothing to do with charting and everything to do with where people's orders are.

Failed patterns are not a footnote to this module. For many experienced traders they are the better trade, precisely because the whole crowd is on the wrong side.

## Why the move against a pattern is so fast

Three separate sources of buying or selling arrive at once.

**Trapped traders must exit.** Everyone who took the breakout is now losing. To get out, they must trade in the *opposite* direction to their entry. A trapped long has to sell. That is not a trader choosing to sell; that is a trader forced to sell, which is the most price-insensitive order there is.

**Their stops are all in the same place.** Because everybody drew the same line and read the same rules, the stops sit in a tight cluster just beyond the pattern boundary. When price reaches them they trigger as market orders, which pushes price into the next cluster, which triggers more. This is the cascade you met in [breakouts and fakeouts](/learn/m03-market-structure/06-breakouts-and-fakeouts).

**The other side gets confirmation.** Traders who thought the pattern was wrong now have evidence. They enter fresh positions in the new direction at the same moment.

Forced exits, triggered stops and new entries, all pointing the same way, inside a few candles. That is why failed patterns produce the sharp, seemingly unprovoked moves that make people say the market is manipulated. It is not mysterious; it is a crowded exit.

<Callout type="eli5">

Imagine a hundred people queuing at the left-hand door of a hall because a sign says the show is that way. Then the sign is corrected: the show is through the right-hand door. All hundred people turn around and rush the other way at once. The rush to the right is much more violent than the walk to the left ever was, because now everyone is moving at the same time and nobody wants to be last.

</Callout>

## Retest or failure? One question settles it

<PatternFigure name="breakout-retest" />

Price coming back to a broken level is completely normal, and you have been told all module to wait for it. So how do you tell a healthy retest from a failure?

**Which side of the level does price close on?**

- A **retest** approaches the broken level from the new side and closes back on the breakout side. Old resistance holds as support. The level is working, in [role reversal](/learn/m03-market-structure/07-role-reversal).
- A **failure** closes back *through* the level, on the side the pattern came from. The breakout has been cancelled.

Wicks into the level are retests. Closes through it are failures. That is the same close-not-wick rule you have used for every completion trigger in this module, applied in reverse.

## The anatomy of a trap

<PatternFigure name="fakeout" />

The classic sequence has four steps, and once you have seen it a few times it is unmistakable.

1. A clean, widely visible pattern forms.
2. Price breaks the boundary, often convincingly, sometimes on a decent-looking volume spike.
3. Within one to three candles, price closes back inside the pattern.
4. Price accelerates to the far side of the pattern and usually beyond it.

Step 3 is the signal. Step 4 is the trade.

## The named failures

**Failed head and shoulders.** Price closes below the neckline, shorts pile in, then price closes back above and runs. Because it is the most-watched pattern in charting, it produces the most crowded short positions, and the squeeze can carry price back above the head. This was flagged in [lesson 2](/learn/m04-chart-patterns/02-head-and-shoulders).

**Failed double top.** A close below the neckline that reverses back above it, often the start of a rally to new highs, because the pattern had convinced everyone the ceiling was permanent.

**Failed triangle break.** Price closes out of one side, reverses, then breaks out of the *opposite* side. Triangles produce this more than any other pattern because their boundaries are so obvious.

**Failed flag.** A breakout that immediately reverses through the whole flag. Flags fail fast, because the traders in them entered with tight stops close by.

## Trading the failure

The rules are the mirror of trading the pattern itself.

**Entry:** the close back inside the pattern. Not the wick, not "it looks like it is coming back", the close.

**Stop:** just beyond the extreme of the fake break. If price gets back through there, the original break may have been real after all.

**Target:** the opposite side of the pattern as a first target, and the pattern's own measured move projected in the new direction as a second.

Worked example, using the ascending triangle numbers from [lesson 4](/learn/m04-chart-patterns/04-triangles):

- Triangle resistance at $75.00, widest low at $68.00, so the height is $7.00
- Price closes above resistance at $75.60, with the fake break's high at $75.90
- The next candle closes back inside at $74.80. That is the signal.
- Entry: short at **$74.80**
- Stop: above the fakeout high, say **$76.10**, so risk is **$1.30**
- First target: the lower line of the triangle
- Second target: $75.00 minus $7.00 = **$68.00**, giving a reward of $74.80 minus $68.00 = **$6.80**
- Reward divided by risk: $6.80 / $1.30 = about **5.2R**

That ratio is the reason experienced traders like these setups. The stop is tiny, because the fakeout extreme is close by, while the target is the full width of a pattern.

## What makes a failure trustworthy

Not every close back inside a pattern is worth trading. The ones with the best record are **clean and widely visible** (obscure patterns have no crowd to trap), reverse **fast**, within one to three candles rather than drifting back over weeks, come on **rising volume**, and agree with the **higher timeframe**.

<Callout type="danger" title="Do not flip on every wiggle, and never average into the failed trade">

Two traps live here. The first is becoming a reversal trader: seeing a failure in every pullback, flipping direction constantly, and paying the [spread](/learn/m00-what-is-a-market/07-bid-ask-spread) and commissions over and over while being whipsawed both ways. Require the close, require it quickly, and take one side.

The second is worse. If you were in the original pattern trade and it failed, your job is to take the stop, full stop. Adding to the losing position because "it will come back", or flipping without first closing the loser, turns a planned small loss into the kind of loss that ends accounts. Trade the failure as a *new* trade, with fresh sizing, after the old one is closed.

</Callout>

## Honest expectations

Failed patterns have a real edge but they are not a free lunch. A good share of them simply chop sideways after the reversal instead of running to the far side, and you will take small losses on those. The spectacular ones, where the whole crowd is offside, are a minority, and they are also the memorable ones, which is exactly why your impression of this setup will be more flattering than your results. Journal them and let your own data settle it.

<KeyTakeaways>
- A failed pattern moves fast because forced exits, a tight cluster of triggered stops and fresh opposite entries all arrive at once.
- The difference between a retest and a failure is which side of the level price closes on; wicks are retests, closes through are failures.
- The classic trap is four steps: clean pattern, convincing break, close back inside within a few candles, then acceleration to the far side.
- Trade it with the close back inside as the entry, the fakeout extreme as the stop and the opposite side of the pattern as the target, which often gives a very high R-multiple.
- The most trustworthy failures come from clean, widely watched patterns, reverse quickly, and agree with the higher timeframe.
- Never add to the original failed trade or flip without closing it; trade the failure as a new position with fresh sizing.
</KeyTakeaways>
