Three children try to jump over a rope. The first clears it. The second, the strongest, clears it easily and by a lot. The third barely gets over and lands awkwardly. Now everyone knows the group is getting tired. Nobody expects the fourth jump to go well.

That is the story a **head and shoulders** pattern tells. It is the most famous shape in charting, and for good reason: it captures the exact moment an uptrend runs out of buyers. Three peaks, the middle one highest, and then a break below the level that connected the troughs. Learn this one properly and every other reversal pattern becomes a variation on it.

## The shape

<PatternFigure name="head-and-shoulders" />

Read the figure left to right.

1. **Left shoulder.** Price rises to a peak (about $104 in the figure) and pulls back.
2. **Head.** Price rises to a higher peak (about $112), then pulls back to roughly the same level as the first pullback.
3. **Right shoulder.** Price rises again but fails to reach the head, topping out near the height of the left shoulder (about $105), and pulls back once more.
4. **Neckline.** A line drawn through the two troughs between the peaks, here near $99. It can be horizontal or slightly sloped.
5. **Break.** Price closes below the neckline. The pattern is complete.

Notice that the pattern is really just an uptrend failing. The head is a higher high. The right shoulder is a **lower high**, the first one in the trend. The neckline break is the first **lower low**. Using the [trend definition](/learn/m03-market-structure/01-trends), the uptrend officially ended when the neckline broke; the pattern just gives that moment a name and a shape.

## The psychology

The left shoulder is a normal peak in a healthy uptrend. Buyers are in charge and the pullback is bought.

The head is euphoria. Price makes a new high, latecomers pile in, and volume is often heavy. But the pullback from the head falls all the way back to the previous trough, which is deeper than a healthy trend should allow.

The right shoulder is the tell. Buyers try again, but they cannot even reach the head. Everyone who bought near the head is now losing and looking for an exit. When price falls back to the neckline for the third time, the buyers who defended it twice are out of ammunition. The break is the moment they give up, and the trapped buyers from the head and right shoulder start selling into it.

<Callout type="eli5">

Imagine a tug of war where the Buyers team wins the first pull, wins the second pull by a lot, and then only just wins the third pull with everyone gasping. When the Sellers pull the fourth time, the Buyers are too tired to hold the line. The head and shoulders is the picture of a team getting tired before it loses.

</Callout>

## Volume signature

The classic signature, visible in the figure, is:

- Volume highest on the left shoulder and head, as the uptrend still attracts buyers.
- Volume noticeably lower on the right shoulder. Fewer people believe in the rally.
- Volume expanding on the neckline break, as trapped buyers sell and short sellers enter.

A right shoulder that forms on *heavy* volume, or a neckline break on *thin* volume, is a warning that the pattern may fail. Volume is not required for the pattern to work, but it strongly affects the odds, as you learned in [the breakouts lesson](/learn/m03-market-structure/06-breakouts-and-fakeouts).

## Completion trigger

The pattern is only complete when a candle **closes** below the neckline on the timeframe you are analysing. Not a wick. A close. Until that happens, you have three peaks and a hypothesis, and plenty of three-peak shapes go on to make new highs.

Many traders wait for a second confirmation: a retest of the neckline from below that fails, the [role reversal](/learn/m03-market-structure/07-role-reversal) flip. The old support (neckline) becomes resistance. This retest happens often enough that it is usually worth waiting for, and it gives you a much tighter stop.

## Measured-move target

The **measured move** is the classic way to estimate how far the break might travel. Measure the height of the pattern, from the top of the head straight down to the neckline, then project that same distance down from the neckline break.

Worked example using the figure:

- Head high: $112
- Neckline at the break: $99
- Height: $112 minus $99 = $13
- Target: $99 minus $13 = **$86**

Price in the figure runs to about $88, close to the estimate. In live markets the move may fall short or overshoot by a lot. Treat the target as a place to consider taking profit, not as a promise. Lesson 9 on measured moves covers why.

## Stop placement

There are two common choices.

**Conservative:** above the right shoulder high. If price gets back above the right shoulder, the "buyers are exhausted" story is clearly wrong. This stop is far away, so the position must be small.

**Tight:** above the neckline retest, once it has happened. If the neckline flips to resistance and holds, a stop just above that retest high risks much less. This is the entry most experienced pattern traders prefer, at the cost of sometimes missing breaks that never retest.

Never put the stop just above the neckline on the breakout candle itself. That is the most obvious place, and the [stop hunts](/learn/m03-market-structure/06-breakouts-and-fakeouts) live there.

## Inverse head and shoulders

Flip the picture and you have the bullish version: three troughs, the middle one deepest, and a neckline drawn across the two peaks between them.

<PatternFigure name="inverse-head-and-shoulders" />

Everything mirrors. The left shoulder is a normal low in a downtrend, the head is a capitulation low, the right shoulder is a higher low (the first one in the downtrend), and the close above the neckline is the first higher high. Volume on the inverse pattern matters even more: the break should come on clearly rising volume, because rallies need buyers to show up while declines can happen on their own.

Worked target: head low $90, neckline $102.80, height $12.80, target $102.80 plus $12.80 = about **$115.60**. The figure runs to $114, again close.

## Common failure modes

**The right shoulder becomes a new high.** Price breaks above the right shoulder before breaking the neckline. The pattern is void; the uptrend continues.

**The neckline breaks and immediately reverses.** A fakeout below the neckline that closes back above it traps short sellers. This is often followed by a strong rally, sometimes to new highs, because the pattern was so widely watched. [Lesson 10](/learn/m04-chart-patterns/10-pattern-failure) covers this failed head and shoulders in detail; it is one of the best-known failure signals.

**Sloped necklines.** A neckline that slopes steeply downward means the pattern is already weak (the second trough was much lower), and the break comes late. A neckline that slopes upward means the trend is still making higher lows, and the pattern is less reliable.

**Seeing it everywhere.** Any three bumps can be called a head and shoulders. Require a clear prior uptrend, a head that is obviously higher than both shoulders, and shoulders of roughly similar height. If you have to squint, it is not one.

<Callout type="danger" title="It only counts after a trend">

A head and shoulders is a reversal pattern. It needs something to reverse. Three peaks in the middle of a sideways range are not a head and shoulders; they are a range with a bump in it. Without a prior uptrend, the measured-move logic and the trapped-buyer psychology both fall apart.

</Callout>

<KeyTakeaways>
- A head and shoulders is three peaks with the middle one highest, plus a neckline through the two troughs; it is an uptrend making its first lower high and lower low.
- Volume typically fades into the right shoulder and expands on the neckline break.
- The completion trigger is a close below the neckline; a failed retest of the neckline from below is the second confirmation and the tighter entry.
- Measured-move target: pattern height (head to neckline) projected down from the break, for example $112 head, $99 neckline gives a $86 target.
- Stops go above the right shoulder (conservative) or above the neckline retest (tight), never just above the neckline on the break candle.
- The inverse pattern mirrors everything and needs rising volume on the break; a fakeout through the neckline that reverses is a strong signal the other way.
</KeyTakeaways>
