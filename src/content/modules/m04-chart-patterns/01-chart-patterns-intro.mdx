Stand on a bridge above a busy square and watch the crowd for an hour. You can't hear a single conversation, but you can see shapes: people cluster at the fountain, thin out near the road, surge toward the station when a train arrives. The shapes tell you what the crowd is thinking without a word being spoken.

A chart pattern is exactly that: the shape a crowd of traders makes over days or weeks. In [Module 2](/learn/m02-candlestick-patterns/01-how-to-read-patterns) you learned that a single candle is a snapshot of one battle. A **chart pattern** is the map of a whole campaign. It shows where the buyers dug in, where the sellers gave up, and where everybody is waiting for the outcome.

This lesson explains what patterns are made of, why they repeat, and, most importantly, how to think about them so they help rather than hypnotise.

## Patterns are made of structure

Every chart pattern is built from the pieces you learned in [Module 3](/learn/m03-market-structure/01-trends): swing highs and lows, support and resistance, trendlines and ranges. A **double top** is two swing highs at the same resistance. A **triangle** is two converging trendlines. A **flag** is a small channel after an impulse. There is nothing new under the hood.

That is good news. If you can mark swings with the [3-bar pivot rule](/learn/m03-market-structure/02-swing-highs-and-lows) and draw a level as a zone, you can already see every pattern in this module. The names are just shorthand for combinations of structure you know.

<Callout type="eli5">

Letters make words. You already know the letters: swing high, swing low, support, resistance, trendline. Chart patterns are just words spelled with those letters. Head and shoulders is a word. Triangle is a word. Once you know the letters, reading the words is easy.

</Callout>

## Why the same shapes keep appearing

Patterns repeat because people repeat. Fear, greed, regret and impatience do not change from one decade to the next. When a stock hits a ceiling twice, the second group of buyers who got rejected feel the same disappointment as the first, and they behave the same way.

Two forces shape every pattern.

**Indecision.** Most patterns are a period where neither side wins: price coils, ranges or drifts while the market waits for new information. The pattern is the shape of the waiting. Volume usually shrinks during this phase, just as it does inside any [range](/learn/m03-market-structure/05-ranges-and-consolidation).

**Resolution.** At some point one side gives up. Price breaks a level, the waiting crowd piles in, the losing crowd gets trapped and forced out, and the move that follows is the resolution. Volume usually expands here. Every pattern lesson in this module names a specific **completion trigger**, the exact event that turns "this looks like a pattern" into "this pattern has resolved."

## Continuation and reversal

Patterns come in two families.

**Reversal patterns** form at the end of a trend and signal it may turn: head and shoulders, double and triple tops and bottoms, rounding bottoms, most wedges. They are the shape of a trend running out of energy.

**Continuation patterns** form in the middle of a trend and signal it may resume: flags, pennants, rectangles, most triangles, cup and handle. They are the shape of a trend catching its breath.

The same shape can occasionally play either role. A triangle in an uptrend usually breaks up, but not always. The label is a tendency, and the completion trigger tells you what actually happened.

## The honest truth about reliability

Chart patterns fail. Often. Studies that have tried to count them find that even the best-known patterns resolve the "expected" way somewhere around 55 to 75 percent of the time, depending on the pattern, the market and how strictly you define success. That means a quarter to nearly half of the time, the textbook is wrong.

Patterns also suffer from **hindsight bias**. A finished head and shoulders is obvious. A half-formed one is just a bumpy chart, and you will see dozens of "almost" patterns for every clean one. The trainer at [/trainer](/trainer) exists partly to show you how much messier live patterns are than the diagrams.

<Callout type="warning" title="A pattern is not a signal until it completes">

The single biggest mistake with chart patterns is trading them before the trigger. Buying inside a bull flag because it "looks ready" is guessing. Waiting for the close above the flag on volume is trading the pattern. The difference is where most of the failed trades live.

</Callout>

## What each lesson will give you

For every pattern in this module, you will get the same six things:

1. **Shape**: what it looks like and how to draw it.
2. **Psychology**: what the crowd is doing to create that shape.
3. **Volume signature**: what volume usually does inside and at the break.
4. **Completion trigger**: the exact event that completes the pattern.
5. **Measured-move target**: a rough estimate of how far price might travel after completion, with a worked example.
6. **Stop placement and failure modes**: where you are wrong, and what the failure looks like.

Treat the target as an estimate and the trigger as a rule. Lesson 9 goes deep on targets; lesson 10 covers what to do when patterns fail, which turns out to be one of the more useful signals you will find.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Chart patterns are crowd behaviour drawn over days and weeks, built entirely from swings, levels and trendlines you already know.
- Patterns repeat because human fear, greed and regret repeat.
- Every pattern has an indecision phase (shrinking volume) and a resolution phase (expanding volume) marked by a completion trigger.
- Reversal patterns form at the end of trends; continuation patterns form in the middle. The label is a tendency, not a promise.
- Patterns resolve as expected only somewhere between roughly half and three-quarters of the time, and hindsight makes them look cleaner than they are.
- Never trade a pattern before its trigger; the trigger is the pattern.
</KeyTakeaways>
