A sprinter runs hard for fifty metres, then slows to a jog for ten, hands on hips, breathing. They have not stopped and they have not turned around. Anyone watching can tell the jog is a pause, not the end of the race.

That jog is a **flag**. After a sharp, near-vertical move, price stops going anywhere for a handful of candles and drifts slightly against the move in a tight little channel. Then it takes off again in the original direction. A **pennant** is the same idea with a slightly different shape: instead of a small channel, the pause coils into a tiny symmetrical triangle.

Flags and pennants are the cleanest continuation patterns in charting, and they come with the most satisfying target rule in this module: the flagpole.

## The two parts

Every flag and every pennant is made of exactly two pieces.

**The flagpole.** A steep, fast, one-directional move, usually on heavy volume, often driven by news or a breakout. This is the part that does the work. No pole, no pattern.

**The flag (or pennant).** A short consolidation immediately after the pole, drifting gently *against* the pole's direction, on visibly shrinking volume.

That ordering matters. The pole comes first and the pause comes second. A tight little channel that appears out of nowhere in the middle of a range is just a range; it is not a flag, and the flagpole target has nothing to measure.

## The bull flag

<PatternFigure name="bull-flag" />

The rules:

1. A sharp advance of at least a few percent in a small number of candles.
2. A pullback that is **shallow**: two parallel lines sloping gently downward, containing price. As a rough filter, the pullback should retrace less than about half of the pole, and the best ones retrace only a third or less.
3. Duration of roughly 5 to 20 candles. Longer than that and it stops being a pause and becomes a proper consolidation, which is a rectangle or a triangle instead.
4. Volume high on the pole, clearly lower inside the flag, and expanding again on the break.

### Psychology

The pole is a stampede. Some people who missed it want in and are waiting for a discount. Some people who caught it want to take a quick profit.

The flag is those two groups meeting. Profit-takers sell a little, latecomers buy a little, and price drifts sideways to slightly down. Crucially, nobody sells hard, which is why the pullback is shallow. When the profit-taking is finished and there is still a queue of buyers who never got their discount, price breaks out and the queue chases.

<Callout type="eli5">

Picture a bucket brigade passing water up a hill, moving fast. They stop for a moment to swap people around. Nobody goes back down the hill. The instant the swap is done, the water starts moving up again at the same speed. The flag is the swap, and the flagpole is how far the water travelled before it.

</Callout>

## The bear flag

<PatternFigure name="bear-flag" />

The mirror image. A steep drop, then a shallow drift *upward* in a small channel, then a continuation down. The psychology is the same in reverse: short sellers cover a little, bargain hunters nibble, and the bounce is weak because nobody with size wants to buy into a collapse.

Bear flags tend to be shorter-lived and sharper than bull flags, because fear moves faster than greed. A bounce that goes on for twenty candles and retraces 60 percent of the drop is not a bear flag; it is a recovery, and the downtrend may be over.

## The pennant

<PatternFigure name="pennant" />

Same pole, same psychology, but the pause converges instead of channelling: lower highs and higher lows inside a tiny symmetrical triangle. In practice you will often see a shape that is half flag and half pennant, and it does not matter. The trading rules are identical.

The one distinction worth keeping: a pennant is a *small* symmetrical triangle appearing straight after an impulse. A full-sized [symmetrical triangle](/learn/m04-chart-patterns/04-triangles) that takes many weeks to form is a different pattern with a different target measurement.

## Completion trigger

A **close beyond the flag boundary** in the direction of the pole. For a bull flag, a close above the upper line of the little channel. For a bear flag, a close below its lower line.

The close matters as much here as anywhere, but flags bring a specific extra risk: they are small. A flag might be only 1 or 2 percent tall, so the difference between "closed above the line" and "wicked above the line" can be a few cents. On very small flags, dropping to a lower timeframe to see the structure inside the pause is often more useful than agonising over a single candle's close.

## The flagpole target

This is the rule the pattern is famous for. Measure the **height of the flagpole**, from where the impulse started to where it ended, then add that same distance to the breakout point.

Bull flag, worked example:

- Pole: price runs from $20.00 to $26.00, so the pole height is **$6.00**
- The flag drifts down to $24.50 over six candles
- Price closes above the flag's upper line at $25.60
- Target: $25.60 plus $6.00 = **$31.60**

Bear flag, worked example:

- Pole: price drops from $50.00 to $42.00, so the pole height is **$8.00**
- The flag drifts up to $44.50
- Price closes below the flag's lower line at $43.60
- Target: $43.60 minus $8.00 = **$35.60**

There is a second, more conservative convention: project the pole from the *low of the flag* rather than from the breakout. For the bull example that gives $24.50 plus $6.00 = $30.50. Both are in common use. The breakout-based version is more optimistic; the flag-low version is the one to quote if you want a target you are more likely to actually reach. Pick one and be consistent, so your journal numbers mean something.

## Stop placement

**Standard:** just beyond the far side of the flag. For a bull flag, below the lowest low of the pause, say $24.35 in the worked example. If price re-enters the flag and keeps going through it, the pause has become a reversal.

**Tight:** below the low of the breakout candle itself. This risks much less and gets stopped out much more often.

**Never:** below the start of the flagpole. That is a huge stop on a pattern whose whole appeal is a small risk relative to a large target. If the only stop you are comfortable with is the bottom of the pole, the position is too big.

Flags are one of the few patterns where the risk-to-reward maths is genuinely attractive: risk the depth of the flag, target the height of the pole, and the pole is usually several times the flag.

<Callout type="danger" title="A deep flag is not a flag">

The most expensive mistake here is relabelling a real reversal as a flag because you want the trade. If the pullback retraces more than about half the pole, takes longer than the pole took to form, or comes on volume as heavy as the pole, the pattern is broken. You are no longer looking at buyers pausing; you are looking at sellers arriving.

</Callout>

## Honest reliability

Flags and pennants inside a strong, established trend are among the better-performing chart patterns, with continuation in the expected direction commonly reported somewhere in the region of 60 to 70 percent. That still means roughly one in three fails.

The important caveat is that the number collapses when the pattern is taken out of context. A "bull flag" in a choppy market with no real pole behind it performs like a coin flip. The pole is the edge, not the flag.

When a flag fails, it usually fails fast: price breaks out, immediately reverses back through the whole flag, and runs the other way as the chasers get trapped. That is covered in [lesson 10](/learn/m04-chart-patterns/10-pattern-failure).

<KeyTakeaways>
- A flag is a shallow, gently counter-trend channel immediately after a steep impulse; a pennant is the same pause coiled into a tiny symmetrical triangle.
- No flagpole means no pattern; the pole is where the edge comes from and what supplies the target.
- Volume should be heavy on the pole, light inside the pause and expanding on the break.
- The completion trigger is a close beyond the flag boundary in the pole's direction.
- Flagpole target: add the pole height to the breakout point, so a $6 pole broken at $25.60 targets $31.60; the conservative version projects from the flag low instead.
- Stops go just beyond the far side of the flag; a pullback deeper than about half the pole means it is a reversal, not a pause.
</KeyTakeaways>
