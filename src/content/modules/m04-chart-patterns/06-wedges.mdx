A car is running out of petrol on a hill. It is still going up, so from behind it looks fine. But each time the driver presses the accelerator the car gains a little less than last time, and the gaps between the surges get smaller. It is still climbing right up until the moment it rolls backwards.

A **wedge** is that climb. Price keeps making new highs, so the chart still looks bullish, but the highs are gaining less and less ground while the lows keep catching up. Two lines drawn across the highs and the lows both slope upward, and they converge. That is a **rising wedge**, and it is usually bearish.

Wedges are the sneakiest patterns in this module, because they look like continuation and behave like reversal. Learning to spot the difference between a wedge and a healthy trend is one of the more valuable skills in chart reading.

## Wedge versus triangle

The distinction is purely about slope, and it is worth stating precisely because people mix them up constantly.

| Pattern | Upper line | Lower line |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Symmetrical triangle | slopes down | slopes up |
| Ascending triangle | flat | slopes up |
| Descending triangle | slopes down | flat |
| Rising wedge | slopes up | slopes up, more steeply |
| Falling wedge | slopes down, more steeply | slopes down |

In a triangle the lines lean toward each other from opposite sides. In a wedge **both lines lean the same way**, and they converge because one is steeper than the other. That single observation flips the meaning of the pattern, so check the slopes before you name the shape.

## The rising wedge

<PatternFigure name="rising-wedge" />

The rules:

1. Both trendlines slope **upward**.
2. The lower line (support) is **steeper** than the upper line (resistance), so the shape narrows as it rises.
3. At least two touches on each line, ideally three, with real space between them.
4. Volume typically **shrinks** as the wedge progresses.

### Psychology

Price is still making higher highs, so the trend definition from [the trends lesson](/learn/m03-market-structure/01-trends) is technically intact. But look at what is actually happening: each new high adds less than the one before, while buyers are having to step in at ever higher prices just to hold the thing up. The floor is rising faster than the ceiling.

That is a market where demand is being exhausted, not created. The people still buying are buying because price went up, not because anything improved. Volume shrinking while price rises is the tell: fewer and fewer people are willing to fund each new high.

When the steep lower line finally breaks, the support that was rising so quickly disappears, and there is very little structure underneath, because the whole wedge was built on a thin, steep climb. That is why wedge breaks are often fast.

<Callout type="eli5">

Imagine climbing a ladder where each rung is closer to the last one. You are still going up, so it feels like progress, but you are getting less height for the same effort every time. When you finally slip, you fall past all of the rungs you just climbed, because they were all squeezed together.

</Callout>

## The falling wedge

<PatternFigure name="falling-wedge" />

The mirror. Both lines slope **downward**, with the upper line steeper than the lower one, so the shape narrows as it falls. Price is still making lower lows, but each new low costs more effort and gains less ground, while rallies keep bumping into a ceiling that is coming down faster than the floor.

This is selling exhaustion. The sellers are still in charge, but only just, and the volume drying up says the supply is thinning. A close above the upper line is usually bullish.

Falling wedges tend to be somewhat more reliable than rising wedges, partly because a downtrend that runs out of sellers has nowhere to go but up, whereas an uptrend can flatten into a range for months instead of reversing.

## Context decides whether it is a reversal

A wedge means "the current direction is running out of fuel". What that implies depends on where it appears.

- **Rising wedge in an uptrend:** bearish *reversal*. The trend is exhausting itself.
- **Rising wedge in a downtrend:** bearish *continuation*. The rising wedge is the weak bounce, much like a [bear flag](/learn/m04-chart-patterns/05-flags-and-pennants) with converging lines.
- **Falling wedge in a downtrend:** bullish *reversal*.
- **Falling wedge in an uptrend:** bullish *continuation*. The falling wedge is the pullback.

In all four cases the break is expected in the same direction: rising wedges break down, falling wedges break up. Only the label changes.

## Completion trigger

A **close beyond the wedge's supporting line**: below the lower line for a rising wedge, above the upper line for a falling wedge. A wick through the line is not a break, and wedges generate a lot of wicks because their boundaries are steep and easy to touch.

Because the lines are sloping, the trigger price moves every candle. Recalculate it. A common error is to keep using the level where the line sat three days ago, which on a steep wedge can be several percent away from where it sits today.

## Measured-move target

Take the **height of the wedge at its widest point**, which is where the pattern began, and project it from the point where price broke out.

Rising wedge, worked example:

- The wedge starts with a swing high at $60.00 and a swing low at $54.00, so the widest height is **$6.00**
- The wedge narrows as it climbs; price eventually closes below the lower line at $62.00
- Target: $62.00 minus $6.00 = **$56.00**

Falling wedge, worked example:

- The wedge starts with a swing high at $40.00 and a swing low at $34.00, so the height is **$6.00**
- Price closes above the upper line at $36.50
- Target: $36.50 plus $6.00 = **$42.50**

There is a second convention specific to wedges that is worth knowing: because a wedge is a thin, steep structure with little real support inside it, price frequently retraces the **entire wedge**, all the way back to where the pattern started. For the rising wedge example that is $54.00, well beyond the measured move. Traders often use the measured move as a first target and the wedge origin as a second.

## Stop placement

For a short on a rising wedge break: above the **last swing high inside the wedge**. For the worked example, if the final high was $63.40, a stop at about $63.60 is the structural choice. If price makes a new high after breaking down, the exhaustion story is wrong.

The tighter option, as always, is above a failed retest of the broken line.

Avoid placing the stop just above the broken sloping line itself. Sloping lines drift, so a stop pinned to one can be hit by a candle that has not actually invalidated anything.

<Callout type="warning" title="Wedges are the most over-drawn pattern on the internet">

Because both lines slope the same way, you can fit a wedge to almost any trend if you are flexible about which highs and lows to use. That flexibility is exactly what makes the pattern dangerous. Require the lines to touch real swing points, require the convergence to be obvious rather than a couple of degrees, and require the volume to be fading. If you need to ignore two candles and squint, it is a trend, not a wedge, and shorting a healthy uptrend because you drew a wedge on it is an expensive habit.

</Callout>

## Honest reliability

Wedges perform reasonably when they are strict and badly when they are loose. Studies that define them tightly report falling wedges breaking upward somewhere around 65 to 70 percent of the time and rising wedges breaking downward a little less often than that. Loosely drawn wedges perform no better than chance, and there is no way to tell from the statistics whether your wedge is a strict one, so be your own referee.

The most common failure is the **throw-over and recovery**: price spikes above the upper line of a rising wedge, everyone calls a breakout, and it collapses back inside within a candle or two before eventually breaking down properly. Annoyingly, that failed breakout is often the top. The reverse happens with falling wedges. [Lesson 10](/learn/m04-chart-patterns/10-pattern-failure) is about trading that behaviour deliberately rather than being caught by it.

<KeyTakeaways>
- In a wedge both trendlines slope the same way and converge; in a triangle they lean toward each other from opposite directions.
- A rising wedge has a steeper lower line and usually breaks down; a falling wedge has a steeper upper line and usually breaks up.
- Shrinking volume while price keeps making new extremes is the exhaustion tell that gives the pattern its meaning.
- Context sets the label: a rising wedge in an uptrend is a reversal, in a downtrend it is a continuation, but it breaks downward either way.
- Measured-move target is the widest height projected from the break, for example a $6 wedge broken at $62 targets $56, and price often retraces the whole wedge to its origin.
- Wedges are easy to over-draw, so demand real swing touches, obvious convergence and fading volume before trading one.
</KeyTakeaways>
