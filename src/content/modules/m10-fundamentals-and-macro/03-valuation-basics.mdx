Two lemonade stands are for sale on the same street. One costs $45 a share, the other costs $200 a share. Which one is cheaper?

You genuinely cannot tell. A $200 share of a stand that earns $20 per share every year is a far better deal than a $45 share of a stand that earns $1. The price on its own says nothing. What matters is what you get for the price. That is the whole idea of **valuation**: turning a share price into a judgement about whether it is cheap or expensive.

This lesson gives you the four numbers every trader hears daily, EPS, P/E, market cap and growth, and shows exactly how they are calculated. It also shows why the most famous one, P/E, misleads people who use it alone.

## EPS: profit per slice

In the [last lesson](/learn/m10-fundamentals-and-macro/02-earnings-and-financial-statements), Lemonade Inc made $3,000 of net profit and has 1,000 shares. **Earnings per share**, or <Term id="eps">EPS</Term>, is simply profit divided by the number of shares.

<Callout type="math" title="EPS for Lemonade Inc">

Net profit: $3,000

Shares outstanding: 1,000

EPS = $3,000 ÷ 1,000 = **$3.00 per share**

If you own one share, $3 of last year's profit is "yours", whether it was paid out as a dividend or kept in the business to buy juicers.

</Callout>

EPS is the number that makes companies of different sizes comparable. A giant earning $10 billion with 5 billion shares has an EPS of $2. Lemonade Inc, with its $3 EPS, actually earns more per slice.

## P/E: how many years of profit you are paying for

The **price-to-earnings ratio**, or <Term id="pe-ratio">P/E</Term>, divides the share price by EPS. It tells you how many dollars you pay for each dollar of annual profit, or, said another way, how many years of current profit it would take to earn your money back.

<Callout type="math" title="P/E for Lemonade Inc">

Share price: $45

EPS: $3.00

P/E = $45 ÷ $3 = **15**

You are paying 15 years' worth of current profit for the share. If profit never changed, it would take 15 years for the company to earn back what you paid.

</Callout>

Now the two stands from the opening question. Stand A costs $200 with an EPS of $20, so its P/E is 10. Stand B costs $45 with an EPS of $1, so its P/E is 45. Stand A, the "expensive" one by price, is the cheaper one by P/E. This is why traders talk about a share as "trading at 15 times earnings" rather than just quoting the price.

For rough orientation, the broad US market has historically averaged a P/E somewhere in the mid-teens to low twenties, with long stretches well above and below that.

## Market cap: the price of the whole company

<Term id="market-cap">Market capitalisation</Term>, or market cap, is the share price multiplied by the number of shares. It is what it would cost to buy every share at today's price.

Lemonade Inc: $45 × 1,000 shares = **$45,000**. That is the market's current opinion of what the entire stand is worth.

Market cap is how companies get sorted into groups, and the groups behave differently.

| Group | Rough market cap | Typical behaviour |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mega cap | Over $200 billion | Slow, liquid, tight spreads, moves the index |
| Large cap | $10 billion to $200 billion | Liquid, followed by many analysts |
| Mid cap | $2 billion to $10 billion | More volatile, fewer analysts |
| Small cap | $300 million to $2 billion | Volatile, wider spreads, easier to move with news |
| Micro cap | Under $300 million | Very volatile, thin, favourite hunting ground of pump-and-dump schemes |

A beginner is usually better off in large caps, where the [spread](/learn/m00-what-is-a-market/07-bid-ask-spread) is a penny and a single order cannot move the price.

## Growth versus value

Two shares with very different P/E ratios can both be fair. What separates them is expected **growth**.

Imagine Stand C earns $1 per share this year but is opening new locations and expects profit to grow 30% a year. Stand D earns $1 too, but has done so for a decade and expects to earn $1 forever. Would you pay the same for both? Obviously not. In five years, Stand C is on track to earn about $3.70 per share; Stand D is still on $1.

That is why fast-growing companies trade on high P/E ratios. Buyers are paying for profits that do not exist yet. A **growth stock** has a high P/E and a story about the future. A **value stock** has a low P/E and a story about being overlooked. Neither is automatically better; both can be traps.

<Callout type="eli5">

Two kids sell cookies. One sells 10 a day and has for years. The other sells 10 a day too, but she just got permission to sell at the school gate next term. If you had to pay to buy either cookie business, you would pay more for the second one, even though today they earn exactly the same. That extra you would pay is what a high P/E is: a price for tomorrow's cookies.

</Callout>

## Why P/E alone misleads

P/E is useful and also dangerous, because it can be low or high for reasons that have nothing to do with cheap or expensive.

**Low P/E can mean trouble, not a bargain.** If a company earned $5 last year but everyone knows profits are about to collapse, the price falls first. The P/E based on last year's earnings looks tiny. This is called a **value trap**. The cure is to look at **forward P/E**, which uses next year's expected earnings, and to ask *why* it is cheap.

**Negative or tiny earnings break the ratio.** A company that lost money has no meaningful P/E. A company that earned one cent per share and trades at $10 has a P/E of 1,000, which sounds absurd but tells you almost nothing.

**Different industries have different normal ranges.** A utility company grows slowly and trades at maybe 12 to 18 times earnings. A software company might trade at 40 and be considered reasonable. Comparing them by P/E is like comparing a tortoise and a hare by leg length.

**Earnings can be adjusted.** As the last lesson warned, "adjusted EPS" can leave out inconvenient costs. A P/E built on flattering earnings is a flattering P/E.

<Callout type="warning" title="Cheap is not a reason to buy">

Beginners see a share with a P/E of 6 and think they have found a bargain. Usually the market has already looked and found a reason. A low P/E is a question to investigate, not an answer. On the chart, a "cheap" share in a [downtrend](/learn/m03-market-structure/01-trends) is still a downtrend.

</Callout>

## Other ratios you will hear

You do not need these yet, but you should recognise them. **Price-to-sales** divides market cap by revenue and is used for companies with no profit. **Price-to-book** compares price to the balance-sheet equity per share. **Dividend yield** is the annual dividend divided by the price. And **PEG** divides the P/E by the expected growth rate, so a P/E of 30 with 30% growth gives a PEG of 1, which some investors treat as a fair line.

## How a trader actually uses this

For most trading on this site, valuation is context, not a trigger. It helps you understand why a share is behaving as it does: a growth stock on a P/E of 60 will be punished savagely if growth slows, while a value stock on a P/E of 8 might barely move on a bad quarter. Knowing which type you are holding tells you how big the earnings gap could be, and therefore how much [risk](/learn/m07-risk-management/02-the-one-percent-rule) you are really carrying.

<KeyTakeaways>
- EPS is net profit divided by the number of shares; it makes companies of different sizes comparable.
- P/E is price divided by EPS: the number of years of current profit you are paying for.
- Market cap is price times shares, the cost of the whole company, and it sorts shares into groups with different behaviour.
- High P/E usually means the market expects growth; low P/E can mean a bargain or a value trap.
- P/E alone misleads when earnings are negative, adjusted, collapsing or from a different industry.
- For a trader, valuation is context that explains how a share is likely to react to news, not a buy signal.
</KeyTakeaways>
