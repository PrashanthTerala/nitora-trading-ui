Imagine two people trying to guess whether a lemonade stand will be busy tomorrow. The first one never looks at the stand at all. She looks at the queue: how long it was yesterday, whether it is growing or shrinking, and what time of day it peaks. The second person ignores the queue completely. He checks the weather forecast, tastes the lemonade, counts the cost of lemons and asks whether a rival stand is opening across the street.

Both are trying to answer the same question. They are just looking through different lenses. The first person is doing **technical analysis**: studying the behaviour of buyers and sellers, as recorded in price and volume. The second is doing **fundamental analysis**: studying the thing itself, the business and the world it lives in.

Everything you have learned so far on this site, from candles to indicators to market structure, sits in the first lens. This module opens the second one. Not so you can abandon charts, but so you understand the forces that make the candles move in the first place.

## The technical lens

Technical analysis starts from one belief: everything that is known about a company, an economy or a coin is already reflected in its price. If a factory burned down last night, you do not need to read the news, because the sellers who read it have already pushed the price down. The chart is a record of what everybody actually did with their money.

That is why a technician studies [trends](/learn/m03-market-structure/01-trends), [support and resistance](/learn/m03-market-structure/03-support-and-resistance), [volume](/learn/m01-reading-price/06-volume) and patterns. She is reading the footprints of the crowd. She usually does not care *why* the crowd is moving, only that it is, and where it is likely to stop.

The strength of this lens is timing. A chart gives you a precise place to enter, a precise place to admit you are wrong, and a precise target. The weakness is that the chart cannot tell you what the crowd will learn tomorrow.

## The fundamental lens

Fundamental analysis starts from a different belief: every share has a "true" value, and price wanders around that value like a dog on a long lead. Sometimes the dog runs ahead, sometimes it lags behind, but it is tied to the owner. If you can estimate where the owner is walking, you can guess where the dog will eventually end up.

To estimate that value, a fundamental analyst reads the company's <Term id="earnings">earnings</Term> reports, works out how much profit it makes per share, compares that to the price, and studies the bigger world: interest rates, inflation, which industries are growing and which are shrinking. The lessons that follow in this module cover each of these.

The strength of this lens is direction over long periods. Over years, profitable companies tend to be worth more than unprofitable ones. The weakness is timing. A share can stay "cheap" for years, and a "too expensive" share can keep rising long after every spreadsheet says it should stop.

<Callout type="eli5">

Think of a hot air balloon. Technical analysis watches the balloon: is it rising, falling, drifting left? Fundamental analysis studies the burner and the wind: how much gas is left, which way the breeze is blowing. Watching the balloon tells you where it is right now. Studying the burner tells you where it probably has to go eventually. Neither one tells you exactly when.

</Callout>

## Two lenses, two clocks

The most useful way to think about the difference is time horizon. The shorter your trade, the less the fundamentals matter and the more the chart matters. The longer your trade, the reverse.

| Holding period | What moves price most | Lens that matters most |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Seconds to minutes | Order flow, liquidity, noise | Almost entirely technical |
| Hours to days | Momentum, news reactions, key levels | Mostly technical, with awareness of events |
| Weeks to months | Earnings, sector trends, interest rates | Roughly half and half |
| Years | Profit growth, valuation, the economy | Mostly fundamental |

A scalper holding for ninety seconds does not care whether the company is profitable this year. A pension fund holding for twenty years does not care about a bearish engulfing candle on the five-minute chart. Both are correct for their own clock.

## Why most traders end up using both

Pure technicians and pure fundamentalists exist, but most experienced traders quietly borrow from the other camp. Here is how the blend usually looks in practice.

**Fundamentals choose what to trade; technicals choose when.** A swing trader might only trade companies that just reported strong earnings, because those shares tend to keep drifting in the same direction for weeks. But she still waits for a [pullback](/learn/m09-strategies/04-pullback-trading) to a moving average before entering, because that gives her a stop and a target.

**Technicals show the move; fundamentals explain it.** When a share gaps up 15% on a Tuesday morning, the chart shows the gap. The news explains that earnings beat expectations. Knowing the reason helps you judge whether the move is likely to last or to fade.

**Fundamentals set the calendar; technicals fill in the days.** Even a day trader who never reads a balance sheet needs to know when the central bank speaks or when a company reports, because those are the moments when charts stop behaving. You will learn that calendar in [the economic calendar](/learn/m10-fundamentals-and-macro/05-economic-calendar).

<Callout type="warning" title="The trap of mixing lenses mid-trade">

The danger is not using both lenses. It is switching lenses to avoid admitting a mistake. You buy on a chart pattern, price hits your stop, and suddenly you are "holding for the fundamentals". That is not analysis; that is a loss you refuse to take. Decide which lens sets your exit *before* you enter, and stay with it.

</Callout>

<KeyTakeaways>
- Technical analysis studies what buyers and sellers have done, as recorded in price and volume; fundamental analysis studies the business and the economy behind the price.
- Technicals are strongest for timing; fundamentals are strongest for long-run direction.
- The shorter your holding period, the more the chart matters; the longer, the more the fundamentals matter.
- Most traders blend them: fundamentals choose what and why, technicals choose when and where.
- Decide before you enter which lens sets your exit, and never switch lenses to avoid taking a loss.
