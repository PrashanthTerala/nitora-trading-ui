A ball is rolling slowly across a table. You can predict where it will be in a second, because nothing is disturbing it. Now someone flicks it. In that instant, all your predictions are useless; the ball could go anywhere, fast.

News is the flick. Most of the time a share drifts along its trend, respecting its levels, doing roughly what the chart suggests. Then a **catalyst** arrives: an earnings report, a guidance change, an analyst upgrade, a regulator's decision, a takeover bid. In the space of one candle, the old chart stops mattering and a new one begins.

This lesson explains the common catalysts, why earnings are the most dangerous of them for a beginner, and the honest maths behind the phrase "holding through earnings is a coin flip".

## The catalysts you will meet

| Catalyst | What it is | Typical size of the move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Earnings report | Quarterly results plus guidance, released outside market hours | 5% to 20% gap in either direction; more in small caps |
| Guidance change | Company raises or cuts its forecast, sometimes between reports | Similar to earnings; a cut is usually punished harder than a raise is rewarded |
| Analyst upgrade or downgrade | A bank changes its rating or price target | 1% to 5%, usually fades within days unless it starts a trend |
| Regulatory or legal news | Drug approval, lawsuit, fine, licence | From tiny to company-changing; biotech approvals can be 50% or more |
| Takeover announcement | Another company offers to buy this one | Target jumps toward the offer price; the buyer often falls |
| Index inclusion | Share added to a major index | Steady buying from index funds ahead of the date |
| Macro release | CPI, FOMC and friends from the [calendar lesson](/learn/m10-fundamentals-and-macro/05-economic-calendar) | Moves the whole market at once |

Notice the pattern: the bigger the surprise, the bigger the move. A scheduled event with a well-known consensus, like earnings, moves less on average than an unscheduled bolt from the blue, but earnings happen four times a year to every share you will ever hold, so they are the catalyst that matters most.

## The earnings gap

You learned in the [gaps lesson](/learn/m01-reading-price/07-gaps) that price can open far from the previous close. Earnings are the main reason. The report lands at 4:05 pm or 7:00 am, the after-hours market reacts, and by the regular open the entire repricing has already happened. There is no candle where you could have exited at a sensible price; there is the close before and the open after.

Three parts of the report drive the gap. The **headline numbers**, revenue and EPS versus consensus. The **guidance**, which is often the bigger driver because it is about the future. And the **tone of the call**, the hour-long conversation with analysts where a nervous answer can undo a good quarter.

<Callout type="eli5">

Imagine a school report day. Every child already knows roughly what mark they expect. The child who expected a C and gets a B runs home happy. The child who expected an A and gets a B goes home in tears. Same B. What matters is the mark compared to the expectation, and whether the teacher's note at the bottom says the next term looks harder.

</Callout>

## The honest maths of holding through earnings

Here is the question every beginner asks: should I hold my position through the report? Let us look at what you are actually buying when you do.

Suppose a share typically moves about 8% on earnings, up or down, with roughly equal odds. You hold a long position with a stop 3% below your entry. What are the outcomes?

<Callout type="math" title="Two scenarios, one coin">

**The share gaps up 8%.** You wake up to a 8% gain. Your stop was never needed. Delightful.

**The share gaps down 8%.** Your stop at minus 3% is skipped entirely, because the first traded price is already 8% below the close. You are filled somewhere near minus 8%, nearly three times the loss you planned. Your 1R risk became a 2.7R loss.

Across many such trades, the average result might be roughly zero *before* costs. But the shape of the outcomes is what matters: the wins are capped by what you can reasonably expect, and the losses are not capped by your stop.

</Callout>

This is why traders call it a coin flip with a big payout distribution. The direction is close to 50/50 for most shares, because the consensus already prices in what is known. The size of the move is large. And your risk controls, the stop loss you worked so hard to size correctly in the [risk module](/learn/m07-risk-management/03-stop-loss-placement), do not function across a gap.

It is not that holding through earnings is always wrong. Some strategies are built around it. It is that if you hold, you are no longer trading your chart setup. You are betting on a report, with a position size that was calculated for a different, smaller risk.

<Callout type="danger" title="If you hold through earnings, size for the gap, not for your stop">

If you decide to hold, recompute the position as if the stop were at the typical earnings move, not at your chart stop. A share that moves 10% on earnings, with a 1% account risk rule, means a position no larger than one tenth of your account. Most beginners hold their normal size and are shocked when a single night costs them a week of gains.

</Callout>

## Trading the reaction instead

The safer way to trade earnings is to wait for the gap and then trade what happens next, using everything you learned in [momentum and catalyst trading](/learn/m09-strategies/07-momentum-and-news). Two patterns recur.

**Gap and go.** The share gaps up on a genuine beat with raised guidance, holds above the opening range for the first 15 to 30 minutes, and continues. This is a momentum trade with a defined stop below the opening range. It works best when the whole [sector](/learn/m10-fundamentals-and-macro/06-sectors-and-rotation) is strong too.

**Gap and fade.** The share gaps up on a mediocre report, fails to hold the open, and fills the gap. This is a mean-reversion trade, and it is common when the gap was driven by relief rather than genuine strength.

Neither pattern works every time. What they have in common is that you are trading with a stop that can actually be honoured, because the market is open and liquid.

## Halts and other surprises

Exchanges can **halt** trading in a share when big news is pending or when price moves too fast. During a halt you cannot exit, and the share can reopen far from where it stopped. Small caps and biotech shares are halted most often. If you are holding a share and see "halted" on your screen, there is nothing to do but wait; the lesson is to size small in names where halts are common.

Upgrades and downgrades deserve a word too. An analyst changing a rating produces a pop or drop at the open, but the analyst is one person with one opinion. Unless the change coincides with a real shift in the business, these moves often fade within a few sessions. Chasing an upgrade at the open is one of the most reliable ways to buy the top of a two-day move.

<Callout type="tip" title="Check the date before every overnight hold">

Before holding any share past the close, look up its next earnings date. It takes ten seconds. If the report is inside your expected holding period, either close before it or size for the gap. Add the date to your journal entry so the review later shows whether event holds helped or hurt you.

</Callout>

<KeyTakeaways>
- A catalyst is news that reprices a share in one candle: earnings, guidance, upgrades, regulatory decisions, takeovers and macro releases.
- Earnings are released outside market hours, so the reaction is a gap and stops cannot be honoured across it.
- Holding through earnings is close to a coin flip on direction with a large, uncapped loss side, because the gap skips your stop.
- If you hold through a report, size the position as if the stop were at the typical earnings move.
- Trading the reaction after the gap, with a stop that can actually fill, is the safer approach for a beginner.
- Halts, upgrades and downgrades produce sharp moves that often fade; check the earnings date before every overnight hold.
</KeyTakeaways>
