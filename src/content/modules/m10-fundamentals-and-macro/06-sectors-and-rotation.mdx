Picture a playground at break time. When it is sunny, the crowd is on the field. When it starts to rain, everybody runs for the covered area. The number of children never changes; they just move from one place to another depending on the weather.

Money in the stock market behaves the same way. It does not vanish when a group of shares falls out of favour; it runs somewhere else. Watching *where* it runs is one of the most useful habits a trader can build, because a share in a sector that money is flowing into has a tailwind, and a perfect chart pattern in a sector that money is leaving is fighting the wind.

This lesson introduces the sector map, the difference between defensive and cyclical groups, and the idea of **rotation**.

## The eleven sectors

The most common classification is called **GICS** (the Global Industry Classification Standard). It sorts every listed company into one of eleven **sectors**, each of which is broken into industries. Each sector has an exchange-traded fund that tracks it, so you can chart a sector as easily as a share.

| Sector | What is in it | Typical examples | Common US ETF |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Information Technology | Software, chips, hardware | Microsoft, Nvidia, Apple | XLK |
| Health Care | Pharma, devices, insurers | Johnson and Johnson, UnitedHealth | XLV |
| Financials | Banks, insurers, exchanges | JPMorgan, Visa | XLF |
| Consumer Discretionary | Things people buy when they feel rich | Amazon, Tesla, Nike | XLY |
| Consumer Staples | Things people buy regardless | Procter and Gamble, Coca-Cola, Walmart | XLP |
| Communication Services | Media, telecoms, social platforms | Alphabet, Meta, Netflix | XLC |
| Industrials | Machinery, airlines, defence, logistics | Caterpillar, Boeing, UPS | XLI |
| Energy | Oil, gas, pipelines | ExxonMobil, Chevron | XLE |
| Materials | Chemicals, metals, mining | Linde, Freeport-McMoRan | XLB |
| Utilities | Electricity, water, gas supply | NextEra, Duke Energy | XLU |
| Real Estate | Property owners and trusts | Prologis, American Tower | XLRE |

You do not need to memorise the list. You need to know it exists, and to be able to answer one question about any share you trade: which sector is it in, and what is that sector doing?

## Defensive versus cyclical

The eleven sectors split into two broad families, based on how much their profits swing with the economy.

**Cyclical** sectors do well when the economy is growing and badly when it slows. People buy new cars, book holidays and build factories in good times, and stop in bad times. Consumer Discretionary, Industrials, Financials, Materials, Energy and Technology mostly live here.

**Defensive** sectors sell things people need whatever the weather. You still buy toothpaste, pay the electricity bill and fill your prescription in a recession. Consumer Staples, Utilities and Health Care are the classic defensives. Their profits are steadier, so their shares tend to fall less in bad times and rise less in good times.

<Callout type="eli5">

Imagine two lemonade stands. One is outside the football stadium and only sells on match days: huge sales when there is a game, nothing when there is not. The other is inside the hospital and sells about the same every single day. The stadium stand is cyclical; the hospital stand is defensive. When the football season is cancelled, the stadium stand suffers and the hospital stand barely notices.

</Callout>

## What rotation looks like

**Sector rotation** is the pattern of money moving from one family to another as the economic mood changes. A simplified version of the cycle looks like this:

| Phase of the economy | Where money tends to flow | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Early recovery | Financials, Consumer Discretionary, Technology | Rates are low and growth is picking up |
| Mid expansion | Technology, Industrials | Profits growing fastest |
| Late expansion | Energy, Materials | Inflation and commodity prices rising |
| Slowdown | Utilities, Staples, Health Care | Investors want steady profits and dividends |

Treat this as a tendency, not a timetable. Real cycles are messy, phases overlap, and a single shock such as a pandemic or a war can scramble the order. But the underlying logic, that money seeks growth when it is confident and safety when it is scared, holds up well.

You can see rotation on a chart without any economics at all. Put XLK and XLU on the same screen. When technology is rising and utilities are flat or falling, the market is in a risk-on mood. When the two swap, the mood has changed, and it usually changes before the headlines explain why.

## Relative strength: the practical tool

The simplest way to measure where money is flowing is **relative strength**: compare each sector's performance to the overall index over a window such as one month or three months. Sectors beating the index are attracting money; sectors lagging are losing it.

Many charting platforms let you plot the ratio of one instrument to another, for example XLE divided by SPY. When that ratio line is rising, energy is outperforming the market, whatever the absolute prices are doing. A rising ratio with a rising sector is a strong tailwind. A falling ratio, even if the sector is up on the day, tells you the money is actually going elsewhere.

<Callout type="tip" title="A three-step sector check before any swing trade">

1. Which sector is the share in?
2. Is that sector above or below the index over the last month?
3. Are most shares in that sector making higher highs, or just this one?

A [breakout](/learn/m09-strategies/05-breakout-trading) in a share whose whole sector is breaking out has far better odds than a lonely breakout in a sector that is bleeding.

</Callout>

## Why this matters for a technical trader

You may wonder why a chart trader should care. Two reasons.

First, [correlation](/learn/m07-risk-management/08-correlation-and-diversification). Five long positions in five technology stocks are, in practice, one big bet on the technology sector. Knowing the sector map stops you accidentally stacking the same risk five times.

Second, context. In the [multi-timeframe lesson](/learn/m03-market-structure/10-multi-timeframe) you learned to zoom out to find the trend. The sector is another layer of zoom. A daily uptrend in a share sitting inside a weekly uptrend in its sector, inside a rising index, is trend agreement at three levels. That alignment is where the easiest trades live.

<Callout type="warning" title="Rotation is not a signal to chase">

Seeing that money flowed into energy last month does not mean it will flow in next month. Rotation is slow to start and can reverse without warning. Use it to filter and to size, not as an entry trigger on its own. The entry still comes from the chart.

</Callout>

<KeyTakeaways>
- GICS sorts every company into eleven sectors, each with a tradeable ETF, so you can chart sectors like shares.
- Cyclical sectors rise and fall with the economy; defensive sectors sell necessities and swing less.
- Rotation is money moving between sectors as the economic mood shifts, from growth-seeking to safety-seeking and back.
- Relative strength, a sector's performance versus the index, shows where money is flowing right now.
- Check the sector before any swing trade: a breakout with the whole sector behind it has far better odds.
- Knowing the sector map prevents accidental correlation and adds a layer of trend agreement to your analysis.
</KeyTakeaways>
