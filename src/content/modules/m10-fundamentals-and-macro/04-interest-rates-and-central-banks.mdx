Here is a strange fact. Eight times a year, a small group of people sits around a table in Washington, and when they stand up, share prices, gold, oil, the dollar and even Bitcoin can all move at once. Nobody bought or sold anything on that table. They announced a number.

That number is the **interest rate**, and the group is the **Federal Reserve**, the central bank of the United States. Every major economy has one: the European Central Bank, the Bank of England, the Bank of Japan, the Reserve Bank of India and so on. Their decisions ripple through every chart you will ever look at, so this lesson explains why.

The good news is that the core idea fits in one sentence. **Higher interest rates make money today worth more and money tomorrow worth less.** Everything else follows from that.

## What an interest rate is

An interest rate is the price of borrowing money, or, seen from the other side, the reward for waiting. If the rate is 5%, lending $100 for a year gets you $105 back. Borrowing $100 costs you $5.

A central bank does not set every rate in the economy, but it sets the one at the very bottom, the rate at which banks lend to each other overnight. Every other rate, from mortgages to car loans to what your broker charges on margin, is built on top of that base. Raise the base and everything above it rises too.

## Why the central bank moves rates

Central banks are usually given two jobs: keep prices stable (**inflation** low and steady, often targeted around 2% a year) and keep people employed. Interest rates are the main tool for both.

When the economy is running hot and prices are climbing too fast, the bank **raises** rates. Borrowing gets expensive, people and businesses spend less, and inflation cools. When the economy is weak and people are losing jobs, the bank **cuts** rates. Borrowing gets cheap, spending picks up, and hiring recovers.

<Callout type="eli5">

Think of the economy as a bath. Inflation is the water rising too fast; a recession is the bath running dry. The central bank controls one tap. Turning it toward "expensive money" slows the water. Turning it toward "cheap money" speeds it up. The trouble is that the tap works with a long delay, so the bank is always adjusting for water that has not arrived yet.

</Callout>

## Why higher rates make shares worth less

This is the part traders need to feel in their bones, so here is a worked example.

<Callout type="math" title="The discount-rate intuition">

Lemonade Inc promises you $100 of profit exactly one year from now. How much is that promise worth today?

If safe interest rates are 1%, you would need to put aside $99.01 today to have $100 in a year. So the promise is worth about **$99**.

If safe rates are 5%, you only need $95.24 today to reach $100 in a year. The promise is now worth about **$95**.

If safe rates are 10%, you need $90.91. The promise is worth about **$91**.

Same $100, same company, same profit. Higher rates made the *future* dollar worth less *today*.

</Callout>

A share is nothing but a bundle of promised future profits. When rates rise, every one of those future dollars gets discounted more heavily, so the whole bundle is worth less. This hits growth stocks hardest, because most of their value sits far in the future. A company earning big profits today loses a little; a company promising big profits in 2035 loses a lot.

Now stretch the example to ten years of profits instead of one, and you see why a single rate rise can knock 5% or 10% off an index in a week.

## Bonds compete with shares

There is a second channel. A **bond** is a loan to a government or company that pays a fixed interest rate, called the **yield**. Government bonds from stable countries are about as safe as money gets.

Ask yourself: if a safe government bond pays 1%, and a share might return 7% with a lot of risk, the share looks attractive. But if the bond now pays 5%, the share is offering only a couple of extra percent for all that risk. Some money leaves shares and buys bonds. Prices of shares fall until they look attractive again.

Traders watch the **10-year Treasury yield** for this reason. When it climbs, it is a headwind for shares; when it falls, it is a tailwind. Watch the yield and the share index on the same screen over a few months and you will often see them pulling against each other.

## What else moves when rates move

| Rates go up | Typical effect | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Growth stocks | Fall the most | Far-future profits discounted heavily |
| Value and dividend stocks | Fall less, sometimes rise | Profits are nearer, and banks earn more on loans |
| Bonds already issued | Fall in price | New bonds pay more, so old ones are worth less |
| The currency | Usually strengthens | Foreign money moves in to earn the higher rate |
| Gold | Often falls | Gold pays no interest, so holding it costs more |
| Property | Cools | Mortgages get more expensive |

Flip every row for a rate cut. None of these is a law; they are tendencies with plenty of exceptions, especially over short periods.

## Hikes, cuts, pauses and "the dots"

Central banks announce decisions on a published schedule. The Fed's committee is called the **FOMC**, and its meeting days are among the most volatile of the year; you will meet them properly in the [economic calendar lesson](/learn/m10-fundamentals-and-macro/05-economic-calendar). Three things move markets on those days.

**The decision itself**: raise, cut or hold. Usually well telegraphed, so the surprise is small.

**The statement and press conference**: the tone. Is the bank worried about inflation (**hawkish**, leaning toward higher rates) or about growth (**dovish**, leaning toward cuts)? One changed word in a statement can move an index 1%.

**The projections**: the Fed publishes a "dot plot" of where each member expects rates to be. Markets trade the gap between those dots and what was already priced in.

<Callout type="warning" title="The market moves on the surprise, not the decision">

If everybody expects a 0.25% cut and the bank cuts 0.25%, shares may barely move, or even fall, because the good news was already in the price. The reaction comes from the difference between what happened and what was expected. Trying to guess "rates go down so stocks go up" on the day is how beginners get whipsawed in both directions inside ten minutes.

</Callout>

## What this means for your trading

You do not need to forecast rates. You need three habits.

First, know when the central bank meets, and be flat or small around it unless event trading is your explicit plan. Second, know which way the wind is blowing: in a rate-hiking cycle, breakouts in growth stocks fail more often, and in a cutting cycle, dips get bought more readily. Third, when the whole market gaps on a rate day, do not read it as a signal about your individual stock. It is the tide, not the boat.

<KeyTakeaways>
- A central bank sets the base interest rate, and every other rate in the economy is built on top of it.
- Rates are raised to cool inflation and cut to support growth and jobs.
- Higher rates make future profits worth less today, so share prices fall, with growth stocks hit hardest.
- Bond yields compete with shares: when safe yields rise, money leaves shares until they look attractive again.
- Markets move on the surprise versus expectations, not on the decision itself, and the tone of the statement often matters more than the number.
- Be flat or small around central bank meetings unless event trading is your explicit plan.
</KeyTakeaways>
