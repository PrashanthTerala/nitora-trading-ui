Suppose your friend Maya runs a lemonade stand and asks you to buy a piece of it. Before you hand over money, you would want answers to three questions. How much did the stand make last year? What does it own, and what does it owe? And where did the actual cash go?

Those three questions are exactly what a company's **financial statements** answer. They are called the income statement, the balance sheet and the cash flow statement. Every listed company publishes them every quarter, and the publication is the event traders call <Term id="earnings">earnings</Term>.

This lesson walks through all three using a made-up company called Lemonade Inc. The numbers are tiny so you can do the sums in your head. Real companies have more zeros and more line items, but the shape is identical.

## Lemonade Inc: the setup

Lemonade Inc has 1,000 shares, and its shares trade at $45 each. Maya owns 600 of them, her uncle owns 200, and the other 200 are held by people like you. Keep those numbers in mind, because the next lesson on [valuation](/learn/m10-fundamentals-and-macro/03-valuation-basics) will use them.

## The income statement: did it make money?

The **income statement** (sometimes called the profit and loss statement) covers a period of time, usually a quarter or a year. It starts with everything the company sold, subtracts everything it cost to do that, and ends with what is left.

| Lemonade Inc, year ended 31 December | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Revenue (sales of lemonade) | $10,000 |
| Cost of goods sold (lemons, sugar, cups) | $3,000 |
| **Gross profit** | **$7,000** |
| Wages | $2,000 |
| Stand rent | $1,000 |
| **Operating profit** | **$4,000** |
| Tax | $1,000 |
| **Net profit** | **$3,000** |

Three words from that table will follow you everywhere. **Revenue** is the top line: money in from customers, before any costs. **Net profit** (also called net income or the bottom line) is what remains after every cost, including tax. And **margin** is profit divided by revenue: Lemonade Inc keeps $3,000 of every $10,000, a net margin of 30%.

When traders say a company "beat on revenue but missed on earnings", they mean sales were higher than analysts expected but profit was lower, usually because costs rose. Both numbers matter, and the market reacts to the gap between what was expected and what was delivered.

<Callout type="eli5">

The income statement is like emptying your pockets at the end of a school fair. You count all the coins you took in, then hand back what you owe for the lemons and the table hire. Whatever is still in your hand is profit. Big pile of coins in, big pile out, small pile left: that is a low-margin business.

</Callout>

## The balance sheet: what it owns and owes

The **balance sheet** is a photograph taken on one day, usually the last day of the quarter. It lists everything the company owns (**assets**), everything it owes (**liabilities**), and the difference, which belongs to the shareholders (**equity**).

| Lemonade Inc, at 31 December | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Cash in the tin | $2,000 |
| Lemons and sugar in stock (inventory) | $500 |
| Stand, juicer and cooler (equipment) | $5,000 |
| **Total assets** | **$7,500** |
| Loan from Maya's uncle | $2,500 |
| **Total liabilities** | **$2,500** |
| **Shareholders' equity** | **$5,000** |

It is called a balance sheet because it always balances: assets equal liabilities plus equity. $7,500 equals $2,500 plus $5,000. If that ever fails, someone made an arithmetic mistake.

Traders scan the balance sheet for two things. First, **debt**: how big is the loan compared to the equity? Lemonade Inc owes $2,500 against $5,000 of equity, which is comfortable. A company owing ten times its equity is fragile; one bad year and the lenders own it. Second, **cash**: can the company survive a slow season without borrowing more?

## The cash flow statement: where did the money actually go?

Here is the puzzle that trips up beginners. Lemonade Inc made $3,000 of profit. So the cash tin should have grown by $3,000, right?

Look again: the tin holds $2,000, the same as last year. The **cash flow statement** explains why. It splits cash movements into three buckets.

| Lemonade Inc cash flows, year ended 31 December | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Net profit | $3,000 |
| Minus sales made on IOUs that customers have not paid yet | -$1,000 |
| **Cash from operations** | **$2,000** |
| Bought a new juicer | -$1,500 |
| **Cash from investing** | **-$1,500** |
| Repaid part of the uncle's loan | -$500 |
| **Cash from financing** | **-$500** |
| **Net change in cash** | **$0** |

Profit is an accountant's opinion; cash is a fact. Lemonade Inc booked $10,000 of sales, but $1,000 of that is IOUs from the school football team. Then it spent $1,500 on a juicer and $500 paying down debt. Profitable year, no extra cash. Neither of those decisions is bad, but you can only see them here.

<Callout type="math" title="Free cash flow">

A number many investors prefer to profit is **free cash flow**: cash from operations minus the money spent on equipment.

Lemonade Inc: $2,000 minus $1,500 equals **$500** of free cash flow.

That is the cash the business genuinely generated after keeping itself running. It is much harder to dress up than profit, which is why some people say cash flow is the "honest" statement.

</Callout>

## What earnings season looks like from a chart

Four times a year, listed companies publish these three statements along with a short commentary. Most also give **guidance**: what they expect revenue and profit to be next quarter. Analysts at banks publish their own forecasts beforehand, and the average of those forecasts is the **consensus estimate**.

The share price reaction on earnings day is driven less by whether the numbers were good and more by whether they were *better or worse than the consensus*. A company can report record profit and fall 10% because the market expected even more. Another can report a loss and rise 10% because the loss was smaller than feared.

Reports are almost always published outside market hours, before the open or after the close. That is why earnings produce [gaps](/learn/m01-reading-price/07-gaps): the whole reaction is compressed into the first print of the next session. Lesson eight of this module deals with trading those gaps.

<Callout type="warning" title="Reported profit can be dressed up">

Companies have some freedom in how they present profit. "Adjusted" earnings often exclude costs the company would rather you ignored, and one-off gains from selling a building can make a bad year look fine. When a headline profit number looks too good, read the cash flow statement. If cash from operations is falling while profit is rising, something is being stretched.

</Callout>

## Reading a real report in five minutes

You do not need to read forty pages. For a quick trader's view, check these in order:

1. **Revenue growth** versus last year, and versus consensus.
2. **Net profit and margin**, and whether the margin is widening or shrinking.
3. **Guidance** for next quarter: raised, kept, or cut?
4. **Cash from operations** compared to net profit. They should be roughly in step.
5. **Debt** compared to equity, and whether it is growing.

Five numbers, one pattern. Growing revenue, steady or widening margins, raised guidance, cash matching profit and manageable debt is the picture of a healthy business. Miss on two or more, and the chart is likely to show it.

<KeyTakeaways>
- The income statement covers a period and shows revenue, costs and net profit; margin is profit as a share of revenue.
- The balance sheet is a one-day snapshot of assets, liabilities and shareholders' equity, and it always balances.
- The cash flow statement shows where cash actually went; profit is an opinion, cash is a fact.
- Free cash flow is cash from operations minus spending on equipment, and it is harder to dress up than profit.
- Earnings-day moves are driven by results versus consensus expectations and by guidance, not by whether the numbers were good in isolation.
- Reports land outside market hours, so the reaction appears as a gap at the next open.
</KeyTakeaways>
