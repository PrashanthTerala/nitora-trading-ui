Every school has a timetable, and every pupil knows which lessons are the scary ones. Nobody is surprised when the maths test lands on Thursday at nine; it was on the wall all term. The smart pupils prepare for it, or at least do not schedule a dentist appointment across it.

Markets have a timetable too. It is called the **economic calendar**, and it lists the exact date and time when governments and central banks release the numbers that describe the economy. Those releases produce the biggest, fastest candles of the month. Traders who know the timetable prepare for them. Traders who do not get run over by a candle they never saw coming.

This lesson introduces the five releases that matter most, explains what "beat" and "miss" mean, and gives you a simple rule for surviving release days as a beginner.

## Why a number on a screen moves the market

Recall from the [interest rates lesson](/learn/m10-fundamentals-and-macro/04-interest-rates-and-central-banks) that the central bank sets rates based on inflation and employment. Economic releases are the raw material for that decision. A hot inflation number makes rate hikes more likely; a weak jobs number makes cuts more likely. So every release is really a message about future interest rates, and rates move everything.

The second reason is positioning. Thousands of funds hold positions based on what they *expect* the number to be. When the actual number lands, everyone who guessed wrong adjusts at the same second. That stampede is what you see as a 30-second candle bigger than the previous three hours combined.

## The big five

| Release | What it measures | Typical timing (US) | Why traders care |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <Term id="cpi">CPI</Term> (Consumer Price Index) | Inflation: how much a basket of everyday goods costs versus a year ago | Monthly, around the middle of the month, 8:30 am Eastern | Hot inflation means higher rates; the single most-watched number in a rate-hiking cycle |
| <Term id="nfp">NFP</Term> (Non-Farm Payrolls) | Jobs added or lost last month, plus the unemployment rate | Usually the first Friday of the month, 8:30 am Eastern | Strong jobs mean a strong economy but also less pressure to cut rates |
| GDP (Gross Domestic Product) | Total output of the economy, reported as a growth rate | Quarterly, with revisions | Confirms whether the economy is growing or shrinking; often less of a surprise |
| <Term id="fomc">FOMC</Term> decision | The Fed's interest rate decision, statement and press conference | Eight times a year, 2:00 pm Eastern statement, 2:30 pm press conference | The rate itself, plus the tone about future moves |
| PMI (Purchasing Managers' Index) | Survey of businesses: above 50 means expansion, below 50 means contraction | Monthly, first business days, 10:00 am Eastern for the ISM version | An early read on the economy before harder data arrives |

Other countries publish their own versions on their own schedules, and forex traders in particular watch the release from each side of the pair they trade. A good calendar shows each event with a rating of how much it usually moves the market, often as one, two or three stars or a colour.

<Callout type="eli5">

The economy is a giant, and the giant is asleep most of the time. Economic releases are the moments the doctor reads out its temperature, its pulse and its weight. Everybody in the room has already bet on what the doctor will say. When the doctor speaks, the losers scramble to change their bets, and that scramble is the big candle.

</Callout>

## Consensus, beat and miss

Before every release, economists publish forecasts. The average forecast is the **consensus**, and the calendar shows it next to the previous reading. When the number arrives:

- A **beat** means the actual number came in stronger than consensus.
- A **miss** means it came in weaker.
- **In line** means it matched, and the reaction is often small or reverses quickly.

Here is the twist that confuses beginners: "stronger" is not always "good for shares". In a period when the market is desperate for rate cuts, a *strong* jobs number can be *bad* for stocks, because it makes cuts less likely. In a period when the market fears recession, the same strong number is *good* news. The meaning of a beat depends on what the market is worried about this month.

<Callout type="math" title="Reading a CPI print">

Consensus: inflation of 3.1% year on year.

Previous month: 3.2%.

Actual: 3.4%.

That is a **hot** print, 0.3 percentage points above consensus. In a hiking cycle, expect bond yields up, the dollar up, growth stocks down, all within the first minute. If the actual had been 2.9%, expect roughly the mirror image.

</Callout>

## What the candle looks like

In the seconds around a major release, three things happen at once. The <Term id="spread">spread</Term> widens, sometimes to many times its normal width, because market makers pull their quotes. Price jumps in both directions as early orders hit a thin book. Then the crowd picks a direction and the candle extends.

That first move is often *not* the final move. It is common for price to spike one way, reverse fully, and only then trend for the rest of the session. Stops placed at "sensible" levels get hit in both directions in the first two minutes. This is not the market being unfair; it is what a thin order book looks like when everyone arrives at once, as you saw in the lesson on [slippage and liquidity](/learn/m06-orders-and-execution/07-slippage-and-liquidity).

<Callout type="danger" title="The beginner rule for release days">

Do not hold a position, and do not enter one, in the fifteen minutes before and after a top-tier release. Your stop can be skipped by a gap, the spread can eat your risk budget on its own, and the first move is frequently a trap. Let the dust settle, then trade the structure that emerges. You will miss some spectacular moves. You will also miss the spectacular losses, which is the point.

</Callout>

## How to use the calendar every week

Make this part of your routine, alongside the [checklists](/learn/m08-trading-psychology/07-routines-and-discipline) you built earlier.

1. **Sunday or Monday:** open a calendar and note every top-tier release for the week, with times converted to your time zone.
2. **Each morning:** check what lands today. If a major release is due at 8:30, plan to have no open positions at 8:15 unless your plan explicitly covers events.
3. **After the release:** wait for the first fifteen minutes to complete. Look at where price settled relative to the pre-release range. A clean hold above the range after a beat is a very different picture from a spike and full reversal.
4. **In your journal:** tag trades taken on release days, so you can later check whether they help or hurt your results. Most beginners find they hurt.

## Earnings are a calendar too

Every listed company has its own release day, and the reaction on the chart is often larger than any macro number for that one share. You saw the mechanics in the [earnings lesson](/learn/m10-fundamentals-and-macro/02-earnings-and-financial-statements), and lesson eight of this module deals with trading around them. For now the rule is the same: know the date before you hold a share overnight.

<KeyTakeaways>
- The economic calendar lists scheduled data releases; the biggest are CPI, NFP, GDP, FOMC decisions and PMI surveys.
- Releases move markets because they change expectations for interest rates and because thousands of positions adjust at the same second.
- A beat means stronger than consensus and a miss means weaker, but whether that is good or bad for shares depends on what the market fears that month.
- Around a release the spread widens, price whipsaws and the first move is often reversed.
- As a beginner, be flat for fifteen minutes either side of a top-tier release and trade the structure that forms afterwards.
- Check the calendar every week and every morning, and tag release-day trades in your journal.
</KeyTakeaways>
