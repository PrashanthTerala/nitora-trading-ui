Walk into a covered fruit market on a Saturday morning. It looks like chaos, but nothing there is random. The building belongs to somebody and has rules about who may set up a stall. There are a dozen stallholders shouting prices for the same oranges. There are shoppers with baskets. There are men with vans buying pallets at a time for restaurants. There is a fellow who buys from one stall and sells at another, pocketing the difference. And somewhere in an office upstairs, an inspector checks the scales.

A financial market has exactly the same cast. When you click "buy" on a screen it feels like you are interacting with a machine, and that hides how many different kinds of people and firms are standing between your click and your shares.

This lesson introduces all of them, and then answers the question every beginner eventually asks: **when I buy, who on earth is selling to me?**

## The exchange: the building and the rulebook

The **exchange** is the covered market itself. It does not buy or sell anything. It provides the venue, the rules and the matching machinery that pairs a buyer with a seller.

Names you will recognise are exchanges: the New York Stock Exchange, Nasdaq, the London Stock Exchange, the Tokyo Stock Exchange. Each maintains a list of companies allowed to trade there, publishes prices, decides opening hours, and halts trading when something goes badly wrong.

At the centre of an <Term id="exchange">exchange</Term> sits a piece of software called the matching engine. It holds every resting buy order and sell order for each instrument, sorted by price, and the moment a buy order and a sell order overlap it matches them and prints a trade. That print is the price you see on your screen, usually within a few thousandths of a second.

<Callout type="eli5">

The exchange is the market hall. It owns the roof, sets the opening time and makes sure nobody uses dodgy scales. It never sells you an orange itself.

</Callout>

## The broker: your way in

You are not allowed to walk into the exchange and shout. Access is restricted to member firms. So you need somebody with a pass to act on your behalf, and that is your **broker**.

A <Term id="broker">broker</Term> holds your account, holds your cash and shares, takes your instruction, and routes it to a venue where it can be filled. The app on your phone is a broker's shop window.

Brokers make money in several ways, and it is worth knowing which apply to yours: commission per trade, a wider spread than the true market, interest on your idle cash, fees for lending out your shares, and in some countries payment for routing your order to a particular firm. "Zero commission" never means free. It means the cost moved somewhere less visible.

Choosing a broker properly is its own lesson late in the course, but one rule applies from day one: use a broker regulated in a country with a functioning regulator, and check the registration yourself rather than trusting the website.

## The market maker: the stall that always has oranges

Here is the piece that makes modern markets work, and the piece beginners have usually never heard of.

Imagine you want to sell 100 shares at 10:31am on a Tuesday. What are the odds that at exactly that moment another ordinary person wants to buy exactly 100 of the same shares? Poor. And yet your order fills instantly. Why?

Because a **market maker** took the other side. A <Term id="market-maker">market maker</Term> is a firm that continuously quotes both a price at which it will buy and a slightly higher price at which it will sell, in the same instrument, all day long. It does not care which direction the price goes. It makes its living from the gap between those two prices, the <Term id="spread">spread</Term>, repeated millions of times.

Think of the stallholder who always has oranges and always wants to buy more oranges. She will buy from you at 90 cents and sell to the next person at 95 cents. She is not predicting the orange market. She is being paid five cents for being there when either of you shows up.

Market makers are why you can trade a large, popular share instantly at a sensible price. They are also why illiquid things are painful: when nobody is willing to stand there quoting, the gap between the buying price and the selling price widens, sometimes enormously.

## Institutions: the vans buying pallets

**Institutional investors** are the big money: pension funds, mutual funds, insurance companies, sovereign wealth funds, endowments and hedge funds. Between them they own the majority of the world's publicly traded shares.

They matter to you for a practical reason. When a pension fund decides to buy $400 million of a share, it cannot simply click buy, because doing so would push the price up against itself. Instead it feeds the order into the market in slices over hours or days, using algorithms designed to hide the size.

That patient, persistent pressure is what creates many of the trends, support levels and volume patterns you will learn to read in later modules. When you see a share grind steadily upward on heavy volume for three weeks, you are usually watching a large buyer work through a shopping list.

**Hedge funds** deserve a separate mention: they are pooled funds allowed to use strategies ordinary funds cannot, including short selling and heavy leverage. They are a small share of the money and a large share of the trading.

## High-frequency firms: the fastest in the room

A **high-frequency trading** firm is a market participant whose entire edge is speed. Their computers sit physically inside or beside the exchange's data centre, they measure their advantage in microseconds, and they typically hold positions for seconds or less.

Much of what they do is market making at enormous scale, which genuinely helps: spreads today are far tighter than they were twenty years ago, and that saving lands in your pocket on every trade. Some of what they do is arbitrage, buying something in one venue and selling it in another where it is a fraction of a cent more expensive.

<Callout type="warning" title="Do not try to compete on speed">

You will never out-race a firm whose cables are measured in metres to shave nanoseconds. Accept it and choose a game they are not playing. Their advantage lives in the tiniest slices of time, so strategies with holding periods of hours, days or weeks are largely unaffected by them. Any strategy of yours that only works if you are fastest is already lost.

</Callout>

## Retail: you

**Retail traders** are individuals trading their own money, which by the end of this course includes you. Collectively retail is a meaningful share of daily volume in some markets and a small share in others, and it has grown a lot since commission-free apps arrived.

Your disadvantages are real: less information, slower execution, worse fees, no research team. Your advantages are also real, and people underrate them. You can sit in cash for six weeks and nobody fires you. You can trade instruments too small for a fund to bother with. You have no investors demanding quarterly performance. Nobody forces you to take a position on a bad day.

## Regulators: the inspector upstairs

**Regulators** are government or quasi-government bodies that write and enforce the rules: the SEC and FINRA in the United States, the FCA in the United Kingdom, ESMA across the European Union, SEBI in India, ASIC in Australia. They license brokers and exchanges, punish manipulation and insider dealing, and set protections such as segregation of client money.

They do not protect you from losing money on a bad trade. They try to make sure the game is not rigged and that your broker does not vanish with your cash. That is a smaller promise than people assume, and it is still the reason you should never use an unregulated venue.

## So who is taking the other side of your trade?

Almost always one of three parties.

| Who | When | What they want |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A market maker | Most small retail orders in liquid instruments | The spread, and to stay flat on direction |
| Another trader or investor | Anyone whose view or horizon differs from yours | To be right about direction, over their horizon |
| An institution working an order | Often, in size, during busy parts of the day | To fill a large order without moving price |

Sit with that for a moment. Every time you buy, somebody equally convinced is selling. They may be a computer that has no opinion at all, or a fund that has held for nine years and is finally trimming, or a trader whose chart says the opposite of yours.

That is the useful humility to carry into Module 1. You are not playing against the market. You are playing against the other people in the hall, and some of them are very good.

<KeyTakeaways>
- The exchange is the venue and the rulebook; its matching engine pairs buy and sell orders and prints the price.
- A broker is your licensed access to that venue, and it earns money through commissions, spreads, interest and order routing.
- Market makers quote both a buy and a sell price continuously, earning the spread, which is why liquid instruments fill instantly.
- Institutions own most shares and must work large orders slowly, which creates much of the trend and volume you will learn to read.
- High-frequency firms compete on speed you cannot match, so avoid any strategy that depends on being fastest.
- Regulators try to keep the game fair and your money segregated, but they never protect you from a bad trade.
</KeyTakeaways>
