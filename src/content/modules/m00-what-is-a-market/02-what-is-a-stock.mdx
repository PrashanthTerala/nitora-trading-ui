Your lemonade stand did well. So well that you now want a proper cart with a chiller, an umbrella and a hundred lemons a week. That costs $1,000 and you have $200. You could borrow the rest, but you hate the idea of owing money. So you try something else.

You cut a piece of card into ten pieces and write on each one: *this card entitles the holder to one tenth of this lemonade business, forever.* You keep six cards and sell four to friends for $200 each. You now have $1,000, a cart, and four people who own a piece of what you built.

Those cards are **shares**. That is genuinely all a share is: a small, permanent slice of ownership in a business, cut into pieces so it can be sold. Everything else, the ticker symbols, the flashing screens, the billion-dollar headlines, is decoration on top of ten pieces of card.

## Cutting a business into slices

A business that does this is called a **company**, and the slices it issues are shares (Americans often say *stock*, which is the same idea used as a mass noun: "I own stock in that company" means "I own some of its shares").

If your cart business issues ten shares and you own six, you own 60% of the business. If it issues a million shares and you own six hundred thousand, you still own 60%. The number of slices does not change the size of the cake; it only changes how thinly it is cut.

A company whose shares anyone can buy on an exchange is a **public company**. One whose shares are held by a handful of founders and investors is a **private company**. The moment a private company first sells shares to the public is its **IPO**, short for initial public offering. That is the day the cards go on sale to strangers.

<Callout type="eli5">

A company is a cake. Shares are slices. Buying a share means buying one slice, so you now own a real, if tiny, piece of the cake, along with everyone else who bought slices.

</Callout>

## Why would anyone sell a piece of a good thing?

This confuses beginners, and it is a fair question. If the cart is going to make money, why give away 40% of it?

**Because 60% of a cart beats 100% of a jug.** Without the $200 cards, you have a folding table and a jug. With them, you have a cart that serves five times as many people. A smaller slice of a much bigger business is worth more.

**Because the money does not have to be paid back.** A loan has to be repaid whether or not it rains all summer. Shareholders take that risk with you. If the business fails, they lose their $200 and you owe them nothing.

**Because the founders eventually want to cash out.** Somebody who spent fifteen years building a company may want a house, or to retire, or to start something else. Selling shares turns years of effort into money they can actually spend.

Real companies do this at enormous scale. When a firm sells shares to the public, it might raise hundreds of millions of dollars for factories, research or paying off debt, and in exchange the founders end up owning a modest slice of something very large.

## What you actually own

Buying a share does not give you the right to walk into the head office and take a laptop. What it gives you is a bundle of three things.

**A claim on the profits.** If the business earns money, some of that money belongs to you in proportion to your slice. Sometimes it is paid out; more often it is kept and reinvested to grow the business, which should make your slice more valuable.

**A vote.** Shareholders vote on big decisions, like who sits on the board of directors. With one share out of a billion your vote is symbolic, but the right is real.

**A claim on what is left if it all ends.** If the company is wound up, shareholders get whatever is left after every debt, supplier and employee is paid. In a failure that is usually nothing at all, which is why shares are riskier than lending money to the same company.

<Callout type="danger" title="Shares are last in the queue">

If a company goes bankrupt, banks and bondholders are paid first, then suppliers and staff, then shareholders get the leftovers. There are usually no leftovers. A share can and sometimes does go to zero. No amount of chart reading changes that order of the queue.

</Callout>

## Dividends: getting paid to hold

When your cart has a good summer and ends up with $500 of spare cash, you have a choice. You can spend it on a second cart, or you can split it across the ten cards at $50 each.

That handout is a **dividend**: a share of the profits paid in cash to shareholders, usually every three or six months. Mature, steady businesses (think supermarkets, utilities, big banks) tend to pay generous dividends because they have fewer exciting things to build. Fast-growing businesses often pay nothing at all, on the argument that they can turn your $50 into $200 by reinvesting it.

Neither approach is better. A dividend is not free money either: on the day a share goes **ex-dividend**, which is the cut-off day after which a new buyer no longer receives the upcoming payout, the price typically drops by roughly the amount being paid. You moved value from one pocket to another.

## Market cap: the price of the whole cake

Here is the piece of arithmetic that makes headlines make sense.

If you multiply the price of one share by the total number of shares in existence, you get what the market currently thinks the whole business is worth. That number is the **market capitalisation**, almost always shortened to <Term id="market-cap">market cap</Term>.

Work it through with a made-up company. Suppose Nova Robotics has 40 million shares in existence and each one last traded at $25.

- Market cap = 40,000,000 shares times $25 = **$1,000,000,000**, or one billion dollars.

Now suppose good news arrives and the share price rises to $30. Nothing about the factory changed overnight, but:

- New market cap = 40,000,000 times $30 = **$1.2 billion**.

The market just marked the whole business up by $200 million, because a handful of people agreed to swap at $30 instead of $25. Remember [lesson 1](/learn/m00-what-is-a-market/01-the-lemonade-stand): that is an agreement, not a measurement.

<Callout type="warning" title="Share price alone tells you nothing about size">

A $500 share is not "expensive" and a $3 share is not "cheap". A company with 2 million shares at $500 is worth $1 billion. A company with 5 billion shares at $3 is worth $15 billion. Only the price times the share count tells you the size of the business. Beginners lose a lot of money buying $2 shares because the number looks small.

</Callout>

<KeyTakeaways>
- A share is a small permanent slice of ownership in a business, nothing more mysterious than that.
- Founders sell shares to raise money that never has to be repaid, and to turn years of work into cash.
- A shareholder owns a claim on profits, a vote, and whatever is left over if the company is wound up.
- Shareholders are last in the queue in a bankruptcy, which is why a share can go to zero.
- A dividend hands out part of the profits in cash, and the share price usually falls by about the same amount.
- Market cap equals share price times the number of shares, and it is the only sensible way to judge a company's size.
</KeyTakeaways>
