It is a hot Saturday and you have set up a table at the end of your street with a jug of lemonade and a stack of paper cups. You have no idea what to charge. You write $1 on a cardboard sign because it feels about right. A neighbour walks past, looks at the sign, hands you a dollar and takes a cup.

That small moment is the entire foundation of every financial market on Earth. Two people looked at the same cup of lemonade, one of them valued it at more than a dollar, the other valued it at less than a dollar, and so they swapped. Nothing else happened. No expert calculated the correct value of lemonade. No authority approved the number.

Everything in this course, all the charts and candles and orders, is built on top of that one idea. So before we touch anything that looks like trading, let us take the lemonade stand apart and see exactly where the number on the sign comes from.

## A price is two people agreeing to swap

Imagine the school playground at lunchtime. You have an apple. Your friend has a chocolate biscuit. You would rather have the biscuit; your friend would rather have the apple. You swap, both of you walk away happier, and nothing was created except happiness.

That is a **trade**. A trade happens when two people value the same two things in opposite directions. If you both wanted the biscuit more, there would be no swap. The disagreement is the whole point.

Now suppose your friend will not swap straight across. They want your apple *and* fifty pence. You think about it and agree. The **price** of the biscuit, in apple-plus-cash terms, has just been discovered. It was not discovered by thinking hard. It was discovered by two people pushing against each other until they met somewhere.

<Callout type="eli5">

A price is not a sticker that comes attached to a thing. It is the number two people finally shook hands on. If nobody shakes hands, there is no price, only wishes.

</Callout>

## Scarcity and desire set the number

Back to the lemonade stand. Two things move your sign.

The first is **scarcity**: how much of it there is. If you brewed twenty litres and only three people live on your street, you will be dropping your price by the afternoon. If you have one cup left and four sweaty joggers arrive at once, that last cup is suddenly worth a lot more than a dollar.

The second is **desire**, which traders call demand: how badly people want it. On a freezing February morning, lemonade is worth almost nothing on the same street where it was worth two dollars in July. The lemonade did not change. The people did.

Put those together and you get the rule underneath every market: **price moves to the level where the number of people willing to buy roughly matches the number of things available to sell.** If your $1 sign has a queue forming, your price is too low. If nobody stops all afternoon, it is too high. The queue is your feedback.

<Callout type="story" title="Two stands on one street">

Now a second kid sets up a stand across the road at 80 cents. Suddenly your $1 has competition and the joggers walk past you. You drop to 75 cents. They drop to 70. Then your rival runs out of lemons and you push back up to 90. Nobody is in charge of that dance. It is just two sellers and a street full of buyers finding a number together, over and over, all afternoon. Swap the lemonade for shares in a company and you have a stock exchange.

</Callout>

## The last price is a number, not a truth

Here is the idea that beginners find hardest, and it is worth reading twice.

When you look up a share and see "$142.60", that is not a measurement of what the company is worth. It is a receipt. It means: the most recent time two strangers agreed to swap this thing, they agreed on $142.60. That is all. It is history, not physics.

Three things follow from that, and they matter for the rest of your trading life.

**The price can be wrong and still be the price.** A cup of lemonade might trade at $5 because a rich tourist was thirsty. That does not mean lemonade is worth $5. It means one transaction happened at $5.

**The price only exists at the moment of the trade.** Between trades the price is stale. The last agreed number can sit there for minutes while the world changes around it.

**There is no single price, even now.** At any instant somebody is willing to buy at one number and somebody is willing to sell at a slightly higher number. You will meet those two numbers properly in [Bid, Ask and the Spread](/learn/m00-what-is-a-market/07-bid-ask-spread).

<Callout type="warning" title="Do not confuse price with value">

"It fell from $200 to $50, so it must be cheap" is the single most expensive sentence in trading. Price tells you what people last agreed. Value is your own opinion about what the thing deserves. They can disagree for years, and the market has no obligation to come round to your view.

</Callout>

## Why any of this needs a market

Your street has one stand and a handful of buyers. That works, badly. If you want to sell a cup at 3am, tough luck. If a buyer wants ten cups, you may not have them.

A **market** is simply a place, physical or electronic, where lots of buyers and lots of sellers gather at the same time so that a swap is always possible. The more people gather, the easier it is to trade quickly at a fair-looking number. Traders call that ease <Term id="liquidity">liquidity</Term>, and you will hear the word constantly.

A market does not set prices. It just makes it very easy for strangers to keep agreeing on them, thousands of times a second. Every candle you will ever stare at is a summary of those agreements.

<KeyTakeaways>
- A trade happens when two people value the same thing in opposite directions and agree to swap.
- A price is the number they agreed on, not a fact about what something is worth.
- Scarcity and desire push the price to the level where buyers and available supply roughly balance.
- The quoted price is a receipt from the most recent trade, so it is history rather than a prediction.
- A market is just a place where enough buyers and sellers gather that a swap is almost always possible.
- Price and value are different things and can disagree for a very long time.
</KeyTakeaways>
