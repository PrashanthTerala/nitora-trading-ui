Go back to the covered market from the last lesson, but this time walk the whole length of it. The first aisle sells single items: one orange, one apple. The second sells ready-made boxes containing a bit of everything. The third has a board on the wall showing the average price of fruit across the whole town, and people are betting on that number without ever touching fruit. At the far end, two men are signing a contract about oranges that will not be picked until November.

Every one of those aisles exists in financial markets, and the thing being bought gets a different name in each. Beginners often assume "trading" means shares, then get confused when someone talks about pips, contracts or expiry.

This lesson walks the aisles. You do not need to master any of them today. You need to be able to hear the words and know roughly what is being talked about, and to know which ones are dangerous to walk into early.

## The eight things people trade

### Stocks

A **stock**, or share, is a slice of ownership in one company, exactly as you met in [lesson 2](/learn/m00-what-is-a-market/02-what-is-a-stock). You buy 20 shares of a car maker at $60, it rises to $66, and you are up $120. You own a real piece of a real business, you may receive dividends, and in a bankruptcy you are last in the queue.

*What makes it tricky:* a single company can be destroyed by a single event. One failed drug trial, one accounting scandal, one product recall, and your share drops 40% overnight while the rest of the market is calm.

### ETFs

An **exchange traded fund** is a basket of many assets, wrapped up so that the whole basket trades like a single share. Buy one unit of a broad US equity <Term id="etf">ETF</Term> and you have, in one click, a tiny piece of hundreds of companies at once. There are ETFs for gold, for bonds, for one country, for one industry.

*What makes it tricky:* the wrapper hides what is inside. Two funds with similar names can hold very different things, charge very different fees, and some specialist ETFs use leverage or derivatives internally that behave badly over long holding periods.

### Indices

An **index** is not a thing you can own at all; it is a *number* that summarises a basket. The S and P 500 measures 500 large US companies, the FTSE 100 measures 100 large UK ones, the Nikkei 225 measures Japan. When the news says "the market fell 1% today", it means an index fell 1%.

Because you cannot own a number, you get exposure to an index through something that tracks it: an ETF, a future, or a contract at your broker. Index products are popular because they are diversified by construction, so no single company can wreck them.

*What makes it tricky:* people forget an index is a construction with rules. Some are weighted so that a handful of giant companies drive most of the movement, meaning your "diversified" index is quietly a bet on a few names.

### Forex

**Forex**, short for foreign exchange, is trading one currency against another. You never buy a currency alone: you always buy one and sell another at the same time, which is why quotes come in pairs like EUR/USD or GBP/JPY. Buying EUR/USD at 1.0850 and selling at 1.0900 means the euro strengthened against the dollar and you profited from the difference.

The forex market is enormous, runs around the clock on weekdays, and moves in tiny increments, which is why brokers offer high leverage on it.

*What makes it tricky:* that leverage. Currency moves are small in percentage terms, so retail forex is usually sold with large borrowed multiples, and large multiples turn ordinary wiggles into account-emptying losses.

### Crypto

**Cryptocurrencies** are digital assets recorded on a blockchain rather than issued by a company or a country. Bitcoin and Ether are the two everyone has heard of; thousands of others exist. You can buy 0.05 of a coin as easily as five, which makes crypto unusually accessible.

Crypto trades 24 hours a day, seven days a week, including Christmas morning.

*What makes it tricky:* everything. Volatility is several times that of shares, regulation varies wildly by country, exchanges have failed and taken customer funds with them, and there is no underlying business producing profits to anchor a valuation.

### Commodities

**Commodities** are physical raw materials: crude oil, natural gas, gold, silver, copper, wheat, coffee, cattle. Their prices are driven by things you can picture, such as weather, wars, mine strikes, harvests and shipping routes. Gold in particular is watched as a place money runs to when investors are frightened.

Most retail traders get commodity exposure through an ETF or a future rather than by taking delivery of a truckload of soybeans.

*What makes it tricky:* supply shocks are brutal and unpredictable. A pipeline problem or a frost in Brazil can move a commodity 15% in a session for reasons no chart could have hinted at.

### Futures

A **future** is a contract to buy or sell a specific quantity of something at a set price on a set date. It began as a genuine tool for farmers and airlines: lock in the price of wheat or jet fuel now so a bad season does not ruin you. Today most futures are traded by people who never intend to touch the underlying goods.

Futures exist on commodities, indices, currencies, interest rates and more. Each contract is standardised: one contract equals a fixed quantity, so buying one is buying a defined chunk of exposure, not a dollar amount you choose.

*What makes it tricky:* built-in leverage and an expiry date. You post a small deposit called margin to control a large contract value, so small moves have outsized effects on your account, and the contract stops existing on a fixed date whether or not you were ready.

### Options

An **option** is the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell something at a fixed price before a deadline. Paying $2 per share for the right to buy a $50 stock at $55 any time in the next three months is buying a call option. If the stock reaches $65, that right is valuable. If it sits at $48, the right expires worthless and your $2 is gone.

Options are used to speculate with limited outlay, to hedge existing positions, and to generate income by selling the rights to other people.

*What makes it tricky:* options lose value simply because time passes, and their price depends on expected volatility as well as direction. You can be right about the direction and still lose everything because you were right too slowly.

<Callout type="eli5">

Stocks are single fruits. ETFs are ready-made fruit boxes. Indices are the scoreboard on the wall. Forex is swapping one country's money for another's. Crypto is a new kind of digital token. Commodities are the raw stuff itself. Futures are a signed promise about November. Options are a ticket that lets you change your mind, and expires.

</Callout>

## The comparison

| Instrument | You are buying | Trades when | Built-in leverage | Beginner friendly |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Stocks | A slice of one company | Exchange hours | No | Yes |
| ETFs | A basket of many assets | Exchange hours | Usually no | Yes |
| Indices | Exposure to a number summarising a basket | Depends on the product | Depends on the product | Fairly |
| Forex | One currency against another | 24 hours, weekdays | Yes, often large | No |
| Crypto | A digital asset on a blockchain | 24 hours, 7 days | Only if you choose it | No |
| Commodities | A physical raw material | Mostly futures hours | Via futures | Not really |
| Futures | A standardised contract with an expiry | Nearly 24 hours, weekdays | Yes | No |
| Options | A right that expires | Exchange hours | Effectively yes | No |

<Callout type="danger" title="Leverage is the common thread in every blow-up">

Look at the leverage column. The instruments that destroy beginner accounts fastest are the ones where a small deposit controls a large position: forex, futures and options. The danger is not that they are complicated. It is that a 2% move against you can cost 50% of your account, and you can lose more than you put in with some products. Learn on something without leverage first.

</Callout>

## Where to start

The boring answer is the right one. **Learn on plain shares and broad ETFs, on the daily and hourly charts.** They move at human speed, they have no expiry date, they cannot lose you more than you put in, and every skill you build there transfers directly to everything else in the table.

Module 11 covers options, futures, forex and crypto properly, once you already understand charts, orders and risk. Reaching for them now is like learning to drive in a race car: the controls are the same, but the consequences of a small error are not.
