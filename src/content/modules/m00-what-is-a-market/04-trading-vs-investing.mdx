Two people own an apple tree. The first waters it, prunes it once a year, and waits a decade for it to produce a reliable crop. The second buys apples at the market gate in the morning and sells them at the station in the afternoon for a few pence more.

Both can make money. Neither is doing the other's job. The gardener would be terrible at the station because she is not watching the crowd, and the market trader would be terrible with the tree because he cannot stand waiting ten years for anything.

Trading and investing are those two people. They happen on the same field, with the same shares, using the same screens, and almost everything else about them is different: the clock, the evidence, the skills and the honest odds. Most beginners lose money because they start doing one and quietly drift into the other without noticing.

## The one real difference: where the money comes from

Strip away the jargon and the split is simple.

An **investor** makes money because the business underneath the share grows. Profits rise, dividends arrive, the company is worth more in ten years than today, and the share price follows over the long run. The investor's partner is time.

A **trader** makes money because the price moves, up or down, over minutes, days or weeks. Whether the business prospers over a decade is irrelevant to a trade that lasts four hours. The trader's partner is volatility.

That single difference drives everything else.

<Callout type="eli5">

An investor buys a puppy and hopes it grows into a fine dog. A trader borrows a bicycle for an afternoon and hopes to rent it out for slightly more than they paid. One cares what the thing becomes. The other only cares what the next person will pay.

</Callout>

## The comparison table

| | Trading | Investing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Holding period** | Seconds to a few weeks | Years to decades |
| **What you analyse** | Price, volume, chart structure, order flow, short-term catalysts | Earnings, revenue, debt, management, industry, valuation |
| **What you need** | Screen time, a tested plan, strict risk rules, emotional control | Patience, savings discipline, a long horizon, ability to ignore noise |
| **How you make money** | Repeated small edges on price movement, compounded over many trades | Business growth, reinvested profits and dividends, compounded over years |
| **Number of decisions** | Hundreds or thousands a year | A handful a year |
| **Main enemy** | Costs, overtrading and your own emotions | Panic-selling in a crash, and fees |
| **Typical outcome** | A large majority lose money or underperform a simple index fund | Broad index investors have historically done reasonably well over long periods |
| **Time commitment** | High and ongoing | Low once set up |

Read the "typical outcome" row again, because it is the row that people skip.

## The honest odds

This site exists to teach you trading, so you deserve the uncomfortable statistic up front rather than in the small print.

Across many studies of retail day traders, in several countries and decades, the consistent finding is that **the large majority lose money over any meaningful period**, and that only a small minority beat what they would have earned by putting the same money in a low-cost index fund and doing nothing. The frequently quoted figures for profitable day traders sit somewhere in the range of one in ten to one in five, and the survivors tend to be the ones who traded for years, kept records and managed risk obsessively.

Professional fund managers, with research teams and better information than you will ever have, mostly fail to beat their benchmark index over ten years too.

<Callout type="danger" title="Nobody is owed a profit">

If somebody tells you trading is a reliable income, a way to replace your salary in six months, or a skill you can buy in a weekend course, they are selling something. Trading is a competitive skill practised against people with faster computers and better information than you. It can be learned, slowly, with real risk of loss. Treat any promise of returns as a red flag.

</Callout>

So why learn it at all? Because the skill is real, the minority who succeed are not lucky in some random way, and because understanding how prices are made will make you a better investor even if you never place a short-term trade. But go in with the correct expectation: you are learning a difficult craft, not unlocking a money machine.

## Which one are you doing?

Most beginners intend to trade and accidentally invest, in the worst way possible. It goes like this.

You buy a share planning to sell it in three days. It falls. Selling would confirm a loss, which hurts, so you tell yourself you are now a long-term investor in this fine company. Six months later you are still holding, down 40%, with money tied up that could have funded twenty good trades.

That is not investing. Investing is a decision made in advance, with a reason and a horizon. What happened there was a loss that got rebranded to avoid the pain of admitting it. The technical name for the habit is loss aversion, and Module 8 is largely about it.

The cure is a rule you write down before you buy: **what kind of position is this, and what would make me exit it?** A trade has a planned exit price. An investment has a planned reason to sell, such as the business deteriorating. If you cannot say which you are doing, you are gambling.

<Callout type="tip">

There is nothing wrong with doing both, with separate money in separate accounts. Many sensible people hold a boring long-term index portfolio for their future and trade a small, clearly ring-fenced amount for the craft of it. What destroys accounts is mixing the two pots and letting the labels slide.

</Callout>

<KeyTakeaways>
- An investor is paid by the growth of the underlying business over years; a trader is paid by price movement over minutes to weeks.
- The holding period, the evidence you study and the skills you need all follow from that one difference.
- Trading needs screen time, a tested plan and strict risk rules; investing mostly needs patience.
- A large majority of active retail traders lose money or underperform a simple index fund.
- The most common beginner mistake is turning a losing trade into an unplanned long-term investment.
- Decide before you buy which game you are playing and what would make you exit.
</KeyTakeaways>
