People who want to lose weight and cannot work out why they are not losing weight are usually told to keep a food diary. Write down everything, every day, including the biscuit at 4pm. Writing it down burns no calories at all. It works anyway, and it works reliably, because a written record is the one thing a person cannot argue with.

Trading has the same problem and the same cure. Ask a struggling trader what went wrong last month and you will hear a story: choppy conditions, bad luck, one stupid Tuesday. Ask them for the numbers and there are no numbers, so the story stands unchallenged. Every month it gets told again, and every month nothing changes.

A **trading journal** is the record that ends that. It is not homework and it is not a diary of feelings. It is the only instrument you own that can tell you which of your ideas actually make money, and this lesson is deliberately placed first in the final module because it is the habit with the highest return per minute spent in all of trading.

## What goes in every row

A journal entry has two halves: what happened, and why you did it. Most beginners record the first half and skip the second, which is exactly backwards, because the first half is already on your broker statement.

| Field | Why it earns its place |
| --- | --- |
| Date and time | Shows whether your losses cluster at particular hours |
| Symbol | Reveals the instrument that quietly costs you money |
| Setup name | The single most valuable column; without it you can never compare like with like |
| Direction | Many traders are far better long than short, or the reverse |
| Entry price | The anchor for everything else |
| Stop price | Proves you had one before you entered |
| Target | Lets you measure whether your targets are realistic |
| Position size | Checks that your sizing follows [the 1% rule](/learn/m07-risk-management/02-the-one-percent-rule) rather than your mood |
| Risk in money | One number, the same on every trade if you are sizing properly |
| Exit price and exit reason | Target, stop, time, or "I panicked" are four completely different trades |
| R result | Makes a $40 trade and a $4,000 trade directly comparable |
| Duration | Exposes the trades you hold far too long |
| Emotional state before entry | The column that eventually explains your worst trades |
| Screenshot of the chart | Memory rewrites charts; pictures do not |
| Mistake tag | A short label like "chased entry" or "moved stop" that you can count later |

<Callout type="eli5">

Think of a football coach filming every match. The team remembers "we played badly". The video shows that they concede almost every goal in the last ten minutes when they stop tracking runners. One of those is a feeling. The other is a fixable problem. Your journal is the camera.

</Callout>

## A sample row

Here is what two real entries look like. Split the table in two so it stays readable: the numbers first, then the story.

| Date | Symbol | Dir | Setup | Entry | Stop | Target | Size | Risk | Exit | R |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 4 Mar | NOVA | Long | Pullback to 20 EMA | 42.10 | 41.30 | 43.70 | 250 | $200 | 43.70 | +2.0R |
| 5 Mar | BLUE | Long | Range breakout | 18.55 | 18.05 | 19.55 | 400 | $200 | 18.20 | -0.7R |

Check the arithmetic, because doing this yourself is half the value. On NOVA the stop is 80 cents away, and 250 shares times $0.80 is exactly $200 of risk. The target is $1.60 away, which is 2R, and it was hit, so the trade paid $400, or +2.0R. On BLUE the stop is 50 cents away and 400 shares times $0.50 is the same $200. The exit at 18.20 lost 35 cents a share, which is $140, and $140 divided by $200 is **0.7R**.

| Date | Exit reason | Duration | State before | Mistake tag | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 4 Mar | Target hit | 1h 40m | Calm, planned the night before | None | Waited for the retest instead of the first touch. Boring entry, and that was the point. |
| 5 Mar | Manual exit, above stop | 22m | Restless, had taken no trade all morning | Entered on thin volume | Breakout had half the usual volume and I took it anyway. Then cut it early out of doubt. |

Notice that the losing trade is the more useful row. It carries a named mistake and an honest note, and if "entered on thin volume" appears nine times this month, you have found a rule to write down rather than a mood to regret.

<Callout type="warning" title="The two ways a journal becomes worthless">

**Recording only the money.** A column of profits and losses tells you what happened, never why. You cannot improve a number; you can only improve a decision.

**Writing it later.** Filling in the emotional state on Sunday for a Tuesday trade produces fiction, and flattering fiction at that. [Hindsight bias](/learn/m08-trading-psychology/04-cognitive-biases) is not a character flaw, it is how memory works. The note has to be written within minutes of the exit, while it still stings.

</Callout>

## Two minutes now, ten minutes later

The journal that survives is the one that is quick. Aim for a two-minute entry at the moment of the trade: screenshot, setup name, the numbers, one honest sentence, one mistake tag if there is one. That is it.

The thinking happens later, in a weekly session of about twenty minutes. Trying to analyse while positions are open is how people talk themselves into revenge trades.

It is also worth logging the trades you did **not** take. A setup you spotted, sized, and then talked yourself out of is data, and a column of missed winners is the clearest possible evidence that your problem is nerve rather than strategy.

## The weekly review questions

Every week, sit with the record and answer these in writing. Short answers are fine; vague ones are not.

1. What was my total R for the week, and how many trades produced it?
2. Which setup made the most R, and which lost the most?
3. Did I follow every rule in my plan? Exactly how many times did I break one, and which?
4. What was my biggest loss, and was it a bad trade or a good trade that lost?
5. Which mistake tag appears most often this week?
6. What was happening in my life or my mood on my three worst trades?
7. What is the **one** thing I will do differently next week?

That last question is the whole exercise, and it has a strict limit of one. The reason for the limit gets a lesson of its own in [the weekly review loop](/learn/m12-becoming-consistent/03-review-and-improve).

## Why this is the highest-return habit

Every other improvement in trading costs money, time or both. Better data costs money. A new strategy costs months of testing. Journaling costs a few minutes a day and pays in three ways at once.

- **It makes your edge visible.** You cannot know that your pullback trades earn 2R and your breakout trades lose 1R until you have labelled them and counted.
- **It makes rule-breaking expensive.** Knowing you will have to write down "moved my stop" is a surprisingly strong deterrent at the moment your finger is over the mouse.
- **It converts randomness into sample size.** One hundred honest rows is the smallest thing that can be called evidence, and evidence is what separates a trader from someone with opinions.

The traders who last are not the ones with the best entries. They are the ones who are still running the same measurable process a year later, having quietly removed the three habits that were costing them most. That removal starts here, with a boring table.

<KeyTakeaways>
- A trading journal records the decision, not just the result: setup, entry, stop, size, exit reason, R, mood and a screenshot.
- The setup name and the mistake tag are the two columns that make improvement possible, because they let you count patterns in your own behaviour.
- Log every trade within minutes of the exit; anything written days later is reconstructed and flattering.
- Recording profit and loss alone is nearly useless, since you can only improve decisions, not numbers.
- A weekly review of seven questions, ending in exactly one change, turns the record into progress.
- It is the highest-return habit in trading because it costs minutes, makes your real edge visible and makes breaking your own rules uncomfortable.
</KeyTakeaways>
