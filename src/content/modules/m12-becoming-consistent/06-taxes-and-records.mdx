Someone opens a small market stall. For the first year they keep the takings in a tin and throw the receipts away, because it is only a little money and it hardly feels like a business. In year two the stall does well, and suddenly they need to prove what they bought, what they sold and what it cost. The tin cannot help them, and reconstructing a year of paperwork costs far more than keeping it would have.

Trading works the same way, with one extra twist: your broker already reports a great deal of it. Tax authorities in most countries receive information about accounts and transactions automatically, so the record exists whether or not you kept your own copy. The only question is whether your version agrees with theirs.

This lesson is about the general shape of the problem and the habit that solves it. It deliberately gives you no rules, because the rules are not the same for any two readers of this page.

<Callout type="warning" title="Read this part carefully">

**Tax rules vary enormously by country, and often by your personal circumstances within a country.** How trading profits are classified, what rates apply, which losses can be used and what must be reported are all different from one jurisdiction to the next, and they change.

Nothing on this page is tax advice, and it is not written for your country. Use it to understand the concepts and to ask better questions. Then **consult a qualified tax professional in your own jurisdiction**, ideally one who has dealt with traders before, and follow their advice over anything you read online, including this.

Two more things worth saying plainly. Someone in a forum confidently explaining what is taxable is not a source. And in most places the legal responsibility for an accurate return sits with you, not with your broker or your accountant.

</Callout>

## Realised and unrealised

The most useful concept to carry into that conversation is the difference between two kinds of gain.

An **unrealised gain** is profit on a position you still hold. You bought at $40, it is now $55, and you are up $15 a share on paper. Nothing has been banked, and the gain can vanish tomorrow.

A **realised gain** is profit on a position you have closed. You sold at $55, so the $15 is now a completed fact.

Many tax systems are built around realisation: the taxable event is generally the closing of a position rather than the rising of a price, though there are jurisdictions and instrument types where that is not so. This has a practical consequence traders feel every December. Closing a winner converts a paper gain into a realised one, and the timing of that decision can sit inside one reporting period or the next.

<Callout type="eli5">

Your football sticker collection is suddenly worth £200 more than you paid. You are richer in a sense, but you have no extra pocket money until you actually sell the stickers. The moment you swap them for cash, something real has happened, and that moment is the one the grown-ups tend to care about.

</Callout>

## Holding periods

Many jurisdictions treat a position held for a long time differently from one held briefly, often more favourably for the longer hold. The dividing line, the size of the difference and whether the distinction exists at all vary widely.

There is a second, related idea worth knowing about. Some systems distinguish between investing and *carrying on a trading business*, based on factors like frequency, organisation and whether it is your main income. The classification can change which rules apply to you entirely, and someone taking dozens of short-term trades a month is much more likely to bump into that question than someone buying twice a year.

This matters for the choice you already made in [trading styles](/learn/m09-strategies/01-trading-styles). The treatment of your profits can depend on your holding period, so the style you pick may have consequences beyond the chart. That is a question for a professional, before you scale up rather than after.

## Losses usually matter

Here is the general principle most beginners do not know: in many systems, **losses can be set against gains**, so what is assessed is closer to your net result than to your winners alone. Unused losses can often be carried forward to later periods as well.

That is precisely why the trader who only records winning trades is making an expensive mistake in two directions at once. Losses that are not documented usually cannot be claimed.

Many jurisdictions also restrict claiming a loss if you sell something and buy it back almost immediately, on the grounds that nothing economically changed. The details differ hugely, so if your strategy involves exiting and re-entering the same instrument quickly, that is a specific question to raise.

## Keep everything, from day one

Whatever your rules turn out to be, the habit is identical and costs nothing.

| Keep | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Every monthly and annual broker statement | The primary record, and brokers do not keep them available forever |
| Every trade confirmation | Dates, prices and sizes for each position |
| All fees, commissions and financing charges | Costs typically reduce the taxable result |
| Currency conversion rates used | Needed when you trade a market in another currency |
| Deposit and withdrawal records | Shows what was capital and what was profit |
| Dividends, interest and other income | Usually treated separately from trading gains |
| Your own journal export | Independent confirmation if a statement is ever unavailable |

Download them as files rather than relying on the platform, because access ends when the account does. Keep them for at least as long as your jurisdiction requires, which is commonly several years and sometimes longer, and keep the currency of the account clearly labelled.

Good news: your [journal](/learn/m12-becoming-consistent/01-the-trading-journal) already collects most of this. A habit you adopted to improve your trading turns out to double as your paperwork, which is the cheapest overlap in this whole module.

## Questions to take to a professional

Write these down and get answers before you trade real money seriously.

1. How are my trading profits classified where I live, and at what rate?
2. Does a holding period change the treatment, and where is the line?
3. Can my losses offset my gains, and can unused losses be carried forward?
4. Are derivatives, foreign shares, forex and crypto treated differently from domestic shares?
5. What must I report, on what form, and by when?
6. Which records must I keep, and for how many years?
7. At what level of activity would I be considered to be running a business?

- An unrealised gain is profit on an open position; a realised gain is profit on a closed one, and many systems are built around the closing event.

- Losses can often be set against gains and carried forward, so undocumented losses are money left on the table.
