Imagine someone knocks on your door with a machine that reliably prints £500 a week. They would like you to have one, for a small fee. The obvious question is not whether the machine works. It is why a person holding a money printer is spending their Saturday knocking on doors.

That question answers almost every trading scam you will ever meet. The financial world is full of people offering you returns, and the offer itself is the evidence. A genuine, repeatable edge is the most valuable thing in finance, it scales with capital, and the cheapest way to profit from it is quietly, with your own money, telling nobody.

This lesson is about recognising the shapes these offers take. Some are outright crime, some are legal businesses with terrible odds, and a few are honest. The rule below sorts them faster than any list can.

<Callout type="danger" title="The one rule">

**Anyone who promises you returns is selling you something.**

Not "might be". Is. Returns cannot be promised by anybody, because nobody controls the market. So the promise is not a forecast, it is a sales technique, and the only question left is what you are being sold: a subscription, a course, a broker referral, or a complete fiction.

Honest educators sell education and say clearly that it may not make you money. That is the tell. Watch what someone claims to sell, then watch what they actually charge for.

</Callout>

## Signal groups and mentors

A **signal group** sends you trades to copy, usually for a monthly fee. Take the economics apart for a second. If the signals were profitable, the sender's income from subscriptions would be a rounding error next to trading them at size. And a group of 5,000 people all buying the same thin instrument at the same moment moves the price against themselves before most of them are filled.

The usual pattern is a channel full of winners with the losers deleted, "entry zones" so wide that almost any outcome can be called correct afterwards, and no stop published in advance. Ask one question: can I see every signal, in order, with entry, stop and exit time-stamped before the outcome was known? An honest answer settles it immediately.

Mentors are the same test. A mentor who reviews your journal and your process is teaching. A mentor who tells you what to buy is a signal group with better branding.

## Screenshots prove nothing

The single most persuasive thing in this industry is a photograph of a profit, and it is also the cheapest thing to manufacture.

- A demo account looks identical to a live one.
- The number in a browser can be edited in ten seconds with the tools built into every browser.
- Open positions show floating profit that has not been realised and may never be.
- Twenty small accounts produce one spectacular chart by luck alone, and the other nineteen are simply never mentioned.
- A screenshot of one trade says nothing about the ninety around it.

The only evidence that means anything is a full, continuous, third-party verified record covering every trade including the losses, over a long period. If someone has that, they will not be posting car photographs.

## Funded account challenges

Proprietary-firm "challenges" are not all fraud, and some are run properly, but the fine print is where the business model lives. You pay a fee to attempt a test on a simulated account, and passing entitles you to trade firm capital for a share of the profits.

| Rule in the fine print | What it actually does |
| --- | --- |
| Fee per attempt | The firm earns whether or not you pass, and failing invites you to pay again |
| Profit target of 8% to 10% | Requires aggressive sizing to hit in the time allowed |
| Daily loss limit around 5% | A single bad day ends the attempt, however good your month was |
| Trailing drawdown | The loss limit rises as your equity rises, so giving back part of a win can fail you |
| Consistency rule | No single day may be too large a share of your profit, which quietly bans your best trade |
| Minimum trading days | Forces trades you would not otherwise take |
| Payout split and schedule | Determines how much of your profit you keep, and when |

Notice the combination: a target that needs risk, and limits that punish risk. Firms that publish pass rates generally report single-digit percentages, and several firms have changed their rules or stopped paying out entirely. Treat a challenge as a paid exam with a low pass rate, not as a route to capital, and never pay for one with money you need.

## Pump and dump groups

An organiser accumulates a thin, cheap instrument. A message goes out to a large audience naming it, often with a countdown. The crowd buys, the price spikes, the organiser sells into that buying, and the price collapses within minutes.

The people you are buying from are the people who told you to buy. This is illegal market manipulation in regulated markets and routine in unregulated ones, and joining one does not make you an insider, it makes you the exit. Anything urging you to buy a specific ticker *right now*, in a group, at a specific time, is this.

## Romance and long-con investment scams

The most financially devastating scams do not look like trading at all. They begin as friendship or romance over weeks or months, on a dating app, a wrong-number text, or a professional network. There is no talk of money for a long time. Eventually a relative or mentor is mentioned who does well with a particular platform.

The platform is real-looking and entirely fake. Small withdrawals are honoured early to build trust. The balance rises beautifully, because it is a web page. When you try to take out a meaningful sum, there is a tax, a fee, a compliance hold, and then another one.

<Callout type="eli5">

A friendly stranger lets you play a fruit machine that always pays out, and lets you cash in your first small winnings straight away so you can see the money is real. Then they encourage you to put everything in. The machine was never connected to anything. The small payout was the advertisement.

</Callout>

Two hard rules protect you completely here. **Never invest through a platform introduced by someone you met online**, however long you have known them and however genuine the relationship feels. And **never pay a fee to release your own money**, because that is always the next stage of the theft, not the last step before payment.

## Gurus paid by your losses

Some educators are affiliates or introducing brokers. They receive a rebate for every lot you trade with their recommended broker, paid per unit of volume, forever.

That arrangement pays them more when you trade more often and larger, which is precisely the behaviour that loses beginners money. It is legal and often disclosed in small print. Just be clear about the alignment: their income depends on your activity, not on your results, and in a business where [roughly three in four retail leveraged accounts lose money](/learn/m11-derivatives-and-other-markets/07-etfs-and-leveraged-products), activity is what the broker wants too.

## How to verify a licence

This takes four minutes and defeats a large share of everything above.

1. **Find the claim.** The firm names a regulator and a licence or reference number, usually in the footer, for the exact legal entity described in [choosing a broker](/learn/m12-becoming-consistent/04-choosing-a-broker).
2. **Go to the regulator's own register by typing the address yourself.** The FCA Financial Services Register in the UK, BrokerCheck and Investor.gov in the US, ASIC's registers in Australia, and so on. Never use a link supplied by the firm, because it can point at a convincing copy.
3. **Search the number and the name together.** Check that both match, and read the permissions granted. Plenty of firms are registered for something unrelated and imply it covers investments.
4. **Compare the contact details.** If the address, phone number or website on the register differ from the ones you were given, you are probably dealing with a **clone firm**: a fraud using a real firm's licence details.
5. **Phone the number on the register**, not the one on the website, and ask whether they contacted you.
6. **Check the regulator's warning list** of unauthorised firms, which most publish and update constantly.

<Callout type="warning" title="If it has already happened">

Report it to the regulator and the police, tell your bank immediately, and stop all contact.

Then expect a second approach, sometimes months later, from a "recovery" service, a law firm, or an official-sounding agency offering to get your money back for an upfront fee. It is frequently the same people selling to a list of known victims. No legitimate recovery process asks for money in advance.

Being caught by one of these is not stupidity. They are designed by professionals to work on intelligent people, and the shame they create is part of the mechanism, because it stops victims talking.

</Callout>

<KeyTakeaways>
- Anyone promising returns is selling something, because a real edge is more profitable used quietly than sold.
- Signal groups fail their own economics, and profit screenshots are trivial to fake with demo accounts, editing or survivorship.
- Funded-account challenges combine an aggressive profit target with strict drawdown and consistency rules, and the fees are the firm's real business.
- Pump groups use their audience as the exit; the people telling you to buy are the ones selling.
- Long-con romance and platform scams show a fake balance, honour small early withdrawals, then demand fees to release your money.
- Verify any licence on the regulator's own register, typed in yourself, checking name, number, permissions and contact details against clone firms.
</KeyTakeaways>
