A learner driver can be genuinely competent in an empty car park. Mirrors, clutch, parking, all fine. Then they pull out onto a real road for the first time with traffic behind them and discover that the skill was never the hard part. The hard part is doing the same thing while a lorry is three metres away and someone is beeping.

Paper trading is the car park. It teaches the mechanics properly, and there is no substitute for it, but it removes the one ingredient that changes everything: the money is not real, so the fear is not real.

This lesson is about the crossing. When you are allowed to make it, how small to start, and how to scale without undoing everything you built. It is the last practical lesson on this site, and it is deliberately conservative, because the most common way a competent simulated trader fails is by going live too early and too large.

## The graduation checklist

Five conditions. Not four, and not "mostly". If any one of them is missing, you are not being denied anything valuable; you are being kept in the car park for a few more weeks.

| # | Condition | How you know |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | At least **100 simulated trades** | Your journal counts them |
| 2 | **Positive expectancy sustained over three months** | Positive in each month, not one great week carrying two bad ones |
| 3 | **Rules followed at least 95% of the time** | At most 5 rule breaks in 100 trades, counted honestly from your mistake tags |
| 4 | A **written trading plan** | Setup, entry, stop, target, size, management and review, all on paper |
| 5 | A **journal habit that is automatic** | You have not skipped an entry in weeks and it takes two minutes |

Each one is there for a reason.

**One hundred trades** is the smallest number at which your metrics stop being anecdotes. As [the metrics lesson](/learn/m12-becoming-consistent/02-performance-metrics) showed, ten trades can be carried entirely by one lucky outlier, and so can thirty.

**Three months** exists because markets have moods. A trend-following method looks brilliant in a trending quarter and dreadful in a choppy one, and a single month tells you which mood you got, not whether you have an edge.

**Ninety-five per cent rule-following** is the most important item on the list, and the one people argue with. The reason is simple: if you break your rules one trade in ten on a simulator, where nothing is at stake, you will break them far more often when real money is involved. Discipline does not improve under pressure. It degrades.

**A written plan** is what makes rule-following measurable at all. Without it there is nothing to follow, and every trade can be justified afterwards.

**The journal habit** matters because you are about to need better data than ever, at exactly the moment you will least feel like writing.

<Callout type="eli5">

Before a pilot flies passengers, they do not just prove they can fly. They prove they can fly the same way, repeatedly, for a long time, while someone watches and writes it down. Nobody is impressed by one good landing. Everyone is impressed by a hundred identical ones.

</Callout>

## The emotional gap

Assume you pass all five. Your first real trade will still feel completely different, and knowing why in advance is half the defence.

| What changes | Simulator | Real money |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A losing trade | A number going down | Money you earned, disappearing |
| A winner at plus 1R | You wait for the target calmly | Your finger hovers over close, every second |
| A stop being approached | You watch it | You start inventing reasons to move it |
| Doing nothing | Easy | Feels like falling behind |
| After the session | You close the laptop | You check the phone at dinner |

This is not weakness, it is [loss aversion](/learn/m08-trading-psychology/04-cognitive-biases) behaving exactly as the research says it will: losses are felt roughly twice as strongly as equivalent gains. It is also why the classic failure mode of a newly live trader is not wild gambling. It is **cutting winners early and letting losers run**, which is precisely the reverse of the plan, and it will show up in your journal as an average win that has quietly shrunk.

Expect your numbers to get worse before they get better. Most traders see their realised reward-to-risk fall in their first live month even with an identical process. Plan for that dip, and do not respond to it by changing the strategy. Respond to it by keeping the size small enough that the fear is manageable.

There is a second, less emotional gap: **costs and fills**. Simulated fills are optimistic. In the real market you pay the spread, you may get [slippage](/learn/m06-orders-and-execution/07-slippage-and-liquidity) on stops in fast conditions, you sit in a queue for limit orders that a simulator fills instantly, and commissions come off every trade. A strategy with a small edge on paper can be a breakeven strategy after costs, which is another argument for starting tiny while you measure the real numbers.

## Your first real size

Two rules govern the first account, and they are not negotiable.

<Callout type="danger" title="Money you can lose entirely">

Fund the account with an amount you could lose completely without changing anything about your life. Not "would rather not lose". **Could lose, entirely, and still pay every bill.**

That means no borrowed money, no credit card, no overdraft, no rent, no emergency fund, and nothing earmarked for anything in the next five years. If losing it would create a problem, the fear it generates will make you trade badly, which makes losing it more likely. The rule protects your trading as much as your finances.

</Callout>

The second rule is about size rather than the total. **Trade a fraction of your simulated size**, somewhere between a quarter and a tenth of it, and there is nothing wrong with going smaller still.

<Callout type="math" title="A worked first size">

Suppose you practised on a $20,000 simulated account risking 1% per trade, which is $200 of risk on each.

Going live, you fund $2,000 of genuinely spare money and risk **0.5%** per trade, which is **$10 a trade**.

That is one twentieth of the risk you are used to, and it is meant to be. At $10 a trade, a six-loss streak costs $60, which is annoying and survivable, and it lets you find out how you behave when the money is real without that discovery being expensive.

A practical warning: at $10 of risk, commissions and spreads can be a large share of the result, and some instruments cannot be traded that small. Fractional shares and micro contracts help. If the arithmetic genuinely does not work in your market, raise the account a little rather than the risk percentage, and treat the first stretch as tuition rather than income.

</Callout>

## Scale only after 30 real trades

Now the ladder. Each rung requires the same two tests, and you never skip a rung because a week went well.

| Stage | Risk per trade | Move up only when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First live stage | 0.5% | 30 real trades done, expectancy still positive, rules followed 95% of the time |
| Second stage | 0.75% | Another 30 trades meeting the same two tests |
| Third stage | 1.0% | Another 30 trades meeting the same two tests |
| Beyond | Add capital, not risk percentage | The process holds at 1% for a long stretch |

Thirty trades is not a statistical proof, it is a behavioural one. It is long enough to include a losing streak, a big winner and at least one boring week, which is the full emotional range in miniature.

The rule also works in reverse, and this is the part people leave out. **If expectancy turns negative or rule-following falls below 95%, step back down a rung.** Halve the size, fix the habit through the [weekly review](/learn/m12-becoming-consistent/03-review-and-improve), and earn the rung back. Traders who only ever climb eventually climb into a size they cannot handle, at the exact moment their discipline is slipping.

And there is no shame in returning to the simulator for a while. It is free, it is always open, and an idea tested there costs nothing but time.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Graduate only with 100 simulated trades, positive expectancy sustained across three months, at least 95% rule-following, a written plan and an automatic journal habit.
- Discipline degrades under pressure rather than improving, so rules you break on a simulator will be broken more often with real money.
- Real money engages loss aversion, and the classic first-month failure is cutting winners early and letting losers run.
- Fund only with money you could lose entirely without changing your life: no borrowed funds, rent or emergency savings.
- Start at a quarter to a tenth of your simulated risk or less, for example $10 a trade after practising with $200.
- Scale one rung at a time after 30 real trades that keep expectancy positive and rule-following above 95%, and step back down if either slips.
</KeyTakeaways>
