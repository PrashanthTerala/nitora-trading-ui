Picture two kids arm-wrestling. They strain for a full minute, faces red, and when the bell rings their hands are exactly where they started. Nobody won. But something important happened: the kid who had been winning every round all afternoon just failed to win this one.

That is a **doji**. It is a candle where the open and the close are almost the same price, so the body shrinks to a thin line. The wicks can be short or long, but the body is nearly nothing. It is the market saying: for this session, the two sides fought to a draw.

On its own, a draw means indecision. But a draw that comes after six rounds of one side winning is news. That is why the doji family, four shapes with the same tiny body, is the first pattern group you should learn. They are also the most misread, because beginners treat every doji as a reversal, and most of them are not.

## What makes a doji a doji

The rule is simple: the body must be very small compared with the full range of the candle. A common working definition is that the body is less than 5% of the distance from high to low. Some traders allow up to 10%. The exact number matters less than the picture: the body should look like a line, not a box.

Because the body is defined relative to the range, a doji on a volatile stock can have a body of 50 cents and a doji on a quiet one can have a body of 2 cents. Always compare the body to the wicks of the same candle, not to a fixed dollar amount.

## The psychology of a draw

Think back to [why prices move](/learn/m00-what-is-a-market/03-why-prices-move): more eager buyers than sellers pushes price up, and the reverse pushes it down. During a trend, one side is clearly more eager every day. A doji says that, for one session, the eagerness balanced out.

In a downtrend, the sellers had been in charge. A doji means they pushed, buyers pushed back, and the close ended up where the open was. The sellers did not lose, but for the first time they did not win either. Momentum has stalled. In an uptrend the mirror applies: buyers had been winning, and now they could not lift the close.

That is the whole meaning: **a doji marks a pause in momentum**. Whether the pause turns into a reversal depends on what happens next.

<Callout type="eli5">

A doji is like a car that has stopped at a junction. It was driving one way, and now it is sitting still. It might turn around, or it might carry on. The doji tells you the car stopped. The next candle tells you which way it goes.

</Callout>

## The four flavours

The family has four members, told apart by where the tiny body sits and how long the wicks are. Each tells a slightly different story.

### Standard doji

Short-to-medium wicks on both sides, body in the middle. Pure indecision. This is the least informative member of the family because it does not tell you who tried hardest. It matters most when it follows a run of strong candles in one direction.

### Long-legged doji

Same tiny body, but the wicks are enormous on both sides. Price ranged widely during the session, swung hard up and hard down, and still closed where it opened. This is not calm indecision; it is violent indecision. Long-legged dojis often appear at the end of sharp moves when a crowd of latecomers piles in and a crowd of early traders cashes out at the same time.

<PatternFigure name="long-legged-doji" caption="Huge wicks both ways, no body, after a run-up. Buyers reached far higher and sellers reached far lower during the session, and neither could hold it." />

### Dragonfly doji

The open, close and high are all at (or very near) the top of the candle, and there is a long lower wick. The story: sellers drove price sharply down during the session, then buyers bought every share on offer and drove it all the way back to the open. It looks like a hammer with no body, and it means the same thing: a bullish rejection of lower prices. It matters after a decline.

<PatternFigure name="dragonfly-doji" caption="Long lower wick, open and close at the top. Sellers won the first half of the session and lost all of it back. Bullish when it appears after a decline and the next candle closes higher." />

### Gravestone doji

The mirror image. Open, close and low sit at the bottom, and there is a long upper wick. Buyers pushed price up hard, and sellers took all of it back. It looks like a shooting star with no body and it is a bearish rejection of higher prices. It matters after a rise.

<PatternFigure name="gravestone-doji" caption="Long upper wick, open and close at the bottom. Buyers tried to break higher and were completely rejected. The red candle after it confirms." />

## Where a doji is valid

A doji is only interesting when there is momentum to pause. That means:

- A doji after a clear decline is a **possible bullish reversal**. Dragonfly is the strongest version.
- A doji after a clear rise is a **possible bearish reversal**. Gravestone is the strongest version.
- A doji inside a sideways range is **noise**. In a range, many candles are small and indecisive, so a doji says nothing new.
- A doji at a <Term id="support">support</Term> or <Term id="resistance">resistance</Term> level, after a trend, is the highest-quality version.

One more subtlety from Steve Nison, who brought candlesticks to the West: dojis at the top of an uptrend tend to be more reliable than dojis at the bottom of a downtrend. Markets can fall on their own weight, so a bottom needs actual buying to hold, while a top only needs buyers to stop buying.

## Confirmation and a trade idea

Never trade the doji candle itself. Wait for the next candle to close. For a bullish doji after a decline, you want the next candle to close above the doji's high. For a bearish doji after a rise, you want a close below the doji's low. Volume rising on the confirmation candle is a bonus.

A typical entry plan, for practice only: enter when the confirmation candle closes, place the stop just beyond the far wick of the doji (below the low for bullish, above the high for bearish), and aim for a target at the next obvious level. The far wick is your line in the sand: if price goes back through it, the draw was resolved the other way and you were wrong.

<Callout type="warning" title="Dojis are everywhere">

On low-volume instruments and small timeframes, dojis show up constantly because there is simply not enough trading to build a body. A one-minute chart of a quiet stock can print a dozen a day. None of them mean anything. Only count a doji when it follows a run of strong, clearly directional candles.

</Callout>

## Honest reliability

Bulkowski's tests are humbling here. The standard doji is close to a coin flip as a reversal signal, and in some studies it acts as a continuation almost as often as a reversal. The dragonfly and gravestone do a little better, roughly 50% to 57% in the predicted direction after confirmation, but the gravestone in particular reverses less often than its scary name suggests.

The long-legged doji is best treated as a volatility warning rather than a directional call. What all four do reliably is tell you momentum has paused. Trade the pause with confirmation, at a level, with a stop, and the odds are workable. Trade the shape alone and you are flipping coins.

<KeyTakeaways>
- A doji has a body that is tiny compared with its range, usually under 5% to 10% of high-to-low.
- It means the session ended in a draw, which only matters after a run of one-sided candles.
- Dragonfly (long lower wick) is a bullish rejection; gravestone (long upper wick) is a bearish rejection.
- Long-legged dojis show violent indecision and often appear at the end of sharp moves.
- Wait for the next candle to close beyond the doji before acting, and stop beyond the far wick.
- On their own, dojis are near coin-flips; context and confirmation are what give them value.
</KeyTakeaways>
