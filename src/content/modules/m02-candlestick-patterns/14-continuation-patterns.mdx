Everything so far in this module has been about turning points: the moment a trend stalls, cracks or reverses. But most of the time, trends do not reverse. They rest. A runner slows to a jog for a few hundred metres, catches their breath, and then picks the pace back up. If you mistook every jog for the end of the race, you would be wrong most of the time.

**Continuation patterns** are the candlestick shapes that mark a rest inside a trend. They tell you: the pause you are seeing is probably just a pause, and the trend is likely to resume. This lesson covers the four main families: the **three methods**, **tasuki gaps**, **windows** and the **mat hold**.

These are less famous than hammers and stars, and beginners often ignore them. That is a mistake, because a trader who can tell a pause from a reversal has a huge advantage: they stay in winning trades longer and avoid fading trends that are merely resting.

## Rising three methods: exact rules

- The market is in an **uptrend**.
- **Candle one** is a large green candle.
- **Candles two, three and four** are **small candles**, usually red, that **stay inside the range of candle one** (between its high and low). They drift lower but do not break candle one's low.
- **Candle five** is a large green candle that **closes above candle one's close**, ideally at a new high.

<PatternFigure name="rising-three-methods" caption="A big green candle, three small red candles that stay inside its range, then a big green candle to new highs. The trend caught its breath and carried on." />

### The psychology

Day one is a strong advance. Then, for three sessions, sellers try to push back and achieve almost nothing: small bodies, all contained within the big green candle. The buyers who drove day one are not selling; they are holding, and the sellers cannot even get price below day one's low. On day five the buyers step back in and the advance resumes at a new high. The pullback was shallow, short and completely absorbed.

<Callout type="eli5">

Imagine a kid climbing a rope. A big pull up (day one), then three little slips as their hands adjust (days two to four), but they never slide below where the last big pull got them. Then another big pull to a new height (day five). The slips were just grip changes, not falling.

</Callout>

The **falling three methods** is the mirror: a big red candle, three small (usually green) candles that stay inside its range, then a big red candle to a new low. The bounce was weak and contained; the decline resumed.

<PatternFigure name="falling-three-methods" caption="A big red candle, a weak three-candle bounce that stays inside its range, then a new low. Sellers held their ground through the bounce." />

The count of three is traditional, not a law. Two or four small candles that stay inside the range still carry the same meaning. What matters is that the pullback candles are small and contained.

## Tasuki gaps: exact rules

The **upside tasuki gap** is a three-candle continuation built around a gap:

- The market is in an **uptrend**.
- **Candles one and two** are both green, with a **gap between them**: candle two opens above candle one's high.
- **Candle three** is red, **opens inside candle two's body**, and **closes inside the gap** without filling it. That is, its close stays above candle one's high.

<PatternFigure name="upside-tasuki-gap" caption="Two green candles with a gap between them, then a red candle that dips into the gap but does not close it. The gap held, so the trend is expected to resume." />

The psychology: the gap up showed strong demand. The red candle is profit-taking; some early buyers sell. But the selling runs out before the gap fills, which means new buyers are stepping in inside the gap zone. That zone has become <Term id="support">support</Term> within a single session. The pattern says the trend is intact.

The **downside tasuki gap** mirrors it: two red candles with a gap down between them, then a green candle that bounces into the gap but fails to close it. The gap held as <Term id="resistance">resistance</Term>.

<PatternFigure name="downside-tasuki-gap" caption="The bearish mirror: a gap down, then a bounce that enters the gap but cannot close it. The trend is expected to continue lower." />

Tasuki gaps are stock-market patterns for the same reason as the gapped stars: they need a genuine gap, which 24-hour markets rarely provide.

## Windows: the gap as a level

Japanese traders call a gap a **window**. A **rising window** is a gap up between two candles in an uptrend; a **falling window** is a gap down in a downtrend. The pattern is just the gap, and the lesson is what happens to it later.

<PatternFigure name="rising-window" caption="A gap up between two green candles. The empty space is the window. It often acts as support on later pullbacks." />

The rule of thumb in Japanese candlestick tradition is that **a window becomes a level**. After a rising window, price tends to find support when it pulls back into the gap zone. After a falling window, the gap zone tends to act as resistance on bounces. The tasuki gap above is really just the first test of a window, one session later.

<PatternFigure name="falling-window" caption="A gap down between two red candles. The window often becomes resistance when price bounces back up into it." />

Why does this work? Because the gap zone contains no trades. Nobody bought or sold there, so nobody has a position to defend at those prices. When price returns, the first thing that happens is that traders who missed the original move see their second chance and step in. That is why windows tend to hold on the first test, and why a window that fully closes is a warning that the trend's energy has faded.

Windows are not a signal to enter on their own. They are a level to plan around. A pullback into a rising window that produces a hammer or an engulfing candle is where the entry lives.

## Mat hold: the strong cousin of three methods

The **bullish mat hold** looks like a rising three methods with one difference: the pullback starts with a **gap up**, and the small candles stay higher, usually not falling back into the body of candle one at all.

- The market is in an **uptrend**.
- **Candle one** is a large green candle.
- **Candle two** gaps up but closes lower (a small red candle above candle one).
- **Candles three and four** drift lower with small bodies, but stay mostly above the midpoint of candle one and do not break its low.
- **Candle five** is a large green candle closing at a new high.

<PatternFigure name="mat-hold-bullish" caption="Like rising three methods, but the pullback begins with a gap up and stays high. Sellers could not even get price back into the first candle. Even stronger continuation." />

The pullback is shallower than in the three methods, which is the whole point: sellers had even less success. The **bearish mat hold** is the mirror, with a gap down starting a weak bounce that stays low.

<PatternFigure name="mat-hold-bearish" caption="The bearish mirror: a gap down starts a weak bounce that never gets back into the first red candle, then a new low." />

## Where continuation patterns are valid

Every pattern here needs an **established trend** before it. That sounds obvious, but the failure mode is real: in a sideways range, a big candle followed by three small ones followed by another big candle happens all the time, and it is not rising three methods, because there is no trend to continue.

The best versions form early in a trend, not late. A rising three methods after a fresh breakout is a strong sign that the breakout has staying power. The same pattern after a six-month rally is still valid but more likely to be one of the trend's last rests.

Volume tells a useful story. In a healthy rising three methods, volume is high on candle one, dries up during the three small candles (no eagerness to sell), and comes back on candle five. If volume is heavy on the pullback candles, sellers are more serious than the small bodies suggest.

## Confirmation and trade ideas

For the three methods and mat hold, the fifth candle closing at a new high (or low) is the confirmation. Entering at its close means buying after the resumption; some traders prefer to enter during the pullback, on the third small candle, with a stop below candle one's low, accepting that the pattern is not complete yet in exchange for a better price.

Rising three methods or bullish mat hold: enter on the fifth candle's close or a small pullback after it. Stop below the low of the pullback candles (or below candle one's low for more room). Target the next resistance or the height of candle one added to the breakout.

Tasuki gap: enter on the candle after the red candle if it closes above the red candle's high. Stop below the gap's lower edge (candle one's high). If the gap fills, the pattern has failed.

Window: no entry on its own. Wait for a pullback into the window that shows a reversal candle, then enter with a stop below the window.

<Callout type="warning" title="A pause can become a top">

Continuation patterns fail in a specific way: the pullback keeps going. Three small red candles become five, then one of them breaks candle one's low, and the "rest" turns out to be a reversal. That is why the stop below the pattern's low matters. Do not talk yourself into holding through a broken pattern because it looked like a textbook three methods. The textbook version ends with a new high; if the new high never comes, it was not the textbook version.

</Callout>

## Honest reliability

Continuation patterns are less studied than reversals, and the numbers are mixed. Bulkowski's data has the rising three methods continuing the uptrend about 74% of the time and the falling three methods continuing the downtrend around 71%, which is good. The mat hold is rare and tests around 78% bullish, but the sample is tiny. The tasuki gaps are close to coin flips in his sample, roughly 54% to 57%, and windows are treated as levels rather than signals, so they are not scored the same way.

The takeaway: the three methods and the mat hold are genuinely useful for telling a pause from a reversal. The tasuki gaps and windows are best used as levels, especially the gap zone as support or resistance, rather than as reasons to enter on their own.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Continuation patterns mark a rest inside a trend, not a reversal; most trends rest several times before they end.
- Rising and falling three methods: a big candle, two to four small contained candles, then a big candle to a new extreme.
- Tasuki gaps: two candles with a gap, then a counter-candle that enters the gap but does not close it.
- A window (gap) tends to become support or resistance; a fully filled window is a warning.
- The mat hold is a three methods whose pullback starts with a gap and stays shallow, an even stronger continuation.
- Three methods and mat hold test around 70% to 78%; tasuki gaps are near coin flips; all need an established trend and a stop beyond the pattern low.
</KeyTakeaways>
