You now know about forty candlestick patterns. That is more than most people who trade for a living. It is also, right now, the most dangerous thing you know, because a head full of patterns and no framework for trusting them turns every chart into a hall of mirrors.

This lesson is the framework. It puts the reliability numbers you have been reading side by side, explains where they come from and what they leave out, and gives you the one rule that keeps pattern traders solvent: **confirmation, at a level, with a stop, or no trade.** Then it deals with the disease every pattern student catches, pattern-hunting, and how to cure it.

Nothing here is exciting. That is the point. The traders who lose money with candlesticks are the excited ones.

## Where the numbers come from

Most of the percentages in this module trace back to Thomas Bulkowski, who tested candlestick patterns across thousands of stocks over many years and published the results in his *Encyclopedia of Candlestick Charts*. His method was mechanical: define each pattern with strict rules, require a clear prior trend, find every instance in the data, and record whether price moved the predicted way afterwards.

Three things about that method matter for you. First, **he required a prior trend**, which means the numbers do not apply to patterns found inside ranges. Second, **"worked" means price moved in the predicted direction**, not that it moved far or that a trade would have been profitable after costs. Third, **his data is daily US stocks**; patterns in forex, crypto and intraday charts behave differently, usually worse, and gapped patterns barely exist there.

Other researchers, using different rules and markets, often get lower numbers than Bulkowski, sometimes a good deal lower. When you see a range in the table below, that spread is real uncertainty, not fussiness.

## Why the numbers are still worth something

A pattern at 55% sounds useless until you remember two things.

First, **you do not trade the pattern alone.** You trade the pattern at a level, after a trend, with confirmation. Each of those filters removes a slice of the losers. Nobody has clean statistics for "hammer at support after a decline with a confirming close", but every serious practitioner reports that stacked conditions beat raw shapes, and the logic is sound: you are demanding more evidence before acting.

Second, **the risk module will show you that win rate is only half of the equation.** A 55% signal where you risk $1 to make $2 is a strong business. A 75% signal where you risk $2 to make $1 loses money. The stop placement you learned in every lesson here, just beyond the pattern's extreme, is what lets you define that risk. Reliability tells you how often you are right; risk management decides whether being right that often pays.

## The confirmation rule, stated once and for all

For every pattern in this module, the trade is not the pattern. The trade is:

1. **Trend.** Is there a move for this pattern to reverse or continue? If you cannot point to it, stop.
2. **Level.** Does the pattern sit at a <Term id="support">support</Term> or <Term id="resistance">resistance</Term> zone, a prior swing point, a window, a round number? If not, the pattern is worth less than half of its table value.
3. **Confirmation.** Has the next candle closed in the predicted direction? For stars and three-candle patterns the third candle is the confirmation. For everything else, wait.
4. **Stop.** Is there a clear price beyond the pattern where you will admit you were wrong? If the stop is so far away that the position would have to be tiny, that is information too.

If any step fails, there is no trade. Not a smaller trade, not a "watch it" trade with real money. No trade.

<Callout type="danger" title="The pattern-hunting disease">

Here is how it goes. You learn twenty patterns. You open a chart. Your brain, which evolved to spot faces in clouds, finds a hammer, then a harami, then an evening star, then another hammer. Each one feels like a discovery. You trade three of them. Two lose. You look back and notice that none of them had a trend behind them, none were at a level, and you entered on the pattern candle rather than the confirmation. You have not been trading candlesticks. You have been trading the feeling of recognising something.

</Callout>

## Curing pattern-hunting

The cure is a set of habits, not more knowledge.

**Start from the level, not the pattern.** Mark the levels on the chart first (the next module teaches how). Then ask whether a pattern has formed at one of them. Patterns found this way are the only ones that count. Patterns found by scanning the whole chart are mostly noise.

**Say the story out loud.** For every pattern you think you see, describe who won the session and why it matters here. If you cannot tell the story, or the story is "it looks like a hammer", you do not have a pattern.

**Count the conditions.** Trend, level, confirmation, stop. Four out of four is a trade. Three is a note in the journal. Fewer is nothing.

**Keep score.** Write down every pattern you trade, the conditions it met, and the outcome. After fifty trades you will have your own reliability table, for your market and timeframe, which is worth more than any published one.

**Use the trainer for recognition and the simulator for judgement.** The [pattern trainer](/trainer) makes shape recognition automatic so you stop spending brainpower on it. The simulator is where you practise the harder skill: deciding whether the shape you recognised is in a place where it means something.

## What patterns are good for

After all the caveats, here is what candlestick patterns genuinely do well. They tell you, in one or two candles, that the balance between buyers and sellers has shifted for a moment. That is a fast, cheap piece of information about crowd psychology, and it is real: the wicks and bodies are records of actual trades.

Used as a timing tool at levels you already care about, they help you enter closer to a turn than you otherwise could, with a stop that has a reason behind it. That is a modest, honest edge. It is not a system by itself, and anyone who tells you a shape alone is a system is selling something.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Most candlestick patterns predict direction 50% to 65% of the time on their own; a few three-candle patterns reach the 70s.
- Published statistics assume a prior trend, count direction only, and come mostly from daily US stocks; other markets usually do worse.
- The trade is never the pattern alone: trend, level, confirmation and a stop are all required, or there is no trade.
- Win rate is only half the equation; risk to reward decides whether a 55% pattern is a business or a leak.
- Pattern-hunting is finding shapes everywhere and trading the feeling of recognition; the cure is starting from levels and keeping score.
- Patterns are a timing tool at levels that already matter, a modest and real edge, not a system.
</KeyTakeaways>
