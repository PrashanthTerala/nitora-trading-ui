Take the hammer from the last lesson and flip it upside down. Now the small body sits at the bottom and the long wick points up. Once again you get two patterns from one shape: the **shooting star**, which appears after a rise and is bearish, and the **inverted hammer**, which appears after a decline and is (cautiously) bullish.

The upside-down shape tells an upside-down story. A long lower wick meant sellers pushed down and were beaten back. A long upper wick means buyers pushed up and were beaten back. Somebody reached for higher prices during the session and the market said no.

Whether that "no" is bad news or, strangely, good news depends on who was doing the reaching. That is the twist that makes the inverted hammer one of the most misunderstood candles in the book.

## What the shape looks like

Both candles share the same rules:

- **Small body** near the bottom of the range. Colour is secondary, though a red body helps the shooting star and a green body helps the inverted hammer.
- **Long upper wick**, at least twice the body. Three times is better.
- **Little or no lower wick.**

The same thumb test works: cover the bottom of the candle and check that at least two-thirds of what remains is wick.

## The shooting star: buyers reach and get slapped

Picture a stock that has risen for a week. Buyers are confident. On the next day the price opens and rockets higher; the late buyers, the ones who read about the rally over the weekend, are piling in. Then the sellers arrive. Price collapses from the high all the way back down to close near the open.

<PatternFigure name="shooting-star" caption="After a rise, buyers pushed far higher during the session and sellers drove price all the way back. The small body at the bottom means the buyers ended the day with nothing. The red candle after it confirms." />

Everyone who bought in that upper wick is now losing money. The rally that looked unstoppable in the morning has been rejected by the afternoon. That is why a shooting star after a rise is a **bearish reversal** candidate, and one of the better ones, because the session ended with the trend actually losing, unlike the hanging man.

<Callout type="eli5">

Think of a kid jumping to touch a high branch. They jump, their fingertips brush it, and they land right back where they started. The long upper wick is the jump. The small body at the bottom is where they landed. They tried to go higher and could not hold it.

</Callout>

## The inverted hammer: sellers test the ceiling and fail

Now put the identical candle at the bottom of a decline. Price has been falling for a week. On this day, it opens, rallies sharply, and then sinks back to close near the open. On the surface, this is a failed rally, which sounds bearish.

But look closer. Sellers have been winning every day. For the first time, somebody was able to push price a long way up during the session. Yes, the sellers pushed it back, but they only managed to get it back to where it opened, not to new lows. **The sellers are no longer able to make new lows.** That is the crack in the downtrend, and it is why the inverted hammer is a cautious bullish signal.

<PatternFigure name="inverted-hammer" caption="After a decline, buyers tested higher prices and were pushed back, but sellers could not make a new low. On its own this is weak. The green confirmation candle is what makes it count." />

The inverted hammer is the weakest of the four hammer-family candles, for the same reason the hanging man is weak: the session ended with the old trend still in control. Buyers tried and failed. What makes it interesting is only that the sellers also failed. This is a pattern that is nearly meaningless without confirmation.

## Where each is valid

| Pattern | Requires | Signals | Strength |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Shooting star | A clear prior rise | Possible bearish reversal | Moderate; the trend lost the session |
| Inverted hammer | A clear prior decline | Possible bullish reversal | Weak; needs strong confirmation |

A shooting star at a <Term id="resistance">resistance</Term> level, where the upper wick pokes above the level and closes back under it, is the classic version and by far the most useful. It shows that the level was tested and rejected in a single session. An inverted hammer at <Term id="support">support</Term> is likewise better than one in open space.

A shooting star that gaps up from the previous candle, so that the entire candle sits above the prior close, is stronger still. The gap shows the excitement that pulled in the last buyers; the collapse shows them being trapped. You met [gaps](/learn/m01-reading-price/07-gaps) in the previous module.

## Confirmation

For a shooting star: the next candle should close below the star's body, and ideally below its low. A gap down at the next open is strong confirmation. For an inverted hammer: the next candle should close above the inverted hammer's body, and ideally above its high. A gap up is strong confirmation.

The inverted hammer really is not a signal until that confirmation candle prints. Treat the inverted hammer as a reason to watch, and the confirmation as the reason to act. Rising volume on the confirmation adds weight to both patterns.

## A trade idea to practise

Shooting star: enter short when the confirmation candle closes below the star's low. Stop above the star's high, with a small buffer. The star's high is the price that buyers reached for and could not hold; if price goes back through it, the rejection has failed. Target the next support or a fixed multiple of your risk.

Inverted hammer: enter long when the confirmation candle closes above the inverted hammer's high. Stop below the inverted hammer's low. Target the next resistance.

<Callout type="warning" title="Upper wicks in strong uptrends">

In a powerful uptrend, price often prints long upper wicks that look like shooting stars and then simply keeps rising the next day. Strong trends shake off single-candle warnings constantly. A shooting star is a much better signal after an extended, tiring rally, or at a level, than in the middle of a fresh, high-momentum move. When in doubt, wait for the confirmation candle, and if it does not come, the star was noise.

</Callout>

## Honest reliability

Bulkowski's numbers put the shooting star at roughly 59% as a bearish reversal after confirmation, which is respectable for a single candle. The inverted hammer is a genuine oddity: in his tests it acted as a bearish *continuation* about 65% of the time, meaning the decline resumed more often than it reversed. That is why its bullish reputation is so misleading and why the confirmation candle is not optional.

The practical takeaway: a shooting star at resistance, confirmed, is worth your attention. An inverted hammer is a note in the margin that says "watch this", and only becomes a trade if the next candle proves buyers meant it.
