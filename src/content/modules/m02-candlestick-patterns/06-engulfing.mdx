Imagine a playground argument. Yesterday, one kid said something small and mean. Today, the other kid answers with a speech so loud and so complete that it covers everything said yesterday and then some. Whatever was said before has been swallowed. The crowd's attention has flipped.

That is an **engulfing pattern**: two candles where the second candle's body completely covers the first candle's body, in the opposite colour. A bullish engulfing is a small red candle followed by a big green one that swallows it. A bearish engulfing is a small green candle followed by a big red one.

It is the most popular two-candle pattern on the planet, and for good reason: it is easy to spot, it has a clear story, and it tests better than most. It is also the pattern that beginners misuse the most, because they see the shape and forget to ask where it appeared.

## What it looks like: the exact rules

- **Candle one** has a body of any size, but the pattern is more meaningful when it is small. Its colour matches the prior trend (red in a downtrend for the bullish version, green in an uptrend for the bearish version).
- **Candle two** is the opposite colour and its **body completely covers candle one's body**. The open of candle two is beyond candle one's close, and the close of candle two is beyond candle one's open.
- Wicks do not need to be engulfed under the classic Japanese definition, only bodies. Some Western traders require the whole range to be covered; that stricter version is rarer and a little stronger.
- Candle two should be **large** relative to recent candles. A tiny candle engulfing an even tinier one is technically valid and practically meaningless.

## The psychology of the bullish engulfing

Walk through the two days. On day one, in a downtrend, sellers win again, but only by a little; the body is small. Enthusiasm for selling is fading. On day two, price opens even lower, which looks like more of the same. Then buyers arrive in force. They do not just recover the day's drop; they push price above where yesterday started and keep going.

By the close, every seller from yesterday and everyone who sold at today's open is losing money. That is a full reversal of control within two sessions. The size of the green body is the measure of how decisive the shift was.

<Callout type="eli5">

Picture a sandcastle that took a week to build, being knocked down one wall at a time. Then a wave comes in that is bigger than all the little kicks combined and washes the whole thing flat. Yesterday's little kick is gone. The engulfing candle is that wave.

</Callout>

## The bearish engulfing

The mirror image, at the top of a rise. Day one is a small green candle: buyers still winning, but barely. Day two opens higher, which pulls in the last optimists, and then sellers take over and drive the close below yesterday's open. Everyone who bought in the last two sessions is now underwater.

<PatternFigure name="bearish-engulfing" caption="At the top of a rise, a small green candle is swallowed by a large red body. The open above yesterday's close trapped late buyers; the close below yesterday's open means they are all losing." />

## Where it is valid

An engulfing pattern is a reversal pattern and needs a trend to reverse. Bullish engulfing needs a decline before it; bearish engulfing needs a rise. This is not a formality. In a sideways range, candles engulf each other constantly because price is oscillating, and none of those engulfings mean anything.

The best locations, in order:

1. At a clear <Term id="support">support</Term> or <Term id="resistance">resistance</Term> level, after a trend.
2. After an extended trend that has started to slow (smaller bodies, spinning tops) before the pattern.
3. After a trend, in open space, with high volume on the engulfing candle.

Some traders also count how many prior candles the engulfing body covers. A green candle that engulfs the last three or four red bodies is stronger than one that only covers yesterday, because more sellers are trapped.

## Confirmation

The classic confirmation is a third candle that closes beyond the engulfing candle's close: higher for bullish, lower for bearish. That is exactly the follow-through candle shown in both figures. When you add that third candle formally, the pattern gets a new name, three outside up or down, which you will meet in lesson 12.

[Volume](/learn/m01-reading-price/06-volume) matters more for engulfing than for most patterns. A bullish engulfing on the highest volume in two weeks means a genuine change of hands at the low. On thin volume, a big candle can be one large order rather than a shift in the crowd.

Higher timeframes matter too. A daily engulfing carries the weight of a whole day of trading by everyone who watches the stock. A one-minute engulfing can be a single order.

## A trade idea to practise

Bullish engulfing: enter when the confirmation candle closes above the engulfing candle's high, or, for a more aggressive version, at the open after the engulfing candle closes. Stop below the low of the engulfing candle (or the lower of the two candles). Target the next resistance or a fixed multiple of your risk.

Bearish engulfing: enter short when the confirmation candle closes below the engulfing candle's low. Stop above the high of the pattern. Target the next support.

The stop logic: the engulfing candle's extreme is where the winning side proved control. If price goes back through it, control has been lost again and the pattern has failed.

<Callout type="danger" title="The engulfing candle that is already the move">

A very common trap: the engulfing candle is enormous, say three times the average range, and you buy at its close. Your stop must go below its low, which is far away, so your risk is huge. Meanwhile most of the move you hoped for may have already happened inside that candle. Big engulfing candles are strong signals and terrible entries. Either wait for a pullback into the candle's body, or accept a smaller position because the stop is wide.

</Callout>

## Honest reliability

Engulfing patterns are among the better-tested candlesticks. In Bulkowski's stock data, the bullish engulfing reverses the downtrend around 63% of the time, and the bearish engulfing reverses the uptrend around 79%, which is unusually high, though later studies with different rules land closer to 60%. Treat the bearish engulfing as one of the top-tier candle signals and the bullish engulfing as solidly above a coin flip.

Two cautions. First, those numbers count patterns that had a proper prior trend; engulfings inside ranges were excluded, and in real trading you will be tempted to count them. Second, "reversal" in those studies means price moved the predicted way afterwards, not that it moved far. An engulfing pattern is a good reason to expect a turn, not a promise of how big the turn will be.

<KeyTakeaways>
- An engulfing pattern is two candles: the second is the opposite colour and its body fully covers the first's body.
- Bullish engulfing after a decline shows buyers absorbed all selling and trapped recent sellers; bearish engulfing after a rise is the mirror.
- It needs a prior trend; engulfing candles inside ranges are constant and meaningless.
- Confirm with a third candle closing beyond the engulfing candle, and look for high volume on the engulfing candle.
- Stop goes beyond the far extreme of the engulfing candle; very large engulfing candles mean wide stops and poor entries.
- Bearish engulfing tests among the best candle patterns (60% to 79%); bullish engulfing sits around 63%.
</KeyTakeaways>
