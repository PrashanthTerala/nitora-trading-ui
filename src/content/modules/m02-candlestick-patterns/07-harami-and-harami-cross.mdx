A runner sprints down the track, stride after stride, each one as long as the last. Then one stride is tiny. She has not turned around. She has not stopped. But something changed: the effort went out of it. Anyone watching would lean forward and wonder whether she is about to pull up.

That tiny stride is a **harami**. The word is Japanese for "pregnant", because the picture is a big candle with a small candle tucked inside its body. The big candle is the sprint; the small one is the stride that lost its power. It is the engulfing pattern in reverse: instead of a big candle swallowing a small one, a small one hides inside a big one.

The harami is a **momentum-stalling** pattern, not a momentum-reversing one. That distinction is why it is weaker than the engulfing pattern, and also why traders who understand it use it as an early warning rather than a trigger.

## What it looks like: the exact rules

- **Candle one** is a large body in the direction of the prior trend: a big red candle in a downtrend (bullish harami) or a big green candle in an uptrend (bearish harami).
- **Candle two** has a small body that sits entirely **inside the body of candle one**. Its open and close are both between candle one's open and close.
- The colour of candle two is usually opposite to candle one, but not required. A small candle of either colour works; what matters is that it is small and inside.
- Wicks of candle two may poke outside candle one's body under the classic definition. Stricter traders require the whole second candle, wicks included, to sit inside the first candle's range (an "inside bar" in Western terms).

## The psychology

On day one in a downtrend, sellers post their biggest win in a while. That is a strong candle, and on its own it says the decline is healthy. On day two, price opens inside yesterday's body, higher than yesterday's close, and then goes almost nowhere. The sellers who were so dominant yesterday could not make a new low. The buyers could not do much either.

The result is a stall. Momentum that was accelerating has gone flat in a single session. This is the difference from an engulfing pattern: the engulfing shows the other side taking control, while the harami only shows the winning side losing its grip. Nobody has taken over yet.

<Callout type="eli5">

Think of a shopping trolley rolling fast down a slope. An engulfing pattern is someone grabbing it and shoving it back up the hill. A harami is the trolley hitting a patch of thick grass and slowing to a crawl. It has not gone back up, but it is no longer racing down, and a small push could turn it.

</Callout>

## The bearish harami

The mirror: at the top of a rise, a big green candle is followed by a small candle inside its body. Buyers who were charging ahead yesterday could not push price to a new high today. The rally has stalled.

## The harami cross: the stall is total

When the second candle is a <Term id="doji">doji</Term> rather than a small body, the pattern is called a **harami cross**. The stall is not just partial; the second session ended in a perfect draw. Because a doji shows deeper indecision than a spinning top, the harami cross is considered a stronger version of the same signal.

<PatternFigure name="harami-cross" caption="A bullish harami where the inside candle is a doji. The sellers' big win was followed by a session where nobody could move price at all." />

Everything in this lesson about context and confirmation applies to the harami cross too. It is stronger than a plain harami, but it is still a stall, not a reversal, until the next candle proves it.

## Where it is valid

Like every reversal pattern, the harami needs a <Term id="trend">trend</Term>. A bullish harami needs a clear prior decline; a bearish harami needs a clear prior rise. Inside a sideways range, small candles inside big ones happen all the time and mean nothing.

The strongest haramis form:

- After a long, extended trend where the first candle is unusually big, because the bigger the sprint, the more meaningful the tiny step.
- At a <Term id="support">support</Term> level (bullish) or <Term id="resistance">resistance</Term> level (bearish).
- When the second candle is a doji (the harami cross).

## Confirmation

Because the harami only shows a stall, confirmation is more important here than for almost any other two-candle pattern. For a bullish harami, you want the third candle to close above the small candle's high, and ideally above the midpoint or the open of the big red candle. For a bearish harami, a close below the small candle's low, ideally below the midpoint of the big green candle.

When that third candle closes beyond the first candle's open, the pattern gets promoted to a three inside up or three inside down, which is lesson 12. That promotion is the whole reason those patterns exist: they are haramis with the confirmation built in.

## A trade idea to practise

Bullish harami: enter when the confirmation candle closes above the harami's high. Stop below the low of the big red candle, which is where sellers had their last real win. Target the next resistance or a multiple of your risk.

Bearish harami: enter short when the confirmation candle closes below the harami's low. Stop above the high of the big green candle. Target the next support.

Because the stop sits beyond the big first candle, the risk on a harami trade is often larger than on an engulfing trade. Size your position accordingly.

<Callout type="warning" title="A stall is not a turn">

The most common harami mistake is treating the small second candle as the reversal and buying (or shorting) at its close. But at that point, nothing has reversed. The trolley has only slowed. About half the time, the trend simply resumes on the next candle and you are stopped out. The harami is a reason to watch closely, and the confirmation candle is the reason to act.

</Callout>

## Honest reliability

Bulkowski's tests are blunt: the bullish harami reverses the downtrend only about 53% of the time, and the bearish harami reverses the uptrend about 53% as well. Both are close to a coin flip. The harami cross fares a little better, around 55% to 57%, consistent with the idea that a doji is a deeper stall.

That is exactly what the psychology predicts. A stall is a stall; it can resolve either way. The value of the harami is in what it tells you to *watch*, not in what it tells you to *do*. Combine it with a level and wait for the third candle, and the odds become workable. Trade it on shape alone and you are guessing.

<KeyTakeaways>
- A harami is a big candle followed by a small candle whose body sits entirely inside the big candle's body.
- It shows momentum stalling, not reversing; the winning side lost its grip but nobody has taken over.
- The harami cross, where the inside candle is a doji, is a stronger version of the same stall.
- It needs a prior trend; inside candles within a range are constant and meaningless.
- Confirmation is essential: wait for a third candle to close beyond the small candle, ideally beyond the big candle's midpoint.
- Plain haramis test near a coin flip (about 53%); the harami cross is slightly better.
</KeyTakeaways>
