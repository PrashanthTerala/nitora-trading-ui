The darkest part of the night is just before dawn. Then the sky goes grey, neither dark nor light, and then the sun comes up. Three stages: night, the in-between, day. Japanese traders named a candle pattern after that picture, and once you see it you will not forget it.

The **morning star** is three candles: a big red candle (night), a small candle that sits below it (the star, the grey in-between), and a big green candle that closes well into the first candle's body (day). The **evening star** is the mirror: a big green candle, a small star above it, and a big red candle. Dawn and dusk of a trend.

These are among the most respected reversal patterns in the candlestick vocabulary, partly because they take three sessions to form. A three-candle story is harder to fake than a one-candle story, and it gives you a built-in piece of confirmation.

## The morning star: exact rules

- The market is in a **downtrend**.
- **Candle one** is a large red candle, continuing the trend.
- **Candle two** has a **small body** (a spinning top or a doji) and its body is **below candle one's body**. Classically it gaps down, so that its body does not overlap candle one's body. In 24-hour markets the gap is often absent, and most traders accept a star that opens near candle one's close.
- **Candle three** is a large green candle that **closes well into candle one's body**, at least past the midpoint. Classically it gaps up from the star.
- The star's colour does not matter.

### The psychology

Day one is a big win for sellers; the decline looks healthy. Day two opens lower, which should be more selling, but then the session goes nowhere. The body is tiny. Sellers had every advantage and could not press it. That is the stall you learned about with the [harami](/learn/m02-candlestick-patterns/07-harami-and-harami-cross) and the doji, and it is the hinge of the whole pattern.

Day three is the resolution. Buyers arrive and drive price back up through most of day one's losses. Everyone who sold on day two or at day three's open is losing. The market has gone from confident selling, to hesitation, to confident buying, across three sessions. That sequence is what makes the pattern trustworthy.

<Callout type="eli5">

Think of a seesaw. First, a heavy kid sits on one end and it slams down (the red candle). Then a lighter kid climbs onto the other end and the seesaw wobbles, nearly balanced (the star). Then a second kid jumps on with the light one and it swings hard the other way (the green candle). The star is the wobble; without it, the seesaw would not have swung.

</Callout>

## The evening star: exact rules

- The market is in an **uptrend**.
- **Candle one** is a large green candle.
- **Candle two** is a small body sitting **above candle one's body**, classically after a gap up.
- **Candle three** is a large red candle that **closes well into candle one's body**, past the midpoint.

<PatternFigure name="evening-star" caption="At the top of a rise: a big green candle, a small star perched above it, then a big red candle closing deep into the green body. The rally that gapped up on the star now has trapped buyers overhead." />

The psychology is the mirror. Day one is a strong rally. Day two gaps up, pulling in the last enthusiastic buyers, and then goes nowhere; the buyers had the advantage and could not press it. Day three sellers take over and drive price through most of day one's gains. Everyone who bought the star is now losing. Dusk.

## Reading the star

The middle candle is where the quality of the pattern is decided:

- **The smaller the star, the better.** A doji star is the strongest (see lesson 13 for the morning doji star and the abandoned baby). A star with a medium body is a weak pattern and may not be a star at all.
- **A real gap between the star's body and its neighbours** strengthens the pattern. It shows the market lurched in one direction and then reversed, leaving the star isolated.
- **The star's colour is irrelevant.** A green star in a morning star is fine; a red star is fine.

## Reading the third candle

The third candle is the confirmation, and it is built into the pattern. That is a big reason stars test better than two-candle patterns: you cannot even call it a morning star until the third candle has closed. The deeper the third candle closes into the first candle's body, the stronger the pattern. A close above the first candle's open is exceptional.

Volume on the third candle matters. A morning star whose third candle prints heavy [volume](/learn/m01-reading-price/06-volume) is showing real buying, not a thin bounce.

## Where it is valid

Both patterns need a clear <Term id="trend">trend</Term>. The best morning stars form after an extended decline, at a <Term id="support">support</Term> level. The best evening stars form after an extended rise, at a <Term id="resistance">resistance</Term> level, especially when the star pokes above the level and the third candle closes back beneath it.

Stars are more reliable on daily and weekly charts than on intraday charts, because the gaps that make the star isolated are a product of overnight sessions. On a five-minute chart, the star will usually just be a small candle with no gaps, which is a weaker version.

## A trade idea to practise

Morning star: enter long at the close of the third candle, or on the next candle's open. Some traders wait for a fourth candle to close higher, trading a slightly worse price for more certainty. Stop below the low of the star, which is the lowest point of the pattern and the level where sellers had their last chance. Target the next resistance or a fixed multiple of your risk.

Evening star: enter short at the close of the third candle. Stop above the high of the star. Target the next support.

The stop is often a comfortable distance away because the pattern spans three sessions. Size your position to that distance rather than shrinking the stop to fit a position size you like.

<Callout type="warning" title="The lazy star">

If the middle candle has a medium-sized body, or its body overlaps the first candle's body heavily, you do not have a star; you have three ordinary candles. Beginners stretch the definition because the pattern is famous and they want to find one. The whole meaning depends on the middle session being a genuine stall. If it was not, the third candle is just a bounce.

</Callout>

## Honest reliability

The stars are among the better performers in Bulkowski's testing. The morning star reverses the downtrend about 78% of the time in his stock data, and the evening star reverses the uptrend about 72%. Those are high numbers by candlestick standards, though other researchers using different rules and markets report figures in the 60s. Part of the strength is structural: the third candle is a built-in confirmation that filters out failed stalls before they are counted.

Two things to remember. First, "reversal" means price went the predicted way afterwards, not that it went far; stars are decent at calling a turn and only average at predicting how big it is. Second, those statistics require a proper prior trend, a small star and a deep third candle. A sloppy star in a range does not inherit the good numbers.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Morning star: big red candle, small star below it, big green candle closing past the midpoint of the red body, after a decline.
- Evening star: big green candle, small star above it, big red candle closing past the midpoint of the green body, after a rise.
- The star is a stall; the third candle is the resolution and a built-in confirmation.
- Smaller stars, real gaps around the star and deep third candles make the pattern stronger.
- Stop beyond the star's extreme; enter at or after the third candle's close.
- Stars test among the best candle patterns, around 70% to 78%, but need a proper trend and a genuine stall to earn those numbers.
</KeyTakeaways>
