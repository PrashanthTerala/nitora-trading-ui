In [Piercing Line and Dark Cloud Cover](/learn/m02-candlestick-patterns/08-piercing-dark-cloud) you learned the 50% rule: a green candle that claws back more than half of the previous red body is a counterattack worth respecting. This lesson is about what happens when the green candle does not get that far.

The **on-neck line**, the **in-neck line** and the **thrusting line** are the three weak bounces. Each is a two-candle pattern in a downtrend where a green candle rallies from below the previous low but stops short: at the previous low, just inside the red body, or somewhere below its midpoint. They look like the start of a recovery and usually are not.

These patterns matter more for what they teach than for how often you will trade them. They train your eye to measure how far a bounce reached, and that single skill will stop you buying a lot of dead cats.

## The three lines: exact rules

All three share the same setup:

- The market is in a **downtrend**.
- **Candle one** is a large red candle.
- **Candle two** is green and **opens below candle one's low** (a gap down at the open, or in 24-hour markets, an open at or below the prior close and a dip below the prior low).

They differ only in **where candle two closes**:

| Pattern | Candle two closes... | Typical meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| On-neck | At or very near candle one's low | Bearish continuation |
| In-neck | Just above candle one's close, barely inside the body | Bearish continuation |
| Thrusting | Inside the body, but below the midpoint | Bearish continuation, weaker |
| Piercing line | Above the midpoint | Bullish reversal |

The neck in the name is the previous candle's low: the bounce reaches the neck and stops, or barely climbs onto it.

### On-neck line

<PatternFigure name="on-neck" caption="A weak green candle that rallies from below and closes only up to the previous candle's low. The bounce reached the neck and went no further. The decline resumes." />

The green candle opened in the hole below yesterday's low, rallied, and ran out of buyers exactly where yesterday's low sat. Sellers who were waiting at that level sold into the bounce. Nothing inside yesterday's body was recovered. This is the weakest of the three.

### In-neck line

<PatternFigure name="in-neck" caption="The green candle closes a fraction inside the red body, just above the previous close. Slightly better than on-neck, but still a bounce that barely got anywhere." />

The bounce made it a hair into yesterday's body. It is a small improvement, but in practice the difference from on-neck is a few ticks, and the meaning is the same: buyers could not do real damage.

### Thrusting line

<PatternFigure name="thrusting-line" caption="A stronger bounce that closes inside the red body but stays below the midpoint. Not enough to be a piercing line, so it counts as continuation more often than not." />

The thrusting line is the closest to a piercing line without making it. Buyers recovered part of the body but not half. Sellers still own more of yesterday's range than buyers do. Of the three, this is the one most likely to turn into a reversal, especially if two or three thrusting lines appear in a row, which some traders treat as a stall.

<Callout type="eli5">

Picture someone who fell into a pit trying to climb out. On-neck: their fingers touch the rim and they slide back. In-neck: they get one hand over the edge. Thrusting: they get their elbows over, but not their body. Piercing line: they get more than half of themselves out. Only the last one is really climbing out; the others are still in the pit.

</Callout>

## The psychology

All three tell the same story with different volume. Sellers made a big push on day one. On day two, price opened even lower, which pulled in some bargain hunters. They pushed price up, but the further they pushed, the more sellers they met, and the rally stopped short of taking back the previous session. By the close, the sellers from day one are still comfortably in profit, and the day-two buyers are holding a weak position that goes underwater the moment the decline resumes.

That last group matters. Weak-bounce buyers become forced sellers when price makes a new low, which is fuel for the continuation. The pattern does not just predict the decline continuing; it partly causes it.

## Where they are valid

These are **downtrend continuation** patterns. They need a clear decline before them and are most meaningful when the decline is not yet extended. Late in a long decline, a series of weak bounces can instead mean sellers are running out of energy, so use extra care.

There are bullish mirrors (a red candle that gaps up and closes at or just below the previous high in an uptrend), but they are rarely named and rarely discussed. The practical lesson works in both directions: a counter-candle that recovers less than half of the previous body is a weak counter, whichever way the trend runs.

## Confirmation and a trade idea

Confirmation is the next candle closing below the green candle's low, ideally at a new low for the decline. That candle also stops out the bounce buyers, which is part of why the move can accelerate.

Short entry: enter when the confirmation candle closes below candle two's low. Stop above candle two's high (the top of the bounce). Target the next support or a fixed multiple of your risk.

For most readers, the more useful application is defensive: **do not buy these bounces.** If you are looking for a long after a decline and the green candle you see recovers less than half of the previous red body, it is not a piercing line, and it is not your entry. Wait for a proper reversal pattern with a level and confirmation.

<Callout type="warning" title="The first green candle is not the bottom">

The single most common beginner mistake after a decline is buying the first green candle because "it has turned". Most first green candles are neck lines: a weak bounce into sellers who are happy to unload at a slightly better price. Measure the bounce against the previous body. If it did not recover half, the market has not turned; it has paused for breath on the way down.

</Callout>

## Honest reliability

Here the statistics agree with the tradition, but not as strongly as the names suggest. Bulkowski finds the on-neck line continuing the downtrend only about 56% of the time, and the in-neck about 53%. The thrusting line is the odd one out: in his data it acts as a bullish reversal slightly more often than as a continuation, around 57%, which contradicts the classic description. That is worth remembering: a bounce that recovers 40% of the body is not as weak as the tradition claims.

The practical conclusion: on-neck and in-neck lines are mild evidence that the decline will continue, worth respecting as a reason not to buy, but not strong enough to short on their own. The thrusting line is a genuine coin flip. Use all three as a measuring tool, not a trigger.

<KeyTakeaways>
- On-neck, in-neck and thrusting lines are weak bounces in a downtrend: a green candle that opens below the prior low and closes short of the prior body's midpoint.
- On-neck closes at the prior low, in-neck barely inside the body, thrusting inside the body but below halfway.
- Weak-bounce buyers become forced sellers when the decline resumes, which helps it continue.
- The main use is defensive: do not buy a bounce that recovers less than half the previous body.
- Short on a close below the bounce candle's low, with a stop above its high.
- On-neck and in-neck test around 53% to 56% as continuations; the thrusting line is a coin flip and slightly favours reversal.
</KeyTakeaways>
