Two kinds of arm wrestle end up in the newspaper. One is over in a second: one arm slams the other flat, no contest. The other goes on so long that both arms shake, the referee gets bored, and it ends without a winner. Everything in between is normal and forgettable.

Candles work the same way. Most are ordinary: medium body, medium wicks. But two extremes carry real information. The **marubozu** is the slam-down: a big body with no wicks, one side in total control from open to close. The **spinning top** is the shaking stalemate: a small body with wicks on both sides, nobody in control. Its bigger cousin, the **high wave**, is the stalemate where both arms nearly broke.

This lesson is about reading those extremes. They are not reversal patterns in the usual sense; they are conviction gauges. Learning them helps you read every other pattern in the module, because engulfing candles, stars and soldiers are all built out of them.

## The marubozu: total control

The word means "bald head" in Japanese, because the candle has no wicks. The rules:

- A **large body**, bigger than most recent candles.
- **No upper wick and no lower wick**, or only tiny ones. A bullish marubozu opens at its low and closes at its high; a bearish one opens at its high and closes at its low.

In practice, a perfect marubozu is rare, so traders accept wicks of a few percent of the body. If the wicks are noticeable, call it a strong candle, not a marubozu.

<PatternFigure name="marubozu-bullish" caption="Open at the low, close at the high, no wicks. Buyers were in control from the first trade to the last and sellers never got a foothold." />

The psychology is the clearest of any candle: **one side won every minute of the session.** In a bullish marubozu, the price never traded below the open (no lower wick) and closed at the very high (no upper wick). Sellers never managed to push price down, even briefly. That is not a fight; it is a rout.

<PatternFigure name="marubozu-bearish" caption="Open at the high, close at the low. Total seller control: buyers could not lift price above the open at any point." />

### Where a marubozu is valid and what it means

A marubozu is not a reversal or continuation pattern by itself. It is a **statement of momentum**, and its meaning depends on where it appears:

- **After a range or at a breakout:** a marubozu breaking out of a sideways area is strong. It shows the breakout had conviction. Traders often use the marubozu's low (for bullish) as the level that must hold.
- **In the middle of a trend:** it says the trend is healthy. Continuation is likely.
- **After a long, extended trend:** be careful. A huge marubozu at the very end of a long run can be the final exhaustion candle where the last buyers pile in. The next day's candle tells you which it was.

A bullish marubozu often becomes the first candle of the three white soldiers pattern, and a bearish marubozu the first of three black crows, which you will meet in lesson 11.

<Callout type="eli5">

A marubozu is a race where one runner leads from the starting gun to the finish line and is never overtaken, not even for a step. Everyone can see who was fastest that day. What they cannot see is whether the runner has anything left for tomorrow.

</Callout>

## The spinning top: nobody in control

The rules for a spinning top are almost the opposite:

- A **small body**, noticeably smaller than recent candles, but not so thin that it is a doji.
- **Wicks on both sides**, each roughly as long as or longer than the body.

The colour of the body is nearly irrelevant. A spinning top's story is that buyers pushed up, sellers pushed down, and the close ended near the open. Neither side could hold a gain.

<PatternFigure name="spinning-top" caption="Small body, similar wicks above and below, after a run-up. Buyers who had been winning every session could not win this one. The trend is pausing." />

The spinning top means **indecision**, exactly like the doji from [lesson 02](/learn/m02-candlestick-patterns/02-doji-family), just slightly less extreme. And exactly like the doji, its meaning depends entirely on what came before:

- After a strong trend, a spinning top says momentum is fading. Watch for a reversal, but do not assume one.
- Inside a range, spinning tops are normal and mean nothing.
- Several spinning tops in a row after a trend are a stronger warning than one.

## The high wave: violent indecision

A **high wave** candle is a spinning top with enormous wicks. The body is still small, but the range from high to low is huge, often two or three times the recent average. Price swung wildly in both directions and finished near where it started.

<PatternFigure name="high-wave" caption="A spinning top with giant wicks. Price ranged violently in both directions and closed near the open. This kind of chaos often appears near turning points." />

High waves usually appear when a crowd of latecomers and a crowd of profit-takers collide, often right around news or at the end of a fast move. They are a volatility warning first and a reversal hint second. If you see a high wave after a long rally, the market has lost its consensus, and the next few candles will be decisive.

## Confirmation and trade ideas

A marubozu does not need confirmation in the normal sense because it is not a reversal call. The useful trade idea is to treat a bullish marubozu's low as a line in the sand: if price holds above it on the following candles, the buyers are still in charge. Some traders enter on a small pullback toward the marubozu's midpoint, with a stop below its low.

Spinning tops and high waves after a trend are treated like dojis: wait for the next candle to close beyond the high or low of the pattern, then enter in that direction with a stop beyond the opposite wick. The pattern itself is not the trade; the resolution of the indecision is.

<Callout type="warning" title="A huge candle is not always a green light">

Beginners see a giant green marubozu and buy at the close, thinking the momentum is obvious. Sometimes it is. But if that candle came after weeks of rising prices, you may be buying the exact candle where the last enthusiastic buyer got in. The bigger the candle and the longer the trend before it, the more you should wait for the next session to show whether there is any follow-through.

</Callout>

## Honest reliability

Marubozu, spinning tops and high waves are rarely tested as standalone reversal patterns because they are not one. Bulkowski's work on the closing and opening marubozu shows them behaving as continuation candles a little over half the time, about 55%, in the direction of the candle. The spinning top and high wave are essentially neutral on their own: they predict a pause, not a direction, and studies put the follow-through in either direction near 50%.

That is not a criticism. Their job is to tell you the temperature of the market: boiling, frozen or somewhere in between. Use them to read conviction, and let the patterns in the rest of the module tell you direction.

<KeyTakeaways>
- A marubozu is a large body with no wicks: one side controlled every moment of the session.
- A spinning top is a small body with similar wicks on both sides: nobody won.
- A high wave is a spinning top with enormous wicks: violent indecision, often near turning points.
- None of these is a reversal pattern by itself; they are conviction gauges that make other patterns readable.
- A marubozu after a long extended trend can be exhaustion; wait for the next candle before assuming continuation.
- Spinning tops after a trend are traded like dojis: wait for the close beyond the pattern's range.
</KeyTakeaways>
