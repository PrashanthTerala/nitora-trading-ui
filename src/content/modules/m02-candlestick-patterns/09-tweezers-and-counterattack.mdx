Imagine trying to push open a door twice. The first time it stops at a certain point. You back off, take a run at it, and it stops at exactly the same point. Now you know something you did not know before: there is something solid behind that door, and it is not moving.

That is what a **tweezer** shows. Two candles, side by side, with matching highs (a tweezer top) or matching lows (a tweezer bottom). The market tested a price twice in a row and was stopped at the same place both times. It is named for the two prongs of a pair of tweezers lining up.

This lesson also covers the **counterattack line**, a two-candle pattern where the second candle gaps sharply against the first and then fights all the way back to close at the same price. Different picture, same lesson: a level was defended, and the side that attacked it gained nothing.

## Tweezer bottom: exact rules

- The market is in a **downtrend**.
- Two consecutive candles have **the same low**, or lows within a tiny fraction of each other (a few ticks, or a few hundredths of a percent).
- The first candle is usually red, continuing the trend. The second is often green, but the colour is secondary; what matters is the matching low.
- Bodies can be any size. Tweezers frequently combine with other patterns: a tweezer bottom whose second candle is a hammer, or whose two candles form an engulfing, is a tweezer plus another signal.

### The psychology

On day one, sellers push price down to a level and are stopped. On day two, they try again, reach the same price to the cent, and are stopped again. That is not a coincidence; it is a floor. Somebody with size is buying at that price and is willing to do it repeatedly.

Once the second attempt fails, sellers who were hoping for a breakdown start covering, and buyers who saw the level hold twice step in. The defended level becomes a reference for the whole market. In the language of the next module, it has just become <Term id="support">support</Term>.

<Callout type="eli5">

Picture a bouncy castle. You jump on it twice and both times your feet stop at exactly the same height because the floor is solid underneath. Two identical bounces tell you where the floor is. A tweezer bottom is the market finding its floor.

</Callout>

## Tweezer top: the mirror

- The market is in an **uptrend**.
- Two consecutive candles have **the same high**.
- The first candle is usually green; the second is often red.

<PatternFigure name="tweezer-top" caption="At the top of a rise, two candles peak at the same price. Buyers failed at the same ceiling twice. The red candle after it confirms the rejection." />

The story is the reverse: buyers pushed to a level twice and were sold into both times. Somebody is offering size at that price. The ceiling has been found, and in the next module it will be called <Term id="resistance">resistance</Term>.

## Counterattack lines

The counterattack line shares the "level defended" idea but draws a different picture. Its rules for the bullish version:

- The market is in a **downtrend**.
- **Candle one** is a large red candle.
- **Candle two gaps down sharply at the open**, well below candle one's close, then rallies to close **at or very near candle one's close**.

<PatternFigure name="bullish-counterattack" caption="After a decline, price gaps down hard at the open and rallies all the way back to close exactly where the previous candle closed. The sellers' big gap gained them nothing." />

Compare it with the piercing line from [the previous lesson](/learn/m02-candlestick-patterns/08-piercing-dark-cloud). The piercing line recovers into the first candle's body, past the midpoint. The counterattack recovers only to the first candle's close, not into the body at all. So on paper it is weaker. What gives it its power is the size of the gap: sellers had a huge head start at the open and buyers erased every bit of it.

The bearish counterattack is the mirror at the top of a rise: a big green candle, then a sharp gap up, then a sell-off that closes right at the previous close.

<PatternFigure name="bearish-counterattack" caption="At the top of a rise, price gaps up, then sells off to close where the previous candle closed. Buyers had a head start and lost all of it." />

The psychology is a squandered advantage. A gap in your favour is the best possible start to a session; giving it all back by the close means the other side was stronger than expected. The trend's side ends the day exactly where it began, with its confidence dented.

## Where they are valid

All four patterns are reversal patterns and need a prior <Term id="trend">trend</Term>. Tweezers are especially prone to false positives because matching highs and lows happen constantly in sideways markets, where price bounces between the same prices for days. A tweezer inside a range is not a signal.

The best tweezers form after an extended trend, at a level that has already mattered before (a prior swing low or high, a round number, a moving average), and where the second candle is itself a reversal shape such as a hammer or an engulfing. Counterattack lines need a genuine gap, which makes them more common in stocks than in 24-hour markets.

Tweezers are often more visible on lower timeframes and intraday charts, where two consecutive candles can have exactly equal extremes. On daily charts, allow a small tolerance rather than demanding identical prices.

## Confirmation

For a tweezer bottom or bullish counterattack: the next candle closes above the high of the second candle. For a tweezer top or bearish counterattack: the next candle closes below the low of the second candle. In both cases, the confirmation candle should not have retested the tweezer level; if it did and held, that is a third test and the level is even stronger, but if it broke through, the pattern has failed.

Volume on the second candle helps. A tweezer bottom where the second candle prints the heaviest volume in weeks shows genuine absorption at the low.

## A trade idea to practise

Tweezer bottom: enter long when the confirmation candle closes above the pattern's high. Stop a small buffer below the shared low. This is one of the cleanest stops in candlestick trading, because the shared low is a defended level: if it breaks, the defence has failed and there is no reason to hold.

Tweezer top: enter short when the confirmation candle closes below the pattern's low. Stop above the shared high.

Counterattack lines: enter in the reversal direction on confirmation, with the stop beyond the extreme of the gapped candle.

<Callout type="warning" title="Two prongs, or just noise?">

Tweezers are the easiest pattern to over-find. On any chart, adjacent candles with similar highs or lows are everywhere. Ask three questions before you count one: was there a trend to reverse, does the level line up with something that mattered before, and is the second candle itself a reversal shape? If the answer to all three is no, you are looking at two candles, not a tweezer.

</Callout>

## Honest reliability

Bulkowski's data puts the tweezer bottom around 52% as a reversal and the tweezer top around 56%. Both are close to a coin flip on their own, which makes sense: two matching prices tell you a level exists, not that it will hold a third time. The bullish and bearish counterattack lines are also modest performers, roughly 55% to 63% depending on the sample, and they are rare enough that the statistics are shaky.

The practical value of tweezers is less as a standalone signal and more as a way to find levels. A tweezer marks a price the market defended. Combine it with a trend, a prior level and a reversal candle, and it becomes a real trade. Alone, it is a mark on the chart.

<KeyTakeaways>
- A tweezer is two consecutive candles with matching lows (bottom, after a decline) or matching highs (top, after a rise).
- Matching extremes show a level tested twice and defended: a floor or ceiling being found.
- A counterattack line gaps sharply against the trend and closes at the previous close; the trend's head start was wasted.
- All four need a prior trend; matching highs and lows inside a range are noise.
- Confirm with a close beyond the second candle; stop just beyond the shared extreme, which is a very clean stop.
- Tweezers test near a coin flip (52% to 56%); their real value is marking levels for other signals.
</KeyTakeaways>
