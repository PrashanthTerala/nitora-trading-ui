Here is a puzzle. Two candles have exactly the same shape: a small body at the top and a long wick hanging underneath. One of them is called a hammer and it is bullish. The other is called a hanging man and it is bearish. Same shape, opposite meaning. How can that be?

The answer is the whole point of this lesson, and it is the same lesson from [How to Think About Candlestick Patterns](/learn/m02-candlestick-patterns/01-how-to-read-patterns): the shape tells you what happened inside one session, but the trend before it tells you what that means. A hammer appears after a decline. A hanging man appears after a rise. The candle is identical; the story around it is not.

If you understand these two, you understand the deepest idea in candlestick reading, which is that a pattern is a sentence, and the trend is the paragraph it sits in.

## What the shape looks like

Both candles follow the same rules, so learn them once:

- **Small body** near the top of the candle's range. The colour barely matters, though a green body is slightly better for the hammer and a red body slightly better for the hanging man.
- **Long lower wick**, at least twice the length of the body. Three times is better. This is the defining feature.
- **Little or no upper wick.** A tiny one is fine; a long one turns it into a different pattern.

A quick test: cover the candle with your thumb from the top and ask whether at least two-thirds of what remains is wick. If yes, the shape qualifies.

## The hammer: buyers hammer out a bottom

Imagine a stock that has fallen for six straight days. Everyone who wanted to sell has been selling. On the seventh day it opens, and the sellers do what they have been doing: they push it down hard. Then something different happens. Buyers show up in size, absorb every share on offer, and drive the price back up to where it opened.

That long lower wick is the record of the sellers' attack. The small body at the top is proof that the attack completely failed. For the first time in a week, the sellers pushed and the market pushed back. That is why a hammer after a decline is a **bullish reversal** candidate.

The psychology matters more than the shape. The sellers who sold at the low of that wick are now losing money by the close. Some of them will cover. Buyers who watched the reversal will be encouraged. The balance of eagerness has shifted, at least for one session.

<Callout type="eli5">

Picture a bouncy ball dropped down a staircase. It bounces a little on each step, then hits the floor and bounces high. The hammer is the candle where the ball hit the floor. The long wick is how far it dropped; the close near the top is the bounce.

</Callout>

## The hanging man: the first crack in a rally

Now take the identical candle and put it at the top of a six-day rise. Buyers have been in control all week. On the seventh day, price opens, and then it falls sharply. Buyers eventually recover the drop and close near the open, so on the surface nothing happened.

But something did happen: **for the first time in the rally, a wave of selling was strong enough to knock price down a long way.** The buyers recovered it, but they had to work for it. Sellers have shown up. The rally that looked effortless now has a crack in it.

<PatternFigure name="hanging-man" caption="Identical shape, after a rise. The long lower wick shows sellers appeared in force for the first time. Only the bearish close on the next candle turns it into a real signal." />

This is why the hanging man is a weaker signal than the hammer. In the hammer, the session ended with the trend losing. In the hanging man, the session ended with the trend winning; the buyers did recover. It is a warning, not a verdict. Steve Nison, who introduced these patterns to the West, was very clear that a hanging man **requires** bearish confirmation on the next candle, ideally a close below the hanging man's body.

## Where each is valid

| Pattern | Requires | Signals | Best location |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hammer | A clear prior decline | Possible bullish reversal | At support, after several red candles |
| Hanging man | A clear prior rise | Possible bearish reversal | At resistance, after several green candles |

Neither means anything inside a sideways range. If you cannot point at the trend that the candle is supposed to reverse, there is no pattern.

The quality of the trend also matters. A hammer after a slow, grinding decline is less powerful than a hammer after a sharp, panicky one, because panic sellers are the ones who capitulate at the low of the wick and then have to buy back.

## Confirmation

For the hammer, confirmation is a next candle that closes above the hammer's close, ideally above its high. For the hanging man, confirmation is a next candle that closes below the hanging man's body, and a gap down is even better. Volume helps in both cases: a hammer on the highest [volume](/learn/m01-reading-price/06-volume) in a week means a lot of hands changed at that low.

A <Term id="support">support</Term> level under a hammer, or a <Term id="resistance">resistance</Term> level above a hanging man, roughly doubles how seriously you should take it. That is the picture from lesson 01 with the hammer sitting exactly where price bounced before.

## A trade idea to practise

For a hammer: enter when the confirmation candle closes above the hammer's high. Place the stop a few ticks below the hammer's low. The logic is clean: the hammer's low is where buyers proved they would defend. If price falls through it, they have stopped defending and you were wrong. Target the next resistance level or a fixed multiple of your risk.

For a hanging man: enter short when the confirmation candle closes below the hanging man's body. Stop above the hanging man's high. Target the next support.

<Callout type="danger" title="The stop that gets hunted">

The hammer's low is an obvious stop for every trader who saw the candle, which means a lot of stop orders sit just under it. Price often dips through that low by a small amount, triggers the stops, and then reverses up. You cannot avoid this completely, but placing the stop a little further away, beyond the noise, rather than one tick under the low, helps. You will study this properly in the risk module.

</Callout>

## Honest reliability

Bulkowski's data is sobering. The hammer, treated as a bullish reversal, works around 60% of the time in his stock tests, which makes it one of the better single candles. The hanging man is close to a coin flip: it reverses the trend only slightly more often than it fails, and in some samples it actually continues the uptrend more often. That matches the psychology: the hanging man's session ended with buyers still in charge.

The practical conclusion is that hammers deserve respect, hanging men deserve suspicion, and both need a trend, a level and a confirmation candle before you touch them. A hammer with all three is a genuinely useful signal. A hanging man with none of them is a long wick and nothing more.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Hammer and hanging man share one shape: small body at the top, lower wick at least twice the body, little upper wick.
- The trend before the candle decides which one it is: hammer after a decline, hanging man after a rise.
- The hammer shows sellers attacked and lost; the hanging man shows sellers appeared for the first time but buyers still recovered.
- Both need confirmation: a next candle closing beyond the pattern in the predicted direction.
- Hammers test around 60% reliable; hanging men are close to a coin flip and need confirmation badly.
- Stops go just beyond the long wick, a little further than the obvious tick to avoid stop hunts.
</KeyTakeaways>
