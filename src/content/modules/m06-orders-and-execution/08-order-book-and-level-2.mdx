Picture a queue at two counters in a hall. At the left counter stand people who want to buy, each holding a sign with the most they'll pay and how many they want. At the right counter stand sellers, each with a sign showing the least they'll accept. The two front people are closest to agreeing; the people at the back are far apart and might wait all day.

The **order book** is that hall, written down. Every resting limit order in a market sits in it, sorted by price. When you learned about the <Term id="bid">bid</Term> and <Term id="ask">ask</Term> in [Bid, Ask and the Spread](/learn/m00-what-is-a-market/07-bid-ask-spread), you were looking at the two people at the front of each queue. Now you get to see the whole hall.

## What the book looks like

Here is a small <Term id="order-book">order book</Term> for a stock trading around $50. Bids on the left, asks on the right, best prices in the middle, sizes in shares.

| Bid size | Bid price | | Ask price | Ask size |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | | | $50.10 | 2,500 |
| | | | $50.08 | 900 |
| | | | $50.05 | 1,000 |
| | | | **$50.02** | **300** |
| **500** | **$49.98** | | | |
| 1,200 | $49.95 | | | |
| 700 | $49.92 | | | |
| 3,000 | $49.90 | | | |

The bold row is the **top of book**: the best bid ($49.98 for 500 shares) and the best ask ($50.02 for 300 shares). The <Term id="spread">spread</Term> is 4 cents. Everything else is **depth**: orders waiting further away.

Every number here is a resting limit order. Nobody's market order appears in the book, because market orders don't wait; they consume what's there and vanish.

<Callout type="eli5">

The order book is a list of everyone's "I'll buy at..." and "I'll sell at..." notes, stacked so the friendliest prices are at the top. The two friendliest notes are the bid and ask. When a buyer and seller's notes match, a trade happens and those notes are torn up.

</Callout>

## Level 1 vs Level 2

**Level 1** data is the top of the book only: best bid, best ask, sizes, and the last trade. It's what most free apps show.

<Term id="level-2">Level 2</Term> shows the depth: several price levels on each side, often with the identity of the market maker or exchange posting each order. Some platforms also show **time and sales**, a running tape of every trade with its price, size and whether it hit the bid or lifted the ask.

Level 2 lets you see what a market order will do *before* you send it. In the book above, a market buy of 1,000 shares would take 300 at $50.02, 700 of the 1,000 at $50.05, and stop. Average: (300 × 50.02 + 700 × 50.05) ÷ 1,000 = (15,006 + 35,035) ÷ 1,000 = **$50.041**. You can see that 2.1 cents of slippage on the screen ahead of time.

## Reading the queue

**Price priority.** Better prices get filled first. A bid at $49.98 fills before a bid at $49.95, always.

**Time priority.** At the same price, whoever placed their order first gets filled first. If 500 shares are bid at $49.98 and you add 200 more at the same price, you're at the back of that 700-share queue. A 300-share market sell hits the original orders, not yours.

This is why "joining the bid" can be slow. You're queueing behind everyone who got there earlier. If you want to be at the front, you have to bid a cent higher, which means a new best bid and a narrower spread. You just improved the market for everyone, and got yourself to the front.

## What the book can and can't tell you

Level 2 is often sold as a window into "what the big players are doing." Be sceptical. Here's what it honestly shows and what it doesn't.

| The book shows | The book does not show |
| --- | --- |
| Resting limit orders at each level, right now | Market orders, which never rest |
| Where the spread is and how deep each side is | Orders on other venues or dark pools |
| Approximate slippage for a given order size | Whether a large order is real or about to be cancelled |
| Imbalances (far more size on one side) | Iceberg orders that display only a slice of their true size |
| The queue you'd join at a given price | Anyone's intention, only their current resting order |

**Spoofing** is placing a large order to look like support or resistance and cancelling it before it fills, to nudge other traders. It's illegal in regulated markets and still happens. **Icebergs** are large orders that show only a small visible portion and refill it as it's eaten. Both mean the displayed sizes are a hint, not a promise.

<Callout type="warning" title="Big size in the book is not a wall">

A 3,000-share bid at $49.90 looks like a floor. It can be cancelled in a millisecond, and often is the moment price approaches it. Treat displayed depth as "what's there now," never as "what will be there when I need it." Many beginners buy in front of a big bid, watch it vanish, and hold the bag.

</Callout>

## Watching a trade happen

Let's run a market sell of 800 shares through the book above, one row at a time.

1. The best bid is $49.98 with 500 shares. The sell takes all 500. That row disappears.
2. 300 shares remain to sell. The new best bid is $49.95 with 1,200 shares. The sell takes 300 of them.
3. The book now shows 900 shares bid at $49.95 as the new top. The last trade printed at $49.95.

The bid moved down 3 cents, not because anyone changed their mind about the stock, but because one impatient seller ate the top of the queue. That's how price moves at the smallest scale: the [tug of war](/learn/m00-what-is-a-market/03-why-prices-move) is fought one row of the book at a time.

## How traders actually use Level 2

- **Judging liquidity before sizing.** If the top three levels hold 800 shares and you want to trade 5,000, you're about to move the market.
- **Choosing a limit price.** Seeing 1,200 shares queued at $49.95 tells you a bid there will wait a while; a bid at $49.99 jumps the queue.
- **Spotting a thinning book.** When asks vanish as price rises, there's little resistance overhead; when bids vanish as price falls, there's an air pocket below. Both are short-lived clues, not signals.
- **Time and sales confirmation.** A steady stream of trades hitting the ask suggests aggressive buying; trades hitting the bid suggests aggressive selling.

For most swing and position traders, Level 2 is a curiosity. For scalpers and day traders in a single instrument it's the main screen. Either way, understanding it turns the bid and ask from two mysterious numbers into a queue you can picture.

<KeyTakeaways>
- The order book is every resting limit order, stacked by price, with the best bid and best ask at the top.
- Level 1 shows only the top of the book; Level 2 shows the depth on each side.
- Orders fill by price first and then by time, so joining an existing level puts you at the back of that queue.
- You can estimate a market order's slippage by walking it through the visible levels.
- Displayed size can be cancelled instantly or hidden as icebergs, so depth is a hint, not a wall.
</KeyTakeaways>
