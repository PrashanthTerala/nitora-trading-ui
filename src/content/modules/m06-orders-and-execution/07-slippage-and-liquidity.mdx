You see a jacket in a shop window priced at $50. You walk in, and by the time you reach the counter it's $52. Not because anyone cheated you, but because three other people grabbed the last $50 ones while you were walking, and the next rack costs more.

That gap between the price you saw and the price you paid is **slippage**. In trading it happens in milliseconds rather than the time it takes to cross a shop, and it happens on almost every market order. Sometimes it's a cent. Sometimes, during news or in a thin market, it's the entire edge of your strategy.

This lesson explains why fills differ from the screen, what liquidity really means, and how to keep slippage small.

## The price on the screen is history

The "last price" you see is the most recent trade. It already happened. The only prices you can actually deal at are the current <Term id="bid">bid</Term> and <Term id="ask">ask</Term>, and even those are only good for the number of shares sitting at them.

From [Market Orders vs Limit Orders](/learn/m06-orders-and-execution/01-market-and-limit-orders): the ask is $50.02 with 300 shares. Buy 500 at market and the last 200 come from the next level up. Your average was $50.032, not $50.02. That 1.2 cents is slippage caused purely by your order being bigger than the top of the book.

<Term id="slippage">Slippage</Term> is the difference between the price you expected and the price you got. It can be positive (a better fill than expected, which happens occasionally) but it's usually negative, because you're the one demanding to trade *now*.

## Liquidity: how deep is the pool?

<Term id="liquidity">Liquidity</Term> is how much you can buy or sell without moving the price. A liquid market has thousands of shares waiting at every cent. An illiquid or **thin** market has a few hundred shares scattered across wide price gaps.

Three signs of a liquid instrument:

- **Tight spread.** A cent or two on a $50 stock. In thin names it might be 20 or 30 cents.
- **High daily volume.** Millions of shares a day rather than tens of thousands.
- **Depth.** Lots of size at each price level in the order book, which you'll see next lesson.

<Callout type="eli5">

Think of a swimming pool. In a deep pool you can jump in and barely make a ripple. In a paddling pool, the same jump throws water everywhere. Liquidity is how deep the pool is. A big order in a shallow market splashes the price around, and the splash is your slippage.

</Callout>

## Three causes of bad fills

**1. Your order is bigger than the top of the book.** Covered above. The fix is to trade instruments where the top-of-book size dwarfs your order, or to split large orders.

**2. The market is moving fast.** Between the moment you click and the moment the exchange matches you, other orders arrive. During a sharp move the ask might tick from $50.02 to $50.10 to $50.25 in a second. Your market order fills wherever it lands. Stop-losses suffer most here, because they trigger *exactly* when the market is lurching.

**3. The book is thin.** Pre-market, after-hours, holiday sessions, small-cap stocks and obscure crypto pairs all have sparse order books. A modest market order can fill several percent away from the last print.

## Worked example: news hits

You're long 500 shares at $60 with a stop at $58. A surprise announcement drops at 2:15 p.m. The book empties as market makers pull their quotes.

| Time | Best bid | Size at bid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2:14:59 | $59.80 | 800 |
| 2:15:00 | $58.00 | 100 |
| 2:15:00 | $57.40 | 200 |
| 2:15:01 | $56.90 | 1,500 |

Your stop triggers when $58.00 trades. It becomes a market sell for 500. It takes 100 at $58.00, 200 at $57.40, and the final 200 at $56.90. Average fill: (100 × 58 + 200 × 57.40 + 200 × 56.90) ÷ 500 = (5,800 + 11,480 + 11,380) ÷ 500 = **$57.32**.

You planned to lose $2 a share, $1,000 total. You lost $2.68 a share, $1,340. Slippage added 34% to the loss. Nothing broke; this is just what a stop does in a fast market. The [risk module](/learn/m07-risk-management/03-stop-loss-placement) will tell you to *plan* for this rather than be surprised by it.

<Callout type="danger" title="Earnings and scheduled news">

Holding a leveraged position through a scheduled announcement means accepting that your stop is decorative. Stocks routinely gap 10% to 20% on earnings, straight through any stop. Either size the position so the gap is survivable, or be flat before the event. The [economic calendar lesson](/learn/m10-fundamentals-and-macro/05-economic-calendar) lists the events that do this to whole markets at once.

</Callout>

## Measuring your own slippage

Keep a column in your journal: expected price versus actual fill. After 50 trades you'll know your average slippage per share. If you're a scalper aiming for 5 cents a trade and your slippage is 3 cents, you've found out why you're not making money. The journal at /journal records fills so you can compute this.

## How to reduce slippage

| Tactic | Why it works |
| --- | --- |
| Use limit orders to enter | You choose the price; no fill is the only downside |
| Trade liquid instruments | Deep books absorb your size |
| Trade the main session | Pre and post-market books are thin |
| Avoid the first and last minutes | Spreads are widest and prices jumpiest at the open and close |
| Split large orders | Several small orders walk the book less than one big one |
| Stay flat through scheduled news | Gaps make stops useless |
| Size so your order is a small fraction of top-of-book | If 5,000 shares sit at the ask and you want 100, you'll fill cleanly |

One thing you should *not* do to avoid slippage is replace your stop-loss with a stop-limit. As the [stop orders lesson](/learn/m06-orders-and-execution/02-stop-orders) explained, that swaps "a bad fill" for "no fill," which is worse.

<Callout type="tip" title="Slippage is a cost, not a mistake">

A little slippage on every market order is the price of immediacy, the same way a taxi costs more than a bus. Budget for it. What you want to avoid is the *big* slippage that comes from trading thin books, big size or news, because those are choices.

</Callout>

<KeyTakeaways>
- The last price is history; you can only deal at the bid or ask, and only for the size resting there.
- Slippage is the gap between the price you expected and the fill you got, and it is usually against you.
- Liquidity is how much you can trade without moving price; tight spreads, high volume and deep books are the signs.
- Fast markets, thin books and oversized orders are the three causes of bad fills, and stops suffer most.
- Limit entries, liquid instruments, main-session trading and staying flat through news keep slippage small.
</KeyTakeaways>
