A ship is built with watertight compartments so a hole in one does not sink the vessel. The trick only works if the walls reach the ceiling. If they stop halfway, water pours over the top and the five compartments behave, at the worst possible moment, like one.

Traders build the same illusion. They open five positions, feel diversified because there are five tickers on the screen, and find on a bad morning that all five stops were hit within twenty minutes. The compartments were never separate.

If you followed the [1% rule](/learn/m07-risk-management/02-the-one-percent-rule) on each of those five trades, you believed you were risking 1%. You were risking closer to 5% on a single idea, split into five tickets.

## Correlation in one paragraph

**Correlation** measures how much two instruments move together, on a scale from plus 1 to minus 1. At **plus 1** they move in lockstep: when one rises 2%, so does the other. At **zero**, knowing one tells you nothing about the other. At **minus 1** they move exactly opposite. Most things you will trade sit between plus 0.3 and plus 0.9 against the broad market, far higher than beginners assume.

<Callout type="eli5">

You have five piggy banks so that losing one does not ruin you. But all five are on the same shelf, in the same room, in the same house. A flood does not care that there are five of them. Spreading your money out only helps if the things you spread it into can actually have different days.

</Callout>

## Five hats, one trade

Suppose you go long five technology stocks, each sized to risk exactly 1% of a $10,000 account, so $100 each.

If they were truly independent, a bad day for one would usually be an ordinary day for the others, and the chance of all five stopping out together would be tiny. At a correlation near plus 0.9 they are the same instrument bought five times. One sector-wide selloff takes out all five stops in a session and you lose $500: **5% of the account in a single event**. That is not a 1% risk strategy. It is a 5% risk strategy with extra commission.

| What you think you have | What you actually have |
| --- | --- |
| Five positions at 1% risk | One position at 5% risk |
| Five chances to be right | One idea, tested once |
| Diversification | Concentration, disguised by ticker count |

The test is not "how many positions do I have?" It is **"how many distinct ways can I be wrong?"** Five tech longs is one way. A tech long, a gold long and a short in energy is three, and even those are not fully independent.

## The layers of correlation

Correlation stacks, and most traders only notice the top layer.

**Sector correlation.** Two semiconductor companies share a demand story, a supply chain and customers. Typical correlations of plus 0.7 to plus 0.9.

**Market correlation.** Almost every stock has a component that is simply "the market went up today", which is why a basket of ten stocks from ten sectors is still largely one bet on the index.

**Theme correlation.** The sneaky one. An airline, a hotel chain and an oil producer sit in three sectors and can still be one trade on fuel prices or travel demand. The sector label does not always describe the actual driver.

**Direction correlation.** Five longs are correlated by being longs. Genuinely separate compartments need some shorts, or instruments with different drivers entirely.

The simulator's instruments are labelled by what they stand for, which makes the sorting easy to practise. NOVA is growth tech, IDX is the broad index and BIOX a small-cap biotech, and in a real market all three would ride the same risk appetite, so holding all three long would be close to one large position. AURM stands in for gold, PETR for energy and FXEU for a currency pair: different drivers, different bets.

One honest caveat. Each simulated instrument is generated independently, so you will not actually see them move together on the charts the way real ones do. Use the labels to rehearse the slot rule below; take the correlation itself from real markets, where it is very real indeed.

## In a crisis, correlation goes to one

Here is the part that matters most and is forgotten most.

The correlations you measure on calm days are not the ones you get on the day you need them. When markets fall hard, investors do not sell selectively; they sell whatever they can, to raise cash. Assets unrelated for two years suddenly drop together.

So carefully constructed diversification works fine during the periods when you did not need it and thins out exactly when the losses arrive. That is not a flaw you can engineer away. It is a reason to keep total exposure modest rather than to trust a clever mix.

<Callout type="danger" title="Leverage plus correlation is how accounts disappear">

With [margin](/learn/m06-orders-and-execution/06-margin-and-leverage), five correlated 1% positions can be a much larger fraction of your buying power than the risk numbers suggest. When they move together against you, the position values fall together, and a margin call can force all five closed at the worst prices of the day, turning a planned 5% loss into something considerably worse. Correlation and leverage are individually survivable and jointly not.

</Callout>

## Capping total open risk

The fix is simple to state and requires no correlation maths at the desk.

<Callout type="math" title="The portfolio heat rule">

**Total open risk across all positions: 3% maximum.**

Total open risk is the sum of what you would lose if every open stop were hit at once. Three positions at 1% each. Or six at 0.5%. Or one at 1% and four at 0.5%. When you are at the cap, no new trade until one closes or a stop moves to breakeven.

</Callout>

Then add the correlation adjustment:

- **Positions in the same sector, theme or direction count as one slot.** If you want two semiconductor longs, they get 0.5% each, not 1% each.
- **One full-size position per idea.** The second expression of the same idea is half size.
- **Once a stop is at breakeven, that position no longer counts** towards the 3%, because it can no longer lose you anything.

A worked case. You hold NOVA long at 1% and now like BIOX long. Both rise with the same risk appetite, so they share a slot: take BIOX at 0.5%, and 0.5% for NOVA if you have not already entered. You still have 2% of heat available, and it should go to a different driver, such as AURM or a PETR short, not a third growth-tech long.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Correlation runs from plus 1 to minus 1 and measures how much two instruments move together.
- Five correlated longs at 1% each are one idea risked at 5%, not five separate 1% risks.
- Correlation stacks across sector, broad market, theme and simple direction, so a ticker count is not a diversification measure.
- In a sharp selloff correlations rise towards one, so diversification thins out exactly when you need it.
- Cap total open risk across all positions at about 3%, and count correlated positions as a single slot.
- Ask how many distinct ways you can be wrong, not how many positions you hold.
</KeyTakeaways>
