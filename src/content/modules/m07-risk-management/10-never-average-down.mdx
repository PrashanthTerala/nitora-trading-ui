There is one sentence that has cost traders more money than any other: *it is cheaper now*.

The sentence is not even stupid. For a long-term investor who valued a business at $80 and can buy it at $45, a lower price really is a better price. That is the [investing](/learn/m00-what-is-a-market/04-trading-vs-investing) game, and it has no stop-loss in it.

A trade is a different game. It came with a price at which you agreed you were wrong. Buying more below that price does not improve the trade; it deletes the part of the plan keeping you alive. The arithmetic is much uglier than the feeling suggests.

## What averaging down actually does

Start with a clean, correctly sized trade on a $10,000 account. Entry $50, stop $48, 50 shares, risk exactly $100, which is 1R.

Price falls to $45. The stop was ignored, so the open loss is 50 × $5 = **$250**, already two and a half times the planned risk. The trader now buys 50 more at $45, making the average cost (50 × $50 + 50 × $45) ÷ 100 = **$47.50** on 100 shares.

Two things just happened and only one is visible. The comforting one: break-even dropped from $50 to $47.50, so a smaller bounce gets you out flat.

The problem: **every further dollar of decline now costs twice as much.** A $1 fall used to cost $50; now it costs $100. The trader has doubled their exposure to the exact outcome they are currently being wrong about.

| Price | Loss on the original 50 shares | Loss after averaging down |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $48 (the original stop) | $100 | — |
| $45 | $250 | $250 |
| $42 | $400 | $550 |
| $40 | $500 | $750 |
| $35 | $750 | $1,250 |

At $42 the planned 1R loss of $100 has become $550. At $35 it is $1,250, which is twelve and a half R and more than 12% of the account, on a trade that was supposed to risk 1%.

<Callout type="eli5">

You are in a hole and you would like to get out. Averaging down is deciding that the fastest way out is to make the hole wider, because then the sides are less steep. The sides do get less steep. You are also much further underground, and it takes a lot more digging to fill it back in.

</Callout>

## Why it feels so right

Averaging down is seductive because it works most of the time. Markets bounce. A trader who adds at $45 usually gets the bounce to $47.50, closes flat or slightly up, and files the episode as a save.

So the habit is rewarded repeatedly for weeks or months. The win rate rises, because losses have become scratches, and the journal looks better. The whole time, one tail is being built: the trade that does not bounce.

Frequent small rewards, one rare catastrophic punishment, and the rewards arrive first. It is the same pattern as an 80% win rate with no stop, from the [win rate lesson](/learn/m07-risk-management/06-win-rate-vs-rr), and that is no coincidence.

## Martingale: averaging down with the maths made explicit

The pure form comes from casinos. Bet $100 on a coin flip. Lose, bet $200. Lose again, bet $400. Whenever you finally win, you recover everything lost plus the original $100. It is guaranteed to work, provided you have infinite money and there is no maximum bet. You have neither.

| Loss number | Next bet required | Total lost so far | Left of a $10,000 account |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $200 | $100 | $9,900 |
| 2 | $400 | $300 | $9,700 |
| 3 | $800 | $700 | $9,300 |
| 4 | $1,600 | $1,500 | $8,500 |
| 5 | $3,200 | $3,100 | $6,900 |
| 6 | $6,400 | $6,300 | $3,700 |
| 7 | $12,800 | $12,700 | Account gone |

Look at row six. You have $3,700 left and the system requires a $6,400 bet. You cannot make it, so the sequence ends there, with almost the entire account gone.

Now the probabilities. Seven losses in a row on a coin flip is 0.5 to the power 7, about **0.8%**. On a realistic 40% win rate strategy it is 0.6 to the power 7, about **2.8%**. From the [streak lesson](/learn/m07-risk-management/07-drawdown-and-risk-of-ruin), that gets roughly ninety-four attempts inside a hundred trades, so you should expect to hit the fatal run **more than twice per hundred trades**.

And what were you playing for? Each completed cycle nets exactly **$100**. You are risking the entire account to make $100, over and over, until the run arrives. It always arrives.

<Callout type="danger" title="Adding because it went against you is always a martingale">

It does not have to double. Adding half a position at each of three lower levels is a gentler martingale with the same structure and the same ending. The test is simple: does the rule increase your exposure *because* the trade is losing? If yes, your worst outcome is unbounded, and unbounded worst outcomes eventually happen.

</Callout>

## Scaling in is a different thing entirely

None of this means you must always enter in one click. Professionals routinely build a position in pieces. The difference is not the number of entries; it is whether total risk was fixed before the first one.

<Callout type="math" title="A planned scale-in">

$10,000 account, 1% risk, so total risk is **$100 and it will not change**.

- **Tranche 1:** 30 shares at $50. Stop $48. Risk 30 × $2 = $60.
- **Tranche 2:** 40 shares at $49, a level chosen in advance. Same stop $48. Risk 40 × $1 = $40.
- **Total:** 70 shares, average cost $49.43, and if the stop is hit you lose 70 × $1.43 = **$100**.

One R in, one R at risk, exactly as planned.

</Callout>

The whole structure was decided before entry. The second level was on the chart in advance. The stop never moved. The total is still 1R.

<Compare left="Planned scale-in" right="Panic averaging">

- Total risk decided before the first entry.
- Add levels chosen on the chart in advance.
- Stop stays exactly where it was.
- Total risk stays at 1R no matter how many tranches fill.
- Written in the trading plan.

- Total risk decided while losing.
- Add level is wherever price happens to be now.
- Stop is moved, or was never placed.
- Total risk grows without limit.
- Justified afterwards.

</Compare>

<Callout type="warning" title="Add to winners, not to losers">

The mirror image is legitimate and underused. **Pyramiding** means adding to a position that is already working, sized so total risk never exceeds 1R, usually by raising the original stop as the new tranche goes on. You increase exposure to something the market is agreeing with, and your worst case stays fixed. Psychologically and mathematically, it is the opposite of averaging down.

</Callout>

<KeyTakeaways>
- Averaging down lowers your break-even price and doubles your loss per point, which is a bad exchange.
- A correctly sized 1R trade can become a 12R loss simply by adding once at a lower price.
- The habit is rewarded most of the time, which is exactly what makes it dangerous: it builds one rare, unbounded loss.
- Martingale doubling demands a $6,400 seventh bet on a $10,000 account just to win $100, and a 40% win rate hits seven-loss runs more than twice per hundred trades.
- Any rule that increases exposure because the trade is losing is a martingale, whatever it is called.
- A planned scale-in fixes total risk and the stop before the first entry; pyramiding into winners is the legitimate version of adding.
</KeyTakeaways>
