A young footballer misses three penalties in a row. Two things can happen next. Either they decide they cannot take penalties and refuse the next one, or they get furious and blast the fourth one over the bar. Neither helps. The same footballer scores a spectacular goal, gets carried off on the crowd's shoulders, and in the next match tries a 40-yard shot every time they touch the ball.

Streaks, in both directions, are where trading psychology gets tested for real. A losing streak makes you doubt everything you know. A big win makes you forget it. Both are normal. Both are dangerous. And both have the same cure: a plan for what to do that was written before the streak began.

## Losing streaks are guaranteed

Let's get the maths out of the way, because it changes how a streak feels. In the [drawdown lesson](/learn/m07-risk-management/07-drawdown-and-risk-of-ruin) you saw that streaks are a mathematical certainty for any strategy with a win rate below 100%.

| Win rate | Chance of 5 losses in a row within 100 trades | Longest likely streak in 500 trades |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60% | About 45% | 6 to 8 |
| 50% | About 80% | 8 to 10 |
| 40% | Over 95% | 11 to 13 |

Read that middle row again. A perfectly good 50% strategy will lose five in a row in most sets of 100 trades, and will produce a streak of eight to ten at some point. That is not the strategy breaking. That is the strategy *working exactly as expected*.

<Callout type="eli5">

If you flip a coin 100 times, four times out of five you will see five heads in a row somewhere. Nobody thinks the coin is broken. A losing streak in a good strategy is the same thing: it looks like a pattern but it is just what randomness looks like up close.

</Callout>

## What a losing streak does to you

<Callout type="story" title="Leo's week">

Leo's swing strategy has a 45% win rate and a 2.5R target. He loses six in a row on the daily chart over two weeks. Each loss was an A-grade trade. By the sixth, he is convinced the strategy has "stopped working". He skips the seventh setup. It runs to target for 2.5R he never collects. He takes the eighth at half size "to be safe". It also reaches target, but at half size it pays him 1.25R instead of 2.5R. Two winners into the recovery, he has collected 1.25R of the 5R that was there, and the streak has cost him more through the trades he shrank and skipped than through the ones he lost.

</Callout>

The pattern is always similar:

- After two or three losses: mild doubt. Still following the plan.
- After four or five: strong doubt. Start second-guessing setups. Enter late or skip.
- After six or more: either abandon the strategy, or flip into [revenge trading](/learn/m08-trading-psychology/03-revenge-trading) at bigger size.

The mechanism is [recency bias](/learn/m08-trading-psychology/04-cognitive-biases) plus [loss aversion](/learn/m08-trading-psychology/01-fear-and-greed). Six losses feel enormous. Five hundred trades of history feel abstract. And because each loss hurts double, six losses feel like twelve.

## The losing streak protocol

Write this into your plan now.

1. **Know your expected streak.** Using your win rate and the table above, write down the streak length that would be *normal* for your strategy. For a 45% win rate, that is around 10. Until you hit that number, a streak is not information.
2. **Check the grades, not the results.** Pull up the streak in the [journal](/journal). Are they A trades? If yes, the process is fine and you change nothing. If they are C trades, the streak is not the strategy; it is you, and the fix is the checklist, not a new strategy.
3. **Reduce size, do not skip.** After four consecutive losses, drop to half size *but keep taking every valid setup*. This is pre-commitment. Half size protects the account; still taking the setups keeps you in the game for the recovery. Skipping is how Leo lost the most.
4. **Return to full size after two wins** at half size, or after the equity curve makes a new high, whichever your plan says. Write it down so it is not a feeling.
5. **Review only at the scheduled time.** Weekly, over a large sample. Never in the middle of the streak.
6. **If the streak exceeds your expected maximum** by a clear margin, and the trades are A grade, *then* it is time to investigate whether the market regime has changed. Even then, the answer is a scheduled backtest, not a panic switch.

<Callout type="warning">

Reducing size after losses is a good rule. Reducing *participation* is a bad one. The moment you start skipping valid setups, you have turned a mathematical streak into a self-inflicted one, because you will always skip the winner that ends it.

</Callout>

## Big wins are just as dangerous

Nobody warns you about this one. A big win, a 5R or an outsized day, feels like the reward for all the hard work. It is also the single most reliable trigger for the worst trade of your month.

<Callout type="story" title="Sam's best day">

Sam catches a NOVA breakout perfectly and makes 6R in a single afternoon. His biggest day ever. He is euphoric. He tells himself he has "finally got it". The next morning he takes a marginal setup at double size, because he is clearly seeing the market well. Then another. By lunchtime he has given back 4R of the 6, and by the end of the week, all of it and more.

</Callout>

The mechanism is **dopamine**. A big, unexpected reward produces a flood of it, and dopamine drives *wanting more*. The feeling of being on a roll is chemical, not analytical. The strategy has exactly the same edge it had yesterday. Only the size and the selectivity have changed, in the wrong directions.

There is a second mechanism: **house money**. Winnings feel like they belong to the market, not to you, so risking them feels free. It is not free. A dollar of profit is identical to a dollar of deposit the moment the trade closes.

## The big win protocol

1. **After any single trade above 3R, or any day above 3% of the account: no new trades for the rest of the session.** Bank it. Close the platform. The most profitable thing you can do after a big win is nothing.
2. **The next session, trade at normal size.** Not bigger. Size comes from the formula, and the formula has not changed.
3. **Reread the trade in the journal.** Was it an A trade that happened to pay big, or a C trade that got lucky? If it was a C, it goes in the dangerous box from the [process lesson](/learn/m08-trading-psychology/06-process-over-outcome), and you treat it as a mistake regardless of the profit.
4. **Watch for the "I've got it now" thought.** That thought is the dopamine talking. Every trader who blew up after a great month had it.

<Callout type="tip">

A simple test after a big win: are you thinking about the *next* trade more than usual? Are you looking for setups on symbols you don't normally trade? Both mean the wanting chemical is driving. Step away until it passes, usually a few hours.

</Callout>

## The flat line is the goal

The emotional life of a consistent trader is boring. Wins are logged. Losses are logged. Streaks are expected. Big days end early. Bad days end early. Size comes from a formula. Rules change only in reviews.

That flatness is not the absence of feeling. You will feel the streaks and the wins just as much as anyone. The difference is that your *behaviour* stays flat while your feelings move, because the behaviour was decided in advance by someone who was not feeling anything at the time: you, on a calm day, writing the plan.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Losing streaks are mathematically guaranteed; a 50% strategy will lose five in a row in most sets of 100 trades.
- During a streak, check the process grades, reduce size after four losses, but never skip valid setups.
- Review the strategy only at scheduled times over large samples, never in the middle of a streak.
- Big wins trigger dopamine and the house-money effect, and reliably precede the worst trade of the month.
- After a big win, take no new trades that session and return the next day at formula size, not bigger.
</KeyTakeaways>
