Every computer program has bugs. Some are rare and harmless; others crash the system at the worst possible moment. Your brain is a program too, written over millions of years for a very different world, and it has bugs. Psychologists call them **cognitive biases**.

The important thing about a bias is that knowing about it does not switch it off. You can read every paper on optical illusions and the two lines will still look different lengths. Biases are the same. You cannot *think* your way out of one in the moment. You can only build rules that route around it.

This lesson covers the six that cost traders the most money: loss aversion, the disposition effect, sunk cost, confirmation bias, recency bias and anchoring. For each one you get a story, the mechanism, and a countermeasure you can put into your plan today.

## Loss aversion: losses hurt twice as much

<Callout type="story" title="Priya's two Tuesdays">

On Tuesday, Priya makes $200. She feels pleased for about an hour, then forgets. The following Tuesday she loses $200. She thinks about it at dinner, wakes up at 3am thinking about it, and is still slightly annoyed on Thursday. Same amount, opposite sign, wildly different weight.

</Callout>

**Mechanism.** Kahneman and Tversky described this in the 1970s, and studies across many countries and many kinds of bets have found the same broad pattern since: a loss is felt roughly **twice as strongly** as an equal gain. The "twice" is an average, not a constant, and it varies with the person and the stakes. Offer someone a coin flip where they win $150 or lose $100, and most people refuse, even though the bet has a positive expected value. The $100 loss looms larger than the $150 gain.

For a trader this is a disaster, because trading *is* that coin flip. A strategy with a 50% win rate and 1.5:1 reward-to-risk is excellent, and loss aversion makes every one of its losses feel like a mistake.

**Countermeasure.** Think in R, never in dollars. A loss is "1R", the planned unit. You learned this in [R-multiples](/learn/m07-risk-management/04-risk-reward-ratio). Also, review your results in batches of 20 trades rather than one at a time. Loss aversion is triggered by individual losses; a batch shows the expectancy.

## The disposition effect: cut winners, ride losers

This is loss aversion in action, and it is one of the most heavily measured biases in trading. Studies of large samples of real brokerage accounts, starting with Terrance Odean's work in the 1990s, find people are noticeably more likely to sell a stock showing a gain than one showing a loss. The result is a portfolio of losers, held, and a list of winners, sold early.

**Mechanism.** Selling a winner *locks in* a pleasant feeling. Selling a loser *makes the loss real*, and while it is open, the brain can pretend it might come back. So the winner gets sold to bank the feeling, and the loser gets held to avoid the feeling.

**Countermeasure.** Bracket orders. Stop and target are placed at entry, as in the [bracket orders lesson](/learn/m06-orders-and-execution/03-bracket-and-oco), and you do not touch them. If you must have a rule for adjusting, make it one-directional: stops can only move *towards* profit, targets can only be hit, never moved.

<Callout type="eli5">

Imagine you have two sweets, one nice and one going stale. The disposition effect is eating the nice one first and keeping the stale one "for later", forever. Trading well means throwing the stale one away, even though it feels like waste.

</Callout>

## Sunk cost: throwing good money after bad

<Callout type="story" title="Leo and the ticket">

Leo bought a $60 concert ticket. On the night, he has flu. Going will make him feel worse and he won't enjoy it. He goes anyway, because "otherwise the $60 is wasted." But the $60 is gone either way. Going only adds a miserable evening on top.

In the market, Leo buys BIOX at $24 with a stop at $22. Price reaches $22 and he does not take the loss, because "it's only $2, it will come back." At $18 he still will not sell, now because "I've already lost $6 a share, I can't sell now." That $6 is gone whether he holds or not. Holding only adds whatever comes next, and what comes next is $15.

</Callout>

**Mechanism.** The brain counts money already spent as still being "in play". Quitting feels like *confirming* the loss, while continuing keeps the hope alive. So people escalate: more time, more money, more risk, to justify what is already spent.

**Countermeasure.** Ask one question: *if I had no position right now, would I enter this trade at this price with this stop?* If no, you should not be in it. Your past cost basis is irrelevant to the future. And, mechanically, the stop is a resting order, not a decision you make later.

## Confirmation bias: seeing what you already believe

**Mechanism.** Once you have a view ("NOVA is going up"), your brain starts filtering. Bullish evidence looks important and obvious. Bearish evidence looks like noise, or you simply do not notice it. This is <Term id="confirmation-bias">confirmation bias</Term>, and it is why two traders can look at the same chart and see opposite things with complete confidence.

It gets worse once you are *in* the trade, because now your money and your ego are both on the bull side. Every green candle is "proof". Every red one is "a shakeout".

**Countermeasure.** Before entering, write down the *bear case* in one sentence: what would have to happen for this trade to be wrong? Then write the exact price where that is confirmed. That price is your stop. Also, in your weekly review, deliberately look for trades where you ignored a signal you would have acted on if you had been on the other side.

<Callout type="warning">

Confirmation bias is the reason "I'll just check one more indicator" is dangerous. You are not looking for information; you are looking for permission. If you already have a view, adding indicators will usually just confirm it. Decide the rules first, then look at the chart.

</Callout>

## Recency bias: the last thing that happened is the whole world

<Callout type="story" title="Sam and the three losses">

Sam's breakout strategy has a 45% win rate over 200 trades. Then it loses three in a row. Sam decides "breakouts don't work anymore" and stops taking them. The next four breakouts all win. Sam is not in any of them.

</Callout>

**Mechanism.** The brain weights recent events far more heavily than old ones. Three losses feel like a trend; 200 trades of data feel like history. This is why traders abandon good strategies during normal losing streaks and pile into bad ones after a lucky run. It also shows up on the chart: after a big up day, "up" feels like the default state of the world.

**Countermeasure.** Know your strategy's statistics *before* the streak. If you know that a 45% win rate produces a five-loss streak roughly once in every 40 trades (you saw this in [losing streaks](/learn/m07-risk-management/07-drawdown-and-risk-of-ruin)), three losses is not information. Have a written rule for when you *are* allowed to change a strategy, for example "after 50 trades with expectancy below zero", so the decision is never made in the middle of a streak.

## Anchoring: the first number sticks

**Mechanism.** The first number you see becomes the reference for everything after. If you saw NOVA at $150 last month, $120 "looks cheap", even though nothing about $150 matters to today's buyers. Your own entry price is the most powerful anchor of all: "it's below what I paid" feels meaningful. The market has no idea what you paid.

**Countermeasure.** Judge every level by *current* structure: recent swing highs and lows, current support and resistance, as you learned in [market structure](/learn/m03-market-structure/03-support-and-resistance). Your entry price does not appear on the market's chart, so it should not appear in your decisions.

## Putting it together

| Bias | What it makes you do | Rule that routes around it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Loss aversion | Feel every loss as a mistake | Think in R; review in batches of 20 |
| Disposition effect | Sell winners early, hold losers | Bracket orders; stops move only towards profit |
| Sunk cost | Hold past the stop to "not waste" the loss | "Would I enter here now?"; stop as a resting order |
| Confirmation bias | See only evidence that agrees | Write the bear case and its price before entry |
| Recency bias | Abandon good strategies after normal streaks | Know the stats; change strategy only by written rule |
| Anchoring | Judge price by old levels or your entry | Use only current structure |

Notice that every countermeasure is a *rule*, not an *effort*. That is the point. You do not beat a bias by trying harder. You beat it by making the decision somewhere the bias cannot reach.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Cognitive biases are built-in bugs; knowing about them does not switch them off, so you need rules, not willpower.
- Loss aversion makes losses feel about twice as heavy as gains, and drives the disposition effect: selling winners early and holding losers.
- Sunk cost keeps you in losing trades to avoid making the loss real; the only question that matters is whether you would enter now.
- Confirmation bias filters evidence to fit your view, so write the bear case and its price before you enter.
- Recency bias makes three losses feel like a broken strategy; know your statistics in advance and change strategies only by written rule.
</KeyTakeaways>
