Airline pilots have flown thousands of hours. They know how to start an engine better than they know how to make toast. And yet before every single flight, they pull out a laminated card and read the checklist aloud, item by item, with a second pilot confirming each one.

They do this not because they might forget. They do it because they *know* they might forget, especially when tired, rushed, or distracted, and they have decided that a boring card is cheaper than a crash. The checklist is not an insult to their skill. It is the reason the skill gets to be used.

Trading is the same. You have learned a lot in eight modules. Under pressure, with money on the line and a candle printing, you will not reliably remember any of it. A routine remembers for you.

## Discipline is a system, not a personality

People talk about discipline as if some traders are born with it. That is not what the research on habits shows. The people who look disciplined from the outside are usually people who have removed the need for discipline: they have made the right action the easy, default, automatic one.

Think about it this way. If your stop is placed as a resting order at entry, you do not need discipline to take the loss. If your daily trade cap is three and you log each one, you do not need discipline to stop at three. If your pre-trade checklist has seven items and you refuse to click until they are all ticked, you do not need discipline to avoid chasing.

<Callout type="eli5">

If you want to eat less chocolate, the hard way is to keep chocolate in the house and resist it all day. The easy way is to not buy it. Trading routines are the "don't buy it" approach. You set things up so the bad choice is not even on the table.

</Callout>

## The three routines

A complete trading routine has three parts: before the session, before each trade, and after the session. Each one is short. The point is that it happens *every time*.

### Before the session (10 minutes)

1. **Check yourself.** Slept badly? Angry about something? Distracted? Rate it 1 to 5. If you score 2 or lower, you trade at half size or not at all. Your state is a risk input, same as volatility.
2. **Check the calendar.** Any scheduled news that could hit your symbols? Mark the times; no new entries in the ten minutes around them.
3. **Mark the levels.** On each symbol in your plan, draw the key [support and resistance](/learn/m03-market-structure/03-support-and-resistance), the prior day's high and low, and whatever your strategy needs. Do this *before* the market opens, while nothing is moving and nothing is tempting.
4. **Write the plan for the day.** One or two sentences per symbol: "NOVA: uptrend on 1h, looking for pullback longs to the 20 EMA above $118. No trade if it opens below $115."
5. **Confirm the limits.** Say your daily loss limit and trade cap out loud. Today it is $X and N trades.

### Before each trade: the pre-trade checklist

This is the laminated card. Print it, or keep it in a note next to your platform. Every item must be a yes before you click.

- [ ] This setup is written in my plan, and I can name it.
- [ ] The higher timeframe agrees with the direction (or my plan says it does not matter).
- [ ] The entry trigger has actually fired. I am not anticipating it.
- [ ] My stop is at a structural level, and I know the exact price.
- [ ] My target is at least the minimum reward-to-risk my plan requires.
- [ ] Position size is calculated from the stop distance and my risk rule, not from a feeling.
- [ ] I have checked for news in the next 15 minutes.
- [ ] I am within my daily trade cap and above my daily loss limit.
- [ ] I am not on tilt: no losses in the last 20 minutes that I am trying to recover.
- [ ] The order is a bracket: entry, stop and target go in together.

<Callout type="tip">

Ten items feels like a lot until you time it. A trader who knows the list takes about 30 seconds to run it. If a setup cannot survive 30 seconds of checking, it was never a setup. If you find yourself skipping the list "because this one is obvious", that is the trade most likely to be a mistake.

</Callout>

### After the session (10 minutes)

1. **Log every trade** in the [journal](/journal) with the setup name, the grade from the [process lesson](/learn/m08-trading-psychology/06-process-over-outcome), the R result, and one line on how you felt at entry and exit.
2. **Log the trades you did not take** if they were valid setups. Skipped setups are data too.
3. **Note one thing** you did well and one thing to fix. Just one each. More than that and none of it sticks.
4. **Close the platform.** Do not "just watch" after your session. Watching leads to trading.

## Why routines beat willpower

<Callout type="story" title="Priya's two months">

For her first month in the simulator, Priya trades on instinct and knowledge. She knows the rules and mostly follows them, but her journal shows 40% of her trades were C grade: chased entries, moved stops, skipped setups. Her expectancy is slightly negative.

In month two she adds the three routines. Nothing about her knowledge changes. Her C-grade rate drops to 10%. Her expectancy turns positive. The difference is not that she got smarter. It is that the checklist caught the trades her instincts would have taken.

</Callout>

Willpower is a resource that runs out. Every decision you make during the day uses some of it, and by the afternoon, or after a loss, there is less left. A routine does not use willpower. It uses habit, which is free.

Routines also give you something to grade. "Did I follow the routine?" is a yes or no question. "Was I disciplined today?" is a feeling. You can improve at the first one.

<Callout type="warning">

The most common way routines fail is not that people abandon them. It is that they *shorten* them. First the after-session log becomes "I'll do it tomorrow". Then the pre-session levels become "I know where they are". Then the checklist becomes a glance. Within a month the routine is gone and the C trades are back. Do the whole routine every time, even on days when you take no trades, because that is how habits survive.

</Callout>

## Making it stick

Start with the pre-trade checklist alone. It is the one with the fastest payoff. Use it for two weeks in the simulator. Then add the after-session log. Then the pre-session routine. Building all three at once usually means building none of them.

Keep the checklist visible. Physically on the desk, or in a note that sits on top of the platform window. Out of sight is out of routine.

And track it. Add a tag in the journal for "checklist-skipped". If that tag ever appears on a losing trade, you have learned everything you need to know about why routines matter.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Discipline is a system that makes the right action the default, not a personality trait some traders are born with.
- A complete routine has three parts: before the session, before each trade, and after the session, each taking about ten minutes or less.
- The pre-trade checklist is ten yes-or-no questions; if a setup cannot survive 30 seconds of checking, it was never a setup.
- Willpower runs out during the day and after losses, while habits are free.
- Routines fail by being shortened, not abandoned, so do the whole thing every time, even on days with no trades.
</KeyTakeaways>
