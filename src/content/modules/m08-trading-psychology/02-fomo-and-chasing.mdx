Picture a bus pulling away from the stop. You are twenty metres behind it, running. You could wait ten minutes for the next one, but *this* bus is right there, and everyone on it is looking out the window at you. So you sprint, trip on the kerb, and the bus leaves anyway.

That is chasing price. The move has already started, everyone seems to be on board, and the thought of being left behind is unbearable. So you buy at the worst possible moment: after the easy part of the move has happened and right before the people who bought early start selling to you.

The feeling has a name: **FOMO**, the fear of missing out. It is the most expensive emotion in trading because it makes you buy high and, when it turns against you, sell low.

## What chasing actually looks like

<Callout type="story" title="Leo and the green candle">

Leo is watching NOVA drift sideways for an hour. Nothing happens, so he opens another tab. When he looks back, NOVA has ripped from $120 to $131 in eight minutes. His chat group is full of rocket emojis. He does not have a setup, a stop, or a target. He just knows that if it goes to $140 without him he will feel sick. He buys at $131.50. Price tops at $132 two minutes later and slides back to $124 over the next hour. Leo sells at $125, down $6.50 a share, on a trade he never planned.

</Callout>

Leo's trade had no edge. He did not buy because a rule fired; he bought because a feeling fired. And the feeling was strongest at exactly the point where the trade was worst: after a big vertical move, with no nearby support, when the early buyers were looking to sell.

## Why the best-looking entry is usually the worst one

A chart looks most exciting when a move is nearly finished. This is not bad luck; it is how moves work.

A move starts quietly, when few people notice. It accelerates as more people pile in. It looks *spectacular* right at the end, because that is when the most people have joined and the candles are biggest. By the time a move is obvious enough to trigger FOMO in you, it has triggered FOMO in thousands of others, and they all bought just before you.

Who is selling to all those buyers? The people who bought early. They are the ones who saw the setup at $120. Chasing means you are systematically taking the other side of the smartest money in the move.

<Callout type="eli5">

Imagine a playground game where the first kids to spot the ice cream van get the shortest queue. By the time the whole school is running towards it, the queue is huge and the van is nearly sold out. Running with the crowd feels safe, but it means you arrive last.

</Callout>

## The mechanism: dopamine and social proof

Two things happen in your brain when you see a vertical candle.

First, **dopamine**. As we saw in [fear and greed](/learn/m08-trading-psychology/01-fear-and-greed), dopamine is the wanting chemical. A sharp move is a huge, sudden reward signal. Your brain does not care that the reward already went to someone else; it just wants.

Second, **social proof**. Humans copy the group. It kept us alive for a very long time: if everyone is running, you run first and ask why later. In markets, "everyone is running" shows up as big green candles, chat rooms, and headlines. The instinct to join is deep and fast.

Add <Term id="loss-aversion">loss aversion</Term> and it gets worse. Missing a move does not register as "nothing happened". It registers as a *loss*, and losses hurt double. So your brain treats staying out as painful, and jumping in as the cure.

## The countermeasure: the missed trade is not a loss

The first fix is a belief, and you need to actually believe it: **a missed trade costs nothing.** Your account balance is identical whether NOVA went to $140 or $100 without you. There is no line in your [journal](/journal) for "money I didn't make". Only chasing shows up there, as a real loss.

The second fix is structural. Here are rules that work:

1. **No setup, no trade.** If you cannot say which rule in your plan fired, you are not allowed to enter. Full stop.
2. **The "already moved" filter.** If price is more than a fixed distance above your moving average (say 2 ATRs, using the [ATR](/learn/m05-indicators/08-atr) you learned), the entry is disallowed. You wait for a [pullback](/learn/m09-strategies/04-pullback-trading) or you let it go.
3. **The 60-second rule.** When you feel the urge to chase, start a timer. You may only enter after it ends, and only if a rule fires. Most urges do not survive a minute.
4. **Pre-plan your entries.** Set alerts at levels *before* the market gets there. If you already have an order at $121 on the retest, a spike to $131 is not a missed opportunity; it is just noise.
5. **Close the chat.** Social proof only works if you can see the crowd. During a trading session, the crowd's opinion has no place on your screen.

<Callout type="warning">

Chasing feels like being decisive. It is the opposite. A decisive trader decided the entry an hour ago and is now simply waiting. If you are making the decision *while* the candle is printing, you are being pushed, not deciding.

</Callout>

## There will always be another bus

The market produces setups every single day, on every timeframe, on every symbol. In the simulator alone you have eight symbols and six timeframes. Missing one move out of hundreds is not a tragedy. It is Tuesday.

The trader who lets moves go without them is not weaker than the one who chases. They are the one who is still trading in a year.

<KeyTakeaways>
- FOMO is the fear of missing out, and it makes you buy after the easy part of a move is over.
- A move looks most exciting at the end, when the most people have joined and early buyers are selling to them.
- Dopamine, social proof and loss aversion combine to make staying out feel like losing.
- A missed trade costs exactly nothing; only chasing shows up in your account as a real loss.
- Fix it structurally: no setup means no trade, a distance-from-average filter, a 60-second timer, and pre-planned entries.
</KeyTakeaways>
