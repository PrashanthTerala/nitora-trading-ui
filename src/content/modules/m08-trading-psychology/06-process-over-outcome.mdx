A child crosses a busy road without looking and gets to the other side fine. Was that a good decision? Of course not. It was a terrible decision with a lucky outcome. Another child looks both ways, waits for the green light, crosses carefully, and gets splashed by a passing car. Bad outcome, good decision.

Everyone understands this with roads. Almost nobody understands it with trading. When a trade wins, we feel we did something right. When it loses, we feel we did something wrong. But in a game with uncertainty, the result of a single trade tells you very little about the quality of the decision.

This lesson is about learning to grade your decisions separately from your results. It is the single most important mental shift in this module, because it is the only way to improve at something where good choices lose regularly.

## The 2x2 that changes everything

Every trade you take lands in one of four boxes.

| | **Good outcome (win)** | **Bad outcome (loss)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Good decision (followed the plan)** | Earned it. This is what the edge looks like when it pays. Log it and move on. | The cost of doing business. A 1R loss on a valid setup is *not a mistake*. Log it and move on. |
| **Bad decision (broke the plan)** | The most dangerous box. You were rewarded for breaking a rule, and your brain just learned the wrong lesson. | Deserved it. Painful, but at least the lesson is clear. |

Most traders live in the top-left and bottom-right and think that is the whole grid. The other two boxes are where accounts are made and lost.

The **bottom-left** is where the damage compounds. You chased a move and it worked. You held past your stop and it came back. You doubled size on a hunch and it paid. Each time, the win feels like proof that the rule was wrong and you were right. Ten of these and you no longer have a plan; you have a set of habits that worked once.

The **top-right** is where good traders quit. Five valid setups in a row lose. Nothing was wrong with any of them. But five losses *feel* like five mistakes, and the trader starts changing rules that were never broken.

<Callout type="eli5">

Imagine a dice game where you win if you roll anything except a one. Rolling a one is not a mistake. You made the right bet and got unlucky. Now imagine someone bets on the one and it comes up. They won, but it was a silly bet, and if they keep making it they will lose nearly every time. Grading the *bet* rather than the *roll* is the whole skill.

</Callout>

## Why the brain gets this wrong

**Outcome bias.** We judge decisions by their results because results are visible and decisions are not. A loss is a number on the screen. The quality of the reasoning behind it is invisible, so the brain uses the number as a proxy.

**Hindsight.** After the fact, whatever happened looks like it was always going to happen. The loser "was obviously going to fail, look at that resistance". The winner "was obviously a good entry". But at the moment of decision you did not know the outcome, and pretending you did makes it impossible to learn.

**Small samples.** As you saw in [expectancy](/learn/m07-risk-management/05-expectancy), a 50% win rate strategy can lose five in a row roughly every 30 trades. The brain cannot feel a sample size of 30. It feels the last five.

<Callout type="story" title="Sam and Leo take the same trade">

Sam and Leo both see a pullback on IDX to the 20 EMA on the 1h chart. Sam checks his plan: uptrend confirmed, pullback to the average, bullish close, stop below the swing low, 1:2 target. He enters. It stops out. Leo skips it because "it doesn't feel right", then buys the breakout an hour later with no stop "because it's obviously going". It runs and he makes 3R.

Leo made more money. Sam made the better decision. Over the next hundred trades, Sam's approach will make money and Leo's will not, because Sam's approach can be repeated and Leo's cannot.

</Callout>

## How to grade a decision

You need a scoring system that ignores the result completely. Here is a simple one. After every trade, answer these with a yes or no.

1. Was the setup one that is written in my plan?
2. Did I enter at the trigger, not before and not after?
3. Was the stop placed where the plan said, before I entered?
4. Was the size calculated from the stop using my risk rule?
5. Did I manage the trade by the rules (no moving stops away, no early exits)?
6. Did I exit at the stop, the target, or a written management rule?

Six yeses is an **A trade**. Five is a **B**. Four or fewer is a **C**, and you write down which rule broke and why. The dollar result is recorded separately and does not affect the grade.

<Callout type="tip">

In the [journal](/journal), use tags for the grade: grade-a, grade-b, grade-c. After 50 trades, filter by grade and look at the expectancy of each group. Nearly every trader finds their A trades are positive and their C trades are negative. That is your edge, measured, and it tells you exactly what the plan is worth.

</Callout>

## What to do with each box

**Good decision, good outcome:** nothing. Do not celebrate too hard; celebration is where the next bad decision comes from.

**Good decision, bad outcome:** nothing. Genuinely nothing. Do not change the plan, do not reduce size, do not skip the next setup. This is the box that requires the most discipline, because doing nothing after a loss feels wrong.

**Bad decision, good outcome:** treat it as a loss. Some traders literally donate the profit from rule-breaking trades to charity so the brain does not get the reward. At minimum, write it up as a mistake with the same seriousness as a losing one.

**Bad decision, bad outcome:** write down the rule that broke, the feeling that broke it, and the countermeasure. Then apply the countermeasure. This is the most useful box for learning, as long as you learn from the *decision*, not the pain.

<Callout type="warning">

Never change a plan based on a single trade or a single week. Change it only in a scheduled review, based on at least 30 to 50 trades, when you are not in a position and not in a streak. Rule changes made under emotion are almost always outcome bias in disguise.

</Callout>

## The long game

Trading is one of the few pursuits where you can do everything right and lose for a month. If you judge yourself by outcomes, you will change your process during every normal losing streak and never give any approach long enough to show its edge.

If you judge yourself by process, losing streaks become boring instead of frightening. You check the grade, see an A, and move on. That boredom is what consistency feels like from the inside.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Every trade lands in one of four boxes: good or bad decision, crossed with good or bad outcome, and the result alone tells you almost nothing about the decision.
- The bad-decision-good-outcome box is the most dangerous because it rewards rule-breaking and trains bad habits.
- The good-decision-bad-outcome box is where good traders quit, mistaking normal losses for mistakes.
- Grade every trade on six process questions, record the dollar result separately, and compare expectancy by grade after 50 trades.
- Change the plan only in scheduled reviews over large samples, never after a single trade or streak.
</KeyTakeaways>
