You have spent seven modules learning to read charts, place orders and size positions. Here is the uncomfortable truth: none of that is the hard part. The hard part is that the person clicking the mouse is a human being, and human beings were built for a world of tigers and berries, not candles and stop losses.

Think about a child at a fairground. Two things make the child do silly things: being scared (running from the haunted house straight into a puddle) and wanting something badly (spending all the pocket money on the first stall). Trading mistakes are the same. Every single one is either **fear** or **greed** wearing a costume.

This module is about spotting the costume. Once you can name what your brain is doing, you can build a rule that does the thinking for you. That is the entire point of trading psychology: not becoming a robot, but building rules so you don't have to be one.

## Why your brain is the wrong tool for this job

Your brain is a survival machine. For most of human history, the worst thing that could happen was losing something you already had: your food, your shelter, your place in the group. So the brain learned to treat losses as emergencies.

Researchers have measured this. Losing $100 feels roughly **twice** as bad as winning $100 feels good. This is called <Term id="loss-aversion">loss aversion</Term>, and it is not a character flaw. It is factory settings. We will look at it properly in [the biases lesson](/learn/m08-trading-psychology/04-cognitive-biases), but you need to know it now because it explains almost everything that follows.

Greed has a mechanism too. When you see a chart rocketing upward, or when a trade goes into profit, your brain releases **dopamine**. Dopamine is not the "pleasure" chemical people think it is. It is the *wanting* chemical. It makes you want more, right now, and it makes waiting feel physically uncomfortable.

<Callout type="eli5">

Imagine your brain has two puppies inside it. One puppy is scared of everything and wants to hide. The other puppy wants every treat it sees and will jump on the table to get it. Neither puppy can read a chart. Trading well means training both puppies to sit and wait while *you* follow the plan.

</Callout>

## Fear in its costumes

Fear rarely announces itself. It shows up as sensible-sounding thoughts. Here is what it looks like in practice.

<Callout type="story" title="Sam and the early exit">

Sam buys 100 shares of BLUE at $50 with a stop at $48 and a target at $56. That's a plan with a 1:3 reward-to-risk. Price rises to $52. Sam feels a warm glow, then a flicker of worry: *what if it comes back down?* He sells at $52 for a $200 gain. Price then runs to $57. His plan would have made $600. Sam's fear of giving back $200 cost him $400.

</Callout>

Sam's mistake is the most common one in trading. It has a name, the **disposition effect**: people sell winners too early and hold losers too long. Fear of losing the paper profit beats the plan.

Fear's other costumes include:

- **Not entering** a valid setup because the last one lost. The chart is the same; only your feelings changed.
- **Moving a stop wider** as price approaches it, because taking the loss feels unbearable. Now a 1R loss becomes a 2R loss.
- **Sizing too small** on the best setups, so your winners never pay for your losers.
- **Freezing** during a fast move and doing nothing while your stop is hit and then some.

Notice that fear can make you act *and* make you fail to act. The costume changes; the engine is the same.

## Greed in its costumes

Greed is louder but sneakier. It tends to arrive after something has gone well, when your guard is down.

<Callout type="story" title="Priya and the extra size">

Priya has three winning trades in a row on NOVA. She feels sharp. On the fourth trade she thinks, *I'm clearly reading this well today,* and doubles her usual position size. The trade is a normal, boring loser. But at double size it wipes out two of the three wins. Priya's edge did not change. Her size did, because dopamine told her she was on a roll.

</Callout>

Greed's costumes:

- **Chasing** a move that has already happened, because watching it without you feels unbearable. This is <Term id="fomo">FOMO</Term>, and it gets [its own lesson](/learn/m08-trading-psychology/02-fomo-and-chasing).
- **Moving a target further away** as price approaches it, so a good win turns into a round trip.
- **Adding to a winner** with no rule for doing so, so the last add is at the worst price.
- **Trading more** after wins, because winning feels good and you want more of the feeling.

<Callout type="warning">

Greed is most dangerous immediately after a win, and fear is most dangerous immediately after a loss. Both are strongest exactly when you feel most certain. If you notice you feel *very* sure, that is a signal to slow down, not speed up.

</Callout>

## The mechanism: emotion arrives before thought

Here is the part that matters. When you see price move sharply against you, your body reacts in about a fifth of a second: heart rate up, palms damp, attention narrowed. Your thinking brain gets the information *afterwards*, and by then it is already looking for reasons to do what the body wants.

This is why "just be disciplined" does not work. Discipline is a thinking-brain resource, and the thinking brain is late to the party. The traders who stay consistent are not calmer people. They have simply moved the decisions to a time when they *were* calm.

## The countermeasure: decide when you are calm

Every effective fix in this module follows one pattern: make the decision *before* the emotion arrives, and make it hard to change *during*.

1. **Write the exit before the entry.** Stop and target go in as a bracket order, the way you learned in [bracket orders](/learn/m06-orders-and-execution/03-bracket-and-oco). Sam's problem disappears if the target order is already sitting there.
2. **Fix your position size by formula.** Priya's problem disappears if size comes from the [1% rule](/learn/m07-risk-management/02-the-one-percent-rule), never from a feeling.
3. **Give yourself a trade limit.** Three trades a day, say. A limit removes the option to "just take one more".
4. **Name the feeling out loud.** It sounds silly, but saying "that is fear" or "that is greed" before acting engages the thinking brain and buys you a few seconds.
5. **Journal the emotion, not just the price.** In the [journal](/journal), tag each trade with how you felt at entry and exit. After 30 trades you will see which puppy is costing you money.

<Callout type="tip">

A simple test before any unplanned action: *would I do this if the position were half the size?* If the answer is "no, I'd just follow the plan", the size is talking, not the chart.

</Callout>

## What this module will cover

Each remaining lesson takes one costume and pulls it off. FOMO and chasing. Revenge trading and tilt. The four big cognitive biases. Overtrading. Then the constructive half: judging process over outcome, building routines and checklists, and surviving the emotional extremes of losing streaks and big wins.

None of this will make you feel nothing. The goal is smaller and more useful: to feel whatever you feel, and follow the plan anyway.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Every trading mistake is fear or greed in a costume; learning to name the costume is the first skill.
- Losses feel about twice as painful as equal gains feel good, which is why traders cut winners short and let losers run.
- Dopamine is the wanting chemical, and it is strongest right after wins, which is when greed does the most damage.
- Emotion arrives before thought, so willpower in the moment is unreliable.
- The fix is to make decisions while calm and make them hard to change: bracket orders, formula sizing, trade limits and journaling.
</KeyTakeaways>
